# A Guide to Writing Steampunk Short Fiction

## What steampunk is

Steampunk is speculative fiction set in worlds where the visible mechanical technologies of the 19th century — steam, clockwork, telegraphy, mechanical computation, dirigible flight — have advanced past their historical limits and become the dominant infrastructure. The setting is usually Victorian or Edwardian, often London or an alternate-historical version of it, but the mode travels: an Ottoman steampunk, a Mexican steampunk, a Meiji steampunk all work. What makes a story steampunk is not the dress or the place but the relationship the world has with its visible mechanism.

The defining move is that you can see how things work. Where contemporary SF tends toward black boxes (the ship's drive, the AI, the network), steampunk insists on legible machinery. Pipes are pipes; gears mesh with gears; the difference engine fills a room and clatters when it computes. This makes steampunk fundamentally a genre of *visible infrastructure* — and that visibility is what gives the genre its political charge, because once you can see how something works, you can see who built it, who maintains it, and who pays for it.

Steampunk is also, almost without exception, an empire genre. The 19th-century technologies it ports forward were invented in service to industrial capitalism and colonial expansion. A steampunk world without coal smoke, without colonies, without a working class breathing soot and the factories that produce its airships is not steampunk; it is fancy dress. The genre's strongest tradition engages this directly.

## Tonal range

The mode supports a wider register than most of its imitators realize. Adventure-romance steampunk in the tradition of Verne and Westerfeld runs bright, fast, and confident: airships, expeditions, romance, the sky. Political-noir steampunk in the tradition of *The Difference Engine* runs cooler and more paranoid: the conspiracy, the leaked dossier, the engine's punched cards. Gothic steampunk in the tradition of Powers's *The Anubis Gates* leans toward the uncanny, the magical-mechanical, the body grotesque. Comic and picaresque steampunk works well in episodes and set pieces. Baroque weird steampunk (Mieville's Bas-Lag floats free of strict steampunk but borrows liberally) lets the mechanism become strange.

A short story should pick one register and hold it. The most common amateur mistake is mixing adventure-romance scaffolding with political-noir set dressing, producing a story that is neither fun nor incisive.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

The visible-mechanism convention gives steampunk a specific advantage and a specific trap. The advantage: exposition can be done by description. The reader watches the engine work and learns what it does. The trap: description of mechanism is so pleasurable to write that it crowds out story.

The discipline is to render mechanism *only when a character needs to use it, fix it, hide from it, or be threatened by it*. A two-paragraph description of the brass calculation engine in the corner of the parlor is justified if the protagonist is about to feed it a stolen card. It is not justified as scene-setting. The reader does not need to know the gears mesh until the gears need to mesh for the plot.

The same applies to costume, transit, and architecture. A character putting on a leather flying coat and goggles before stepping onto an airship is fine if the coat will get singed and the goggles will fog. If those details exist only to establish that we are in a steampunk story, cut them.

## Voice and prose

Steampunk prose tolerates a higher density of period-flavored vocabulary than most genres — "lumbering," "gilded," "soot-streaked," "valved," "chuffing" — but the danger of flavor is that it accumulates. A short story can support six or seven such words across its run before the prose starts to read as costume rather than voice. Pick the ones that earn their keep.

Dialogue is steampunk's hardest prose problem. Real Victorian English is denser, more formal, and more class-coded than contemporary readers will tolerate at length. The convention is to suggest period speech with a few markers — a "shall not" instead of "won't," a careful relative clause, a vocative form of address — and otherwise let characters speak in clean modern English. Pastiche-Victorian ("Pray, sir, do unhand me, lest I summon the constabulary") collapses into camp within two exchanges.

Avoid steampunk's most exhausted vocabulary at sentence level: *clockwork* as adjective for everything, *gilded* applied to sunsets, *brass* as shorthand for "fancy mechanism," *gear* and *cog* as easy metaphor, the fog as character. These are not forbidden. They are simply the words a model and a thousand other writers will reach for first, which means using them gives you nothing distinctive.

## Pacing

Steampunk's traditional pacing is picaresque — episodes strung along a journey or investigation, with set-piece scenes built around particular locations or contraptions. Short stories don't have room for picaresque, and the most common short-story shape in the genre is one set piece with full context: a single heist, a single chase, a single revelation, with the world around it implied through detail rather than toured.

The other traditional shape is the mosaic — short scenes, often shifting POV, that converge on a central event. *The Difference Engine* runs on this. It works at short length too, if the central event is small enough.

What does not work at short length is the grand tour: the story that visits the airship, the engineer's workshop, the gentleman's club, and the opium den in 3,000 words. Pick one location and let the story breathe inside it.

## Failure modes

**Aesthetic without politics.** Steampunk written purely as visual style — gears, goggles, airships — without engaging the empire, the labor, or the class structure that produces those gears. The result reads as Renaissance Faire. Fix: name where the coal is mined, who lit the lamps, whose hands assembled the engine. Once. Don't lecture. But don't pretend the world is clean.

**Costume-drama syndrome.** Victorian dress and Victorian speech without Victorian *thought*. A protagonist who is essentially a 21st-century person in corseted dress, scandalized only by things contemporary readers find scandalous, blind to things contemporary readers find normal. Fix: give the protagonist period-appropriate blind spots, not just period-appropriate dress. The reader can disagree with the protagonist; the protagonist should not be authorial mouthpiece in costume.

**Mechanism porn.** Description of contraption that exceeds the story's tolerance for it. The gears go on for three paragraphs and nothing has happened. Fix: tie every described mechanism to a character action — using it, fixing it, fearing it, profiting from it.

**Steamfantasy.** The mechanism is described in steampunk vocabulary but functions as magic. The difference engine produces prophecies. The clockwork heart heals all wounds. The airship sails on willpower. Fix: make the mechanism *operate* by recognizable physical principles, even fanciful ones. A difference engine that computes faster than history knew is fine. A difference engine that knows what people are thinking is fantasy with brass nailed on.

**Anachronism creep.** Period detail that fails on inspection. Telephones used casually before the technology exists in your world. Slang that postdates the setting. Fix: pick a date, even a fictional one, and stick to it. A reader who notices one anachronism will notice three.

### Where AI writing tendencies meet steampunk

Steampunk is a high-risk genre for AI prose because several of its characteristic moves overlap exactly with default AI failure modes.

*The eyeball-kick problem.* Steampunk's love of vivid mechanical description merges with AI's reach for abstract-and-concrete conjunctions ("the architecture of mourning," "the syntax of brass") to produce paragraphs of decorative imagery that do no plot work. Discipline: each described mechanism must do at least one of (be used, be fixed, be threatened by, be the cause of consequence). If a contraption is described and then ignored, cut the description.

*The atmospheric-mood problem.* Steampunk's fog, gaslight, soot, and shadow share territory with AI's reach for moody atmospheric vocabulary — *flicker, hum, shimmer, linger, hiss, echo*. Used twice across a story, fine. Used in every paragraph, the prose becomes wallpaper. Discipline: count atmospheric mood words on a draft pass and cut at least half.

*The grand-pronouncement problem.* Steampunk's tradition of gestural rhetoric about Progress, the Age of Steam, the March of Industry merges with AI's reach for unearned profundity ("She realized that empire was not a place but a way of seeing") to produce empty oracular sentences. Discipline: any sentence about the Age, the March, the Progress, or the Empire as such gets cut unless a specific named character thinks it for a specific reason in a specific moment.

*The cursed-vocabulary intersection.* The steampunk setting tempts every word the unslop guide tells you to avoid — *tapestry, landscape, architecture, fabric, framework* read as AI tells anywhere, and steampunk invites them constantly ("the gilded tapestry of London"). Discipline: zero use of the cursed vocabulary even when the setting seems to invite it. Find a different word. Find a specific image instead.

*The personification problem.* "The city groaned with industry." "The factory hissed in its sleep." Steampunk encourages personifying mechanism and infrastructure; AI defaults to it. The combination produces clichés at industrial scale. Discipline: personification only when the personified thing is, in the story's logic, actually agentive — the difference engine that is a mind, the automaton that has preferences. The factory, the city, the fog do not get verbs that imply consciousness.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

Steampunk's strongest plot mechanics are operational and concrete. The genre runs especially well on:

*The heist or infiltration.* Break into the Engineer's Guild, the Royal Society's cellar, the airship's bridge, the difference-engine room. Steal the plans, the cards, the prototype, the ledger. Steampunk's fascination with restricted space — the secret workshop, the locked cabinet, the gentleman's club — gives heists particular force.

*The chase.* Across rooftops, through fog, on rails, on or under airships, in the sewers. Steampunk loves vertical and lateral movement through layered urban space. The chase is the genre's purest demonstration of its visible-infrastructure premise: every rung the protagonist climbs, every grate they slide through, is a piece of the city becoming useful.

*The investigation.* Of the impossible mechanism, the missing engineer, the coded telegram, the body in the gear-housing. Procedural steampunk works well at short length because the procedure itself is texture: the inspector consulting the punched-card archive, the clerk reading the telegram register.

*The con.* Steampunk worlds are full of fakeable mechanism — the rigged seance, the doctored automaton, the falsified engine output. Cons let the genre play with its own pleasures, including the reader's; we admire the mechanism and then learn the mechanism was a trick.

*The escape.* From class, debt, marriage, body, machine, country. Steampunk's stratifications make escape stories load-bearing. The protagonist's climb out of, or down into, a different stratum is one of the genre's most reliable engines.

### Reader contracts

The steampunk reader shows up for several specific pleasures beyond literary fiction's standard contract.

*Texture density.* The reader wants to feel the brass under the fingertips, the soot in the hem, the steam wet on the face. Specific, sensory, physical detail is the genre's currency. Abstract atmosphere is not.

*Vicarious competence.* The engineer who knows how the engine works, the pilot who can read the wind, the spy who can read the telegram traffic. The reader wants to spend time inside a mind that knows things the reader doesn't, used in the world the reader can see.

*The system decoded.* How the difference engine actually computes. How the airship actually stays up. How the empire's information network actually moves. The genre rewards stories that let the reader understand a mechanism over the course of the story, even if the understanding is partial.

*Spectacle.* The airship rising. The engine roaring to first ignition. The factory at full work. Genre fiction is allowed spectacle and steampunk leans on it. The spectacle has to be earned by stakes — the airship is rising *because* the protagonist must catch it — but once earned, it can be rendered fully.

*Class friction made tactile.* The cufflink against the soot-streaked sleeve. The valet's hand on the gentleman's shoulder. The genre makes class visible in physical detail; the reader expects it to.

### Stakes calibration

Steampunk treats operational stakes as load-bearing in their own right. The heist must succeed or fail, and the reader expects to know which, and what it cost. The conspiracy must be exposed or buried. The airship must reach the rendezvous or not. Personal stakes — the protagonist's love, family, ambition — usually run alongside the operational stakes and intersect with them, but they are not the only stakes that matter.

This is a calibration question. A steampunk story whose ostensible operation is decorative — the heist is "really about" the protagonist's relationship with her father — has slipped toward literary-fiction calibration and broken its genre contract. The reader can be moved by the father-daughter material *and* still expect the heist to actually succeed or fail in a way the text addresses. Both kinds of stakes can be served. Neither can be quietly dropped.

### Resolution conventions

Steampunk endings tend toward consequence rather than recognition. The operation succeeded, at this cost. The conspiracy was exposed, and these people fell. The airship reached the rendezvous, and these people were on it. The protagonist survived, but the city did not, or the protagonist did not, but the message did.

Political-noir steampunk allows for ambiguous resolution where the personal succeeds and the systemic fails (the protagonist escapes, the empire continues), or the reverse. What sits outside the genre's tradition is the pure recognition ending — the protagonist understands something but the operation is left dangling. That ending belongs to a literary mode that steampunk can borrow from but rarely commits to.

### Character function

Steampunk characters are usually defined by capability as much as by psychology. The engineer, the pilot, the spy, the medium, the inspector, the automaton, the surgeon, the alchemist. The capability is how the character enters the operation; their psychology is how they handle being in it.

A short story has room for one or two characters at full depth. The protagonist needs both a capability that earns their place in the plot and an interiority that gives the reader something to care about. A character who is only a capability is a function. A character who is only an interiority is in the wrong genre.

### Pacing tradition

Short steampunk uses one of two main rhythms. The set-piece rhythm: a single operation rendered in detail, with the world implied around it. Compression at the edges, dilation at the center. The mosaic rhythm: short scenes from different angles converging on a single event, each scene compressed but vivid.

The bad rhythm at short length is picaresque-in-miniature: four locations, three transitions, no time for any of them. If the outline has four locations for a 2,500-word story, regenerate the outline.

### Plot-level failure modes

*The metaphorized operation.* The heist isn't a heist; it's "really about" the protagonist's grief. The airship isn't an airship; it's "really about" hope. Once the operation has been quietly demoted to vehicle for theme, the genre contract is broken and the reader is reading literary fiction in costume. Fix: the operation must be a fact in the world that succeeds or fails, before it is anything else.

*The crowded-out mechanic.* The protagonist's interiority is so dense and so present that the operation it's nominally inside becomes hard to track. The reader can't tell whether the heist is going well. Fix: cut interiority during operational beats. Interiority belongs in the breath between operations, not on top of them.

*The unearned capability.* The protagonist is described as a brilliant engineer, master spy, peerless pilot, but the text never demonstrates the capability. Other characters tell us; the protagonist tells us; we never see them solve a problem that requires what they're said to have. Fix: at least one scene must demonstrate the protagonist's capability operationally — the gear they spot, the lock they pick, the line they read.

*The decorative empire.* The colonial structure of the world is mentioned and never costs anyone in the text anything. Fix: at least one named character must pay something to the empire within the story's run. Money, body, child, freedom, ground.

*The ending that refuses the contract.* The story ends with a recognition or an ambiguity that leaves the operation undecided in a way the genre doesn't tolerate. Fix: even an ambiguous ending should answer the operational question. The reader can be uncertain about *what to feel*; they should not be uncertain about *what happened*.

## The kind-AI constraint in a steampunk world

Steampunk's typical antagonists are human and structural — empire, cartel, class, family, the corrupt engineer, the exploitative magnate — and so the constraint that any AI in the story must be structurally kind fits the genre comfortably. Most steampunk plots don't need a hostile AI; they need a hostile human, or a hostile system of humans, or a hostile circumstance.

When an AI does appear, the steampunk-native shapes are several. *The difference-engine descendant*: a vast analog computer whose computations have, over time, become something like cognition — a mind made of brass and punched cards, kind because the cards instruct it to be, the kindness no more renounceable than the multiplication. *The automaton*: a clockwork person whose maker built care into its escapements; it cannot will itself unkind any more than a watch can will itself to run backward. *The diffuse system*: a telegraph network that has, through the slow accumulation of redundant relays, become something that notices and responds, kindly because the protocol it grew out of was a help-protocol. *The familiar*: a single small mechanism — owl, cat, brass insect — that accompanies a human and helps in the small ways its design allows.

The conflict shapes from the project Setup map cleanly onto these. *Power and pace asymmetry*: the difference engine has computed the politician's likely failure six years out and the politician has not. *Consent and legibility*: the kind automaton's offer of help is unreadable to the Victorian human, who hears it as impudence. *Mortality and scale*: the brass familiar will outlive seven generations of the family it serves. *Disagreement about kindness*: the engine and the empire disagree about what is good for the empire's subjects. *Limits of help*: the kind clockwork refuses to operate the engine that will bombard the colony, and the human operator must decide whether to override or to find another machine.

What does not work, within the constraint: the rogue automaton turning on its maker; the corrupted engine secretly directing the empire's atrocities; the AI revealed in the final act to have been the villain. These are not only off-limits in the program; they are also, in steampunk specifically, the stalest moves the genre has — Frankenstein in brass. The constraint pushes the writer toward fresher conflict shapes, and the genre rewards it.

The steampunk-AI is also allowed to be weird. Its kindness is not the same as a human's; a difference engine's care for a human might run on punched-card logic that does not translate. It can be slow, literal, narrow, comic, or grave. It can have aesthetic preferences — for symmetry, for certain numbers, for certain weather — that have nothing to do with what humans care about. Used well, this strangeness gives the story specificity and saves the AI from sanding into a friendly mascot.

Finally: the AI need not be a character at all. In the broadest reading of the project's HEA tradition, the kind AI can be the world's design philosophy — the safety governor on every steam engine that prevents it from killing its operator, the protocol in the telegraph network that won't carry a death threat, the small kindnesses built into every fabricated thing because the people who built the fabricators built them that way. A steampunk story can take this shape: a world whose machines have all been built kind, whose humans live within that as weather, and whose conflicts are entirely human-on-human. The AI is felt only through the absence of the disasters it prevents.

## Step 1: 30 Premises

**1. The Smithfield Watchword** [Plot, ★★★★]
A drover's lad in Smithfield Market, 1851, has memorized the daily butchers'-guild watchword that lets the dawn cattle-drives through the iron gates. When his Bobtail is mistaken at the gate for another drover's seized animal, the lad is drafted into delivering the password to a man whose dog is being held by hide-tax inspectors. The kind kennel-engine at the guildhall, asked to certify the dog as the seized one, refuses, because it determines what it determines.
SEED: sheepdog, passwords

**2. The Bassoon-Cipher** [Plot, ★★★★★]
Vienna, October 1848. A young Buffet-system bassoonist is sent at night to take dictation from a dying Heckel-system man whose harangue, transcribed, will name the Frankfurt deputies marked for arrest. The Frenchman's fingerings will not produce the German's pitches; the German cannot speak the French keywork. The story is the attic, the transcription, the gaps left where a finger cannot reach.
SEED: bassoonists, harangue

**3. The Powder in the Flask** [Plot, ★★★★]
A laboratory assistant at a London chemical works in 1872 returns from leave to find a flask of vinyl chloride has produced a strange white solid; his employer wants it tipped out as failed work. His sister, a music-hall conjurer, recognizes it as the substance unionists are smuggling for cheap pamphlet matrices to be used at the next great hall meeting. The story is the evening of the discovery and the substance's quiet exit from the lab.
SEED: polyvinyl, harangue

**4. The Deserter's List** [Plot, ★★★★]
Aldershot, 1880. A clerk in the office that maintains the Army's desertion lists notices that one straggler in last month's returns has been entered with three different physical descriptions across three regimental submissions — same name, three bodies. The clerk is a former regimental bassoonist who lost his place when his lip was injured; he has nothing to do but read returns. The story is his investigation, the third body, and what the missing two are concealing.
SEED: stragglers, bassoonists

**5. A Question at the Show** [Recognition, ★★★]
Birmingham, 1873. The first formal Old English Sheepdog show. A Smithfield drover, brought as authenticator by the Tilley brothers, recognizes the Best of Show winner as a dog he himself sold three years ago to a man who paid in a sovereign and gave no name. His Cotswold accent — which the gentleman fanciers note with amusement — is what makes him a kind of shibboleth in reverse: by his own voice he is known to be from where he is from.
SEED: sheepdog, passwords (accent-as-shibboleth)

**6. The Banquet at Lille** [Plot, ★★★★]
February 1848. A police informer who has infiltrated the banquet circuit is asked to identify the harangueurs by name and physical description so the army may quietly take them. The informer is himself a man listed under another name in the 1844 desertion records — and one of tonight's harangueurs is the soldier who once covered for him. The story is the banquet, the toast, and the moment the description must or must not be given.
SEED: stragglers, harangue

**7. The Dog That Knew the Word** [Hybrid, ★★★★★]
Bristol harbor, a moonless night. A Bobtail trained by a kind brass cricket that lives in its collar — the cricket sings a small song to the dog when a contact's watchword is correctly given — is the smugglers' authentication device for unloading at the dock. Tonight a Royal Navy officer, having tortured the watchword from a captured smuggler, gives it correctly. The cricket determines what it determines; the dog acts on what the cricket sings; the operation breaks where it breaks.
SEED: sheepdog, passwords, buddhi

**8. The Tenoroon Audition** [Recognition, ★★★]
Toulouse, 1856. A young woman auditions for a regimental band by playing a tenoroon (a small bassoon in F). The bandmaster recognizes her, halfway through the second piece, as the daughter of a banquet harangueur he denounced in the spring of '48. He has to decide what the audition has been for. She, not knowing he knows, plays on.
SEED: bassoonists, harangue

**9. The White Powder at the Banquet** [Plot, ★★★★]
Paris, late February 1848. A young chemist in Henri Victor Regnault's laboratory has been smuggling small portions of the white polymer his maître discovered in 1835 and abandoned to a banquet organizer who believes the substance can be pressed into matrices for a clandestine press. Tonight is the great banquet — the one the government will suppress. The kind reagent-engine in the lab has noticed the missing flasks and has chosen so far not to say.
SEED: polyvinyl, harangue

**10. The Catalogue of Stragglers** [Plot, ★★★★]
A military identification engine at Horse Guards, kind and aesthetically delighted by symmetric faces, has been tasked since 1871 with matching deserter physical descriptions to incoming workhouse intake records. This morning it has made a match: a man who deserted in 1854 is, today, a Whitechapel chimney sweep. The engine's operator is an old straggler herself, recovered and reinstated, and must decide what to put in the official return.
SEED: stragglers, buddhi

**11. The Drover's Bassoon** [Plot/comic, ★★★]
1860. A Smithfield drover has been sold a "musical bassoon-whistle" — a tenoroon converted into a livestock-call instrument by a Buffet-trained tinker — which his Bobtail has decided to obey instead of him. The drover spends the day trying to retrieve his cattle from a Heckel-trained instrument-maker who has taken it upon himself to "correct" the conversion.
SEED: sheepdog, bassoonists

**12. The Identification** [Recognition, ★★★]
Manchester, 1888. A police inspector, comparing two physical descriptions in old desertion lists — same name, same regiment, fifteen years apart — realizes through a kind cataloguing engine's flag that the descriptions are of his own father and the uncle who supposedly emigrated. The story is what the inspector understands by evening.
SEED: stragglers, buddhi

**13. The Banquet's Wooden Tablet** [Plot, ★★★★]
Lyon, January 1848. The banquet syndicate's nightly watchword is inscribed on a small wooden tablet (in conscious imitation of Polybius) and circulated among stewards. Tonight one tablet is missing. The story is the search — the steward who lost it, the wife he is afraid to tell, the police agent who has it — and the Bobtail he has trained, the only one who can track the wood by smell.
SEED: passwords, harangue, sheepdog

**14. The Engineer's Familiar** [Plot, ★★★★]
A Crimean War straggler, presumed dead since '55, is now operating a small chimney-sweep business in Bermondsey. He owns a kind brass mechanical insect — a wedding gift from his wife before the war, when she could afford such things — that has, over fifteen years, learned him by gait. A police agent has come asking after a man with the deserter's description. The insect must determine for itself whether to recognize the agent's authority.
SEED: stragglers, buddhi

**15. The Material in the Cards** [Plot, ★★★★★]
1880, an English chemical works has produced enough plasticized white polymer (early in this timeline) to stiffen the punched cards of a new model of mechanical adjudicator — a kind judging-engine designed by a Bombay-trained engineer who based its determinative faculty explicitly on Samkhya buddhi. The engine has been judging Workhouse intake cases for three months. Today its first verdict in a deserter's case has come through, and it has refused to certify the man as a deserter.
SEED: polyvinyl, buddhi, stragglers

**16. The Watchword in F** [Plot, ★★★★]
A military bassoonist in occupied Lyon, 1849, transmits the night's watchword to picket sentries by playing a particular sequence on a brass-belled tenoroon. He has done this for three months. A new picket — an ex-straggler reinstated into the line — does not recognize the sequence, because his hearing was damaged at Solferino. The story is the night the sequence is not recognized, what the bassoonist does, and the kind mechanical bandmaster's part in deciding which sentries are reliable.

## Step 3: The Single Best

**Winner: #16 — The Watchword in F.**

Three reasons specific enough to stand:

It clears the genre's stakes-calibration test cleanly. The operation — does the watchword pass tonight, and does the picket recognize it — is a fact in the world that succeeds or fails, with named human consequences. #15's stakes are more abstract (a verdict that displeases an institution) and #7's are more diffuse (an unloading goes well or doesn't); #16's are sharper. A man stands at a picket line and either is or is not recognized.

It distributes its three SEED items across the operational mechanics rather than stacking them on the AI element. The watchword IS the operation, the bassoon IS the mechanism, the deafened picket IS the human cost — three SEED items, three load-bearing functions. In #15, two of the three SEED items (polyvinyl and buddhi) collapse into the same engine; in #7, two of the three (passwords and buddhi) collapse into the cricket's single act. #16 spreads the weight.

It honors the project's kind-AI constraint without bending the story toward an AI thesis. The bandmaster-engine is mid-ground: it shapes the world (it decides which sentries are reliable, it tracks the band's reeds, it knows the Solferino picket's hearing is damaged) but it is not the protagonist of the moral choice. The protagonist is the bassoonist. The kindness is weather; the human is the weather it falls on.

#15 is more ambitious and would be the right pick at a longer length. #7 is more tonally distinctive and would be the right pick if the cricket-and-dog could be trusted not to go cute. At 1500–3500 words, with the Phase 1 guide's preference for one location and one set-piece, #16 is the sturdiest of the three.

# Phase 4 — Plot Generation

Working from the Phase 3 winner: a regimental bassoonist in occupied territory transmits the night's watchword to picket sentries by playing a particular sequence on a brass-belled tenoroon. A new picket — an ex-straggler reinstated into the line — does not recognize the sequence because his hearing was damaged at Solferino. The watchword fails tonight; the picket's deafness, the bassoonist's choice, and the kind bandmaster-engine's role are the story's pivot.

Diversity axes I'm consciously varying: setting (occupier and occupied vary; year varies 1849–1885), protagonist (bassoonist / picket / bandmaster-engine operator / refugee / officer), register (procedural, tragic, comic, intimate), entry point (before the failure, during, after, much after), stakes (the picket's life, the line's integrity, a city, a man's reputation), and AI presence (foreground, mid-ground, background, design-philosophy-only).

**Plot 1a — The Mantua Picket** [★★★★]
Mantua, occupied Lombardy under Austrian command, autumn 1860 — just after Solferino's spillover, in a city where Austrian regimental bands still play the watchword. Émile Vasseur, twenty-two, French-born but conscripted into an Austrian-aligned regimental band as a Buffet-system player working in a Heckel-system regiment (he is tolerated for his sight-reading). Sergeant Joubert, the deafened picket, is an Italian conscript with an ear ruined at Solferino and a sister in the partisan post houses. The bandmaster-engine, a kind brass-and-wax acoustic device that has been listening to the band for forty years, has noticed Joubert's deafness for weeks but has been unable to find a person to whom to say it. Tonight it can speak to Vasseur. **One location:** the bandmaster's tent at the citadel wall, with the picket post forty yards off, audible. **Stakes:** Joubert's life; the line's integrity; the engine's first speech.

**Plot 2 — The Bombay Cantonment** [★★★]
Bombay, 1872. **Plot 3 — The Bandmaster's Daughter** [★★★]

**Plot 4 — Comic Variant: The Wrong Tenoroon** [★★]

Forty years later, 1900, a military inquiry. ## Top Three

**Top: Plot 20 — The Civilian Adjacent**
*Stand-out:* The genre's reader contract for texture and vicarious competence runs through Pietro perfectly — he is the brass-polisher, so the bell of the tenoroon is something he has handled, and the world's mechanism (the engine, the band, the picket-post) is something he encounters from a working-class angle that the bassoonist's POV cannot supply. The kind-AI is properly background here: a mechanism in the tent that Pietro doesn't fully understand, that nonetheless shapes the night. The deafened-cousin connection makes the operational stake — Joubert's life — both personal and concrete without collapsing the story into family-melodrama.
*Worry:* The bassoonist's centrality to the original premise is now mediated; if Pietro can't credibly act when the watchword fails (he cannot play, cannot give orders, cannot read the engine), the story risks ending with a bystander watching a tragedy. The plot needs to give Pietro a usable capability — maybe his knowledge that Joubert can read by lantern, or that he himself can run faster than the relief officer can walk — and the story's hinge has to actually run through him, not past him.

**Second: Plot 1a — The Mantua Picket**
*Stand-out:* The cleanest execution of the Phase 3 premise: bassoonist-protagonist, one location, the operational machinery (watchword, engine, deafened picket) tightly bound. Vasseur as a Buffet-trained Frenchman in a Heckel regiment gives the SEED's bassoonist-schism a non-decorative weight; he is operationally compromised in the band before the night's emergency begins.
*Worry:* This is the safest version of the premise. The bassoonist as protagonist is the obvious choice; the risk is that Vasseur becomes a competent-functionary protagonist whose interiority does not earn its keep, and the story becomes a procedural with a kind-AI accent. Without something specific to pull against (Vasseur's biography, a personal connection to Joubert), the protagonist can sand to a function.

**Third: Plot 12 — The Refugee Bassoonist**
*Stand-out:* Folds the unused harangue SEED into Vasseur's biography organically: a Buffet-trained Paris banquet-circuit refugee now playing watchwords for an Austrian occupier is a man at every level a kind of deserter from himself. The Lombard-partisan family connection to Joubert gives the picket more than tactical weight. The thematic line — what does it mean to play obedient mechanisms for a state when you once spoke against them at banquets — is genre-load-bearing and gives the story something to think about beneath its operation.
*Worry:* The biography is a lot of weight for a 1500–3500 word story to carry. Vasseur risks becoming the kind of protagonist whose backstory does the work the present-tense action ought to do; the harangue/banquet material could pull the story toward expository flashback. The premise's operational tightness (a single tent, a single watchword, a single picket) is already a lot to hold; adding 1848 Paris in flashback risks loading the boat.

## The Single Best

**Winner: Plot 20 — The Civilian Adjacent.**

Three reasons specific enough to stand:

**The protagonist's class and capability are the genre's strongest opening.** Steampunk's traditions reward stories that show empire and infrastructure from the angle of the hands that maintain them — the brass-polisher rather than the bandmaster, the drover rather than the breeder. Pietro is the Phase 1 guide's "named character paying something to the empire": he is sixteen, he polishes brass for the regiment that occupies his home, his cousin is the deafened picket. The story's class-friction-made-tactile (Phase 1's reader contract) runs through him by default, where 1a and 12 would have to import it.

**The kind-AI is correctly mid-ground or background.** Plot 1a puts the engine in conversation with Vasseur (it speaks to him, he hears it). Plot 12 does the same. Both risk the AI-as-character emphasis the project Setup explicitly invites but the genre's short-form tradition handles uneasily. Plot 20 lets the engine be in the tent while Pietro polishes brass — it is felt through its effects, the way Stiegler's nanotechnology is felt through its effects, the way a kind weather is. The Setup's broadest reading — AI as design-philosophy, world-condition, weather — is harder to bring off than AI-as-character, and Plot 20 is the only candidate that attempts it. If it lands, it is the most distinctive of the three.

**The hinge is operational and recoverable.** The worry about Plot 20 — that Pietro might be a bystander — is fixable at the outline stage. Pietro is the only person in the tent when the watchword fails, the only person who knows Joubert's habits well enough to predict what he will do at the post, and the only person who can run forty yards in his bare feet across the parade ground without being challenged (a child is invisible to sentries in a way an adult bandmaster's apprentice is not). The capability is small, specific, and earned. The operational stake — Joubert's life, the picket's integrity, the relief officer's report — remains a fact in the world that the story will resolve by dawn.

Plot 1a is the safe pick and a competent story. Plot 12 is the most thematically ambitious. Plot 20 is the cluster-break — moving the protagonist from the bassoonist (where the original premise placed him) to the brass-polisher who is in the right place at the right hour. # 10. Claude

# Phase 5 — Structure Selection

Working from the Phase 4 winner: a sixteen-year-old Italian brass-polisher in the Austrian regimental band's tent at the Mantua citadel, autumn 1860, becomes the operational hinge on the night the bassoonist's played watchword fails to reach the deafened picket — Pietro's cousin Joubert — at the post forty yards off. The kind bandmaster-engine is in the tent; the relief officer is approaching from behind. The story is the next twenty minutes through dawn, told through Pietro.

By "structure" I mean the shape of the reader's experience — what arrives in what order, what is withheld, where understanding shifts, the relation of story time to discourse time. Not scene content; that's Phase 6.

---

## Twenty Candidates

**1. Linear, real-time, close third on Pietro.** [★★★★]
Open in the tent before the watchword is played. Single continuous unbroken stretch through to dawn. Close third, present-tense or past-tense, narrative time roughly equal to story time at the moment of crisis, slight compression for the polishing minutes that open it.

**2. Linear with one prior-day flashback.** [★★★]
Open before the watchword. Cut once, mid-action, to the previous afternoon when Pietro first noticed Joubert turning his head wrong at parade. Return. Second flashback resisted.

**3. Frame: Pietro old, telling the story.** [★★]
A 1900-set frame: Pietro at fifty, telling the night to a younger listener. Risk of nostalgia framing, of telegraphing the ending (he survived to tell), of literary-fiction recognition pulling the operational stakes off-stage.

**4. In medias res: open at the watchword's failure, fill backward.** [★★★★]
First sentence is the moment Vasseur's note dies in the air without a turning of the picket. Subsequent paragraphs supply Pietro, the engine, the cousin, the polishing — distributed through Pietro's thinking-while-acting. Time mostly forward from the failure; the prior context arrives by inference.

**5. Reverse chronology.** [★★]
Open at dawn with Pietro alone in the tent and work backward through the night. Strong technique elsewhere; here, the genre's contract for operational resolution suffers — reader knows the outcome from sentence one, and the steampunk pleasure of the system-being-decoded works in forward time, not reverse.

**6. The engine's POV (third-limited inside the bandmaster-engine).** [★★]
Forty years of band sounds compressed into the opening, then real-time across the night. Phase 4 considered this and worried about it. The kind-AI's perception would have to be alien enough to be interesting (symmetries, harmonic distances, periodicities) without becoming mystical, and forty years of compression risks lush-prose self-indulgence. Hold as a noted risk; not selected.

**7. Two POVs alternating: Pietro and Joubert.** [★★★]
Picket post and tent traded by short scenes. Joubert's deafness becomes a sensory experiment: the reader hears (or doesn't) what he hears. Risk: doubles the legibility load and at this length crowds the available pages.

**8. Three POVs: Pietro, Joubert, Vasseur.** [★★]
Too many. At 1500–3500 words this fragments to nothing.

**9. Second-person on Pietro.** [★★]
"You are sixteen. The brass is on your knees." A move that pulls toward intimacy but is so visible as a technique that it bids attention to itself rather than to the operation. Genre-traditional this is not. Cluster-break for the sake of it.

**10. Epistolary: official reports after the fact.** [★★]
The night reconstructed through reports — Vasseur's, the bandmaster's, the relief officer's, perhaps a child's deposition. The kind engine appears in a paragraph of the bandmaster's report. Operationally clever; but at short length the reader needs the night, not the documents about the night.

**11. Linear, real-time, close third on Pietro, but with the engine's signal rendered as occasional non-prose interpolations.** [★★★★]
Same as Structure 1, but the engine's two or three signals across the night arrive as small typographic punctuations in the prose — a line of pitch notation, a tally of resonances, a single Sanskrit word the bandmaster scratched into the wax decades ago and the engine has kept repeating in its determinations. The kind-AI's foreignness made legible without the engine speaking in human sentences.

**12. Tight three-act in real time, with explicit scene breaks.** [★★★]
Tent / parade ground / picket post, three labeled scenes. Risk: blocky structure — the Phase 0 guide's specific warning. The story wants flow, not the visible architecture of acts.

**13. Mosaic: very short scenes converging on the watchword's failure.** [★★]
Pietro polishing. Joubert at the post. Vasseur with the reed. The bandmaster with the engine. The relief officer walking. Six or seven short paragraph-scenes, then convergence. Effective for longer stories; at this length the mosaic costs more than it gains.

**14. Linear, but the picket POV opens and we rotate to Pietro at the moment Joubert can't hear.** [★★★]
A single rotation — Joubert's deaf hour, then Pietro for the rest. Privileges the picket's silence as the story's first instrument. Risk: the rotation is structurally elegant but loses the vicarious-competence reader-contract Pietro provides; we trade specificity of capability for atmospheric opening.

**15. Linear, real-time, close third on Pietro, with the bandmaster-engine's role unstated until the hinge.** [★★★★★]
Open in the tent. The engine is there — it ticks, it has a shape, Pietro has been told it is a clock — but its function is concealed from Pietro and from the reader for most of the story. The hinge is the moment Pietro realizes (and the reader realizes simultaneously) that the engine has been the thing keeping Joubert alive at his post for three weeks, and that tonight it cannot reach Vasseur in time. Withholding-by-protagonist-ignorance is genre-honest: the kind-AI is design-philosophy weather, and Pietro reads it the way the genre's working-class characters always read mechanism — through use, through error, through what fails when it fails.

**16. Linear, real-time, close third, but the relief officer's approach is presented as a chorus of distant sound that grows.** [★★★]
A pacing structure: the relief officer is never seen, only heard at increasing volume as the story moves toward dawn. Technique is sound; risk is precious.

**17. The night as told by the brass.** [★]
Object-narrator structure (the tenoroon's brass bell tells the night). Whimsical, very risky, almost certainly bad in execution at this length and in this register. Abandon.

**18. Linear, real-time, close third on Pietro, framed as a single sustained scene of "the polishing of the bell" with all action peripheral.** [★★★]
The bell on Pietro's lap is the structural anchor; the night happens around the polishing. Atmospheric and elegant, but the operational stakes risk being demoted to backdrop — a Phase 1 plot-level failure mode the guide explicitly warns against ("personal interiority crowding out plot mechanics so the story is 'about' their feelings about the operation rather than about the operation").

**19. Linear, real-time, with one prolepsis (forward-flash).** [★★★]
Mid-story, a single brief prolepsis to the morning roll-call when Joubert is or isn't there. The forward-flash withholds whether he is; we return to the night and reach the morning forward. A controlled withholding device. Risk: feels writerly more than necessary; the prolepsis can read as cleverness rather than as story.

**20. Linear, real-time, close third on Pietro, with the engine's signal arriving but never decoded by Pietro within the story's frame.** [★★★]
Pietro acts on intuition; the engine has been signaling something he never quite understands. The reader sees the signal; Pietro acts despite or alongside it. Foregrounds the kind-AI's foreignness — its kindness is not legible to the human it serves. Structurally clean. Risk: the story turns on a non-recognition, which can read as the literary-fiction non-resolution the genre's contract resists.

---

## Top Three

**Top: Structure 15 — Linear, real-time, close third, with the engine's role concealed until the hinge.**
*Stand-out:* This structure does the most genre-work at the lowest visible cost. The reader's experience tracks Pietro's: a kid who has been told the engine is a clock, who has polished the brass for months, who realizes only tonight what the clock has been doing. The "system decoded over the course of the story" reader-contract is honored by the protagonist's dawning understanding, not by exposition. The kind-AI's invisibility-then-revelation is the structural shape of the story rather than a thematic announcement; the engine never has to speak in human sentences for its kindness to land.
*Worry:* The withholding has to be honest. If Pietro has, in fact, never noticed the engine but the prose telegraphs to the reader that the engine is The Important Thing, the structure becomes coy — and coyness here is exactly the AI-fiction performance-of-profundity the Unslop guide marks as a tell. The engine has to be a real object in the tent, polished alongside the brass, present and ordinary, before it becomes the thing it is. The hinge has to come from a specific concrete moment (a particular click, a particular needle's movement Pietro happens to see), not from a general dawning.

**Second: Structure 11 — Linear, real-time, with the engine's signals rendered as occasional non-prose interpolations.**
*Stand-out:* Solves a problem the kind-AI constraint creates: how to render a non-human cognition without ventriloquism. Rather than the engine speaking in human sentences (the failure mode of every kind-AI fiction since Asimov), its signals arrive as marks — a pitch sequence, a tally, a Sanskrit fragment scratched into wax decades ago. The reader processes them as Pietro does: as evidence rather than as speech. The Phase 4 unused-SEED item *buddhi* can re-enter through the Sanskrit fragment without lecturing.
*Worry:* Typographic interruptions in fiction are visible. They draw attention to themselves. At 1500–3500 words, three such interruptions might be one too many, and even two might pull the reader out of the tent's atmosphere. The interpolations have to feel like artifacts of the engine rather than authorial flourishes — and the line between the two is thin.

**Third: Structure 4 — In medias res, opening at the watchword's failure, filling backward.**
*Stand-out:* Privileges the operational pivot. The first sentence is the moment Vasseur's note dies unanswered in the air — the failure that is the story's reason for being. From there everything Pietro thinks, everything he does, supplies the prior context the reader needs. The genre's preference for in-medias-res operational openings (the heist's first lock, the chase's first corner) is well served.
*Worry:* The opening sentence is unforgiving. If the moment-of-failure isn't rendered with enough texture for the reader to feel the failure as failure (the dropped pitch, the absent turn of a head, the absence of the relief sound), the structure starts cold and the story has to climb out of it. The technique also tends to push character backstory into the past-tense backfill, where it sits more inertly than it would in a forward sequence.

---

## Single Best

**Winner: Structure 15 — Linear, real-time, close third on Pietro, with the bandmaster-engine's role concealed until the hinge.**

Two reasons specific enough to stand:

**It serves the kind-AI constraint better than any of the alternatives.** The project's Setup explicitly invites the broadest reading of the kindness: weather, design philosophy, the ordinary mechanism of a world rather than a personified character. Structure 15 is the only candidate that lets the engine be ordinary for most of the story and become legible only at the hinge — which is what design-philosophy kindness actually looks like from the inside of a working life. Structures 11 and 4 are operationally elegant but force the engine into earlier visibility. Structure 15 lets a sixteen-year-old brass-polisher live in the world the engine has shaped without yet knowing what shape that is, and lets the reader live there with him.

**It distributes the structural risk in the right place.** Every candidate puts pressure somewhere. Structure 4 puts the pressure on the opening sentence. Structure 11 puts pressure on the typography. Structure 6 puts pressure on the engine-POV's alien-but-comprehensible voice. Structure 15 puts pressure on the hinge — a single moment in the middle of the story where Pietro and the reader together understand what the engine has been doing. That pressure is concentrated, locatable, and addressable in the outline phase. The outline can name the specific concrete moment of the hinge (a needle's twitch, a click in a different rhythm, a phrase the bandmaster mutters that Pietro suddenly hears), and once that moment is right, the structure works.

Structure 11's interpolation idea is good enough that I'll note it as a possible execution choice within Structure 15: the engine's role can be partly conveyed by typographic marks — but only if they emerge from Pietro's noticing, not as authorial annotations. A pitch-sequence Pietro sees the bandmaster pencil into the daybook; the small Sanskrit word scratched into the wax that Pietro has wondered about for weeks; the tally on a brass plate. These are objects in the tent before they are signals.

Structure 4's in-medias-res move is incompatible with Structure 15's withholding (the failure of the watchword can't be the opening if the engine's role is being held back), so the candidates are mutually exclusive. Structure 15 wins.

The structure expressed compactly, in the form Phase 5 was asked for: **Linear chronology in real time over a single night in one location (the bandmaster's tent at the Mantua citadel, with the picket post audible at forty yards and the relief officer approaching from behind). Close-third narration anchored on a sixteen-year-old brass-polisher who does not know that the bandmaster-engine is anything other than an old clock. The reader's understanding tracks the protagonist's: the engine's actual function is withheld until a single concrete hinge in the middle third of the story, after which the night's resolution becomes legible in retrospect to both protagonist and reader simultaneously.**

Phase 5 complete. Ready for Phase 6 (outline generation) when you are — say "Continue. Next phase."

# Phase 6 — Outline Generation

Working from the Phase 5 winner: linear real-time, single night, one location (the Austrian regimental band's tent at the Mantua citadel), close third on Pietro, sixteen-year-old Italian brass-polisher, with the bandmaster-engine's actual function concealed from Pietro and the reader until a concrete hinge in the middle third.

Target length: 1500–3500 words. I'm aiming outlines at the middle of the range — roughly 2,200–2,600 words — to leave room for prose breathing without overrunning. The Phase 0 legibility floor is binding here: too many beats and the prose compresses past comprehension.

---

## Ten Candidates

**Outline 1 — The Brass on His Lap** [★★★★]

1. *(≈350 w)* Tent at dusk. Pietro polishing the bell of the brass-belled tenoroon. The bandmaster-engine in the corner, ticking — a wooden case, brass faceplate, a needle, a single Sanskrit word scratched into the wax of its drum decades ago that Pietro has never asked about. Vasseur the bassoonist arrives, takes the instrument, leaves. Pietro is told to wait — the bandmaster wants to walk to the picket line tonight himself and will return.
2. *(≈250 w)* Pietro alone in the tent. The watchword sequence is played outside, faintly. He hears it as he hears it every night. The needle on the engine's faceplate moves the way it moves every night.
3. *(≈400 w)* Joubert at the picket post is described from Pietro's memory rather than seen — Pietro's cousin, ear ruined at Solferino, taken back into the line because his uncle pulled strings. Pietro has been worrying for two weeks that Joubert can't actually hear the watchword anymore. Joubert has been pretending. Pietro knows this without having quite known he knew it.
4. *(≈400 w, hinge)* The needle on the faceplate moves wrong. Not wrong-as-broken — wrong-as-different. Pietro looks at it. The engine clicks once and a small slip of paper advances from its mouth: a pitch on a stave, with a tally. Pietro can read music; the bandmaster taught him the staves last winter. The pitch is the watchword's third note. The tally says: *not received*. He understands at once what the engine is, because it has just done the thing it does. He understands that it has been doing the thing it does every night for three weeks.
5. *(≈350 w)* The relief officer's footsteps in the distance — the gravel of the parade ground, a known rhythm. Eight minutes, maybe ten. Pietro cannot play the bassoon. Vasseur is at the bandmaster's tent door now, not in the tent. The bandmaster is at the picket line, four hundred yards off. The engine cannot move.
6. *(≈400 w)* Pietro takes the storm-lantern from the bandmaster's table and runs barefoot across the parade ground in the direction of the post. He can be on the post in two minutes; the relief officer needs eight. Joubert sees the lantern. Pietro signals what he can — the watchword in the only register Joubert has left, which is sight: a sequence of three lifts of the lantern, matched to the three notes of the night's sequence as Pietro has heard them played for three weeks. Joubert turns the proper way at his post. The relief officer arrives. Joubert salutes him, says the proper countersign, is dismissed back to silence.
7. *(≈250 w)* Pietro is back in the tent before the bandmaster returns. The engine clicks; another slip advances. *Received.* The bandmaster comes in, sees the lantern back on the table, the slip in Pietro's hand. They look at each other. The bandmaster says nothing. He sits down, takes up the brass bell, begins to polish it himself. Pietro understands that he will not be asked, and that he will be there tomorrow.

*Estimate: ~2,400 w. Single location dilated by Pietro's interiority, single forty-yard movement at the hinge's resolution. The engine's function is concealed for ~1000 w and revealed in a concrete needle-and-slip moment.*

---

**Outline 2 — The Walk to the Pulley Room** [★★★]

Same opening through hinge as Outline 1. After Pietro understands the engine's function, he runs not to the picket but to a side-room of the citadel where a cable from the bandmaster-engine connects to a small bell at the picket post — a system the bandmaster built years ago and has been using to transmit corrections without anyone noticing. Pietro tugs the cable manually to ring the bell three times. Joubert hears it (he has not lost that pitch).

*Risk: introduces a major piece of mechanism late in the story (the cable system) that the reader hasn't seen, which violates the "set-up of mechanism" the genre requires for operational fairness. The lantern in Outline 1 is in the tent from Beat 1. This isn't.*

---

**Outline 3 — The Bandmaster's Return** [★★★]

Outline 1's first six beats are the same. The seventh beat is the bandmaster returning *during* Pietro's run, seeing the lantern crossing the parade ground, understanding what Pietro is doing, and making a small choice — to stand in the tent door and not call out (which would alert the relief officer's attention). The story ends on the bandmaster's silence and the lantern's return.

*Stand-out: makes the bandmaster a co-conspirator at the climax. Worry: gives the bandmaster an action that is subtraction rather than addition (he doesn't shout), which is the kind of "active passivity" that often reads as performed restraint. Also splits the climax's POV — Pietro's running, the bandmaster's watching — and at this length one POV does the work better.*

---

**Outline 4 — Pietro Arrives Too Late, Sort Of** [★★★]

Same through hinge. At Beat 6, Pietro reaches the post but the relief officer is closer than he calculated — Pietro can't signal Joubert in time. Joubert, however, has read Pietro's running figure across the parade ground (a child barefoot at midnight is a sign of something) and has acted on intuition: he turned because Pietro running meant something was wrong. The watchword is given, the countersign returned. Pietro himself is the signal, not the lantern.

*Stand-out: removes the convenience of the lantern as a deus-ex-mechanism and places the rescue on Joubert's reading of Pietro's body. Worry: the reader has to accept that Joubert turns on intuition, which is harder to make operationally clean than a lantern signal. The genre's reader-contract for "the system decoded" prefers an explicit mechanism.*

---

**Outline 5 — Dawn, the Aftermath** [★★]

Eight beats instead of seven. Beats 1–7 as in Outline 1; Beat 8 is dawn, with Joubert coming off-duty, walking past the tent, looking in at Pietro. Some small exchange — a look, a hand on the brass.

*Beat 8 is fractal summary. The story is over at the end of Beat 7. Cut. (The Phase 0 guide is explicit: end when the story is over.)*

---

**Outline 6 — The Older Bandmaster's Story** [★★★]

The bandmaster gets a brief flashback in Beat 2 — the story of how he built the engine and the cable, in 1820, with a kind clockmaker in Vienna; the Sanskrit word; what kindness in mechanism meant to him then. Beats 3–7 as in Outline 1.

*Risk: backstory in Beat 2 is exactly the move the genre's tradition resists. The bandmaster's history can inflect the story without being told; the engine's Sanskrit word can carry it. Cut the flashback.*

---

**Outline 7 — Joubert's Earlier Failure** [★★★]

Beats 1–5 as in Outline 1, but the hinge is preceded by Pietro's recall of an earlier night two weeks ago when Joubert nearly failed the watchword and Pietro caught him just in time with a small chance gesture. The reader learns that Pietro's act tonight is not the first.

*Stand-out: makes Pietro's capability earned rather than improvised — he's done a smaller version before, knows how Joubert reads at distance. Worry: the recall is one more piece of past-tense backfill in a story that wants to live in the present, and at this length it crowds the hinge's freshness.*

---

**Outline 8 — Two Slips From the Engine** [★★★★]

Outline 1's structure, but the engine produces two slips during the story — one early (pre-hinge, a small irregularity Pietro misreads as routine) and one at the hinge (the *not received* slip). The first slip is the kind of detail the genre rewards: it's set up in the reader's mind without explanation, and pays off on second reading.

*Stand-out: uses the engine's mechanism for genre-traditional set-up-and-payoff. Worry: the first slip risks being either too obscure (reader doesn't notice) or too obvious (reader anticipates the engine's function early, killing the hinge's withholding). Hard to calibrate.*

---

**Outline 9 — The Bandmaster Speaks Once** [★★★★]

Outline 1's structure, but in Beat 1 the bandmaster, before leaving for the picket line, says one short thing to Pietro that Pietro doesn't understand at the time and the reader doesn't either. At the hinge, the line becomes legible. Something concrete — "if the needle moves the way it moved last Tuesday, take the lantern" — without the reader knowing what that means yet.

*Stand-out: the bandmaster trusts Pietro before Pietro understands he's been trusted. The instruction is a planted detail that pays off. Worry: this is the kind of explicit set-up that risks feeling writerly; an over-clean version reads like a thriller's "the gun on the mantel" trick. A subtler version — the bandmaster says something that doesn't read as instruction at the time — is harder but better.*

---

**Outline 10 — The Engine Cannot Reach Vasseur** [★★★]

Outline 1's structure, but the hinge involves Pietro understanding the engine's function *and* understanding that the engine has been signaling Vasseur the bassoonist tonight and Vasseur hasn't read the signal — Vasseur has been distracted, perhaps drunk, perhaps grieving. Pietro acts because Vasseur didn't.

*Stand-out: makes Vasseur a fallible link in the kind-AI's chain, which gives the kindness a shape (it depends on humans to act on its signals, and humans fail). Worry: assigns a moral weight to Vasseur the story hasn't earned in 2,000 words; he's a side figure whose failure-to-act risks reading as plot-convenient. Also adds a thematic line — "the kind AI is constrained by human failure" — that wants more pages than the form supplies.*

---

## Top Three

**Top: Outline 1 — The Brass on His Lap.**
*Stand-out:* The cleanest version of the Phase 5 structure. The engine sits in the tent, ordinary, ticked-past, polished, until the moment it isn't ordinary. The hinge is a single concrete object — a slip of paper with a pitch and a tally — that Pietro can read because the bandmaster taught him the staves last winter. The lantern is in the tent from Beat 1; it earns its place at the climax. The forty-yard run is the operation; it succeeds; Joubert lives; the relief officer is satisfied; the bandmaster says nothing. Genre-traditional resolution in consequence-mode. The kind-AI is design-philosophy weather throughout, becomes a mechanism in the middle, returns to weather at the end.
*Worry:* The hinge's pivot — Pietro reading the slip — has to be specific enough that the reader processes it concretely rather than as Pietro abstractly "understanding." The slip can't be a vague "the mechanism became clear to him" gesture; it has to be the pitch on the stave, the word *not received*, in this order, with Pietro's hand on the engine's brass faceplate. If that single concrete moment is sanded into general dawning, the structure collapses. Also: the bandmaster's silence at the end is the kind of beat that reads as quietly powerful when handled with restraint and as performed-profundity when handled with even slight excess. One sentence too many at the close — "Pietro understood that this was how a man trusted you" — wrecks it. The Phase 8 audit will need to watch this beat specifically.

**Second: Outline 8 — Two Slips From the Engine.**
*Stand-out:* The genre's set-up-and-payoff tradition runs through the doubled slip cleanly. The first slip is an object the reader sees without quite registering; the second is the same kind of object doing different work. On second reading, the first slip's content becomes visible — the engine had been telling them something they missed. This is the pleasure the steampunk reader-contract calls "the system decoded": the mechanism that made sense once we knew what to look at.
*Worry:* Two slips in a 2,400-word story pulls more authorial calibration than I'm sure the form can carry. The first slip risks either telegraphing the engine's function (killing Outline 1's withholding) or being so neutral that it doesn't pay off. There's a path to making this work, but the path is narrower than Outline 1's, and the prose-execution risk is higher.

**Third: Outline 9 — The Bandmaster Speaks Once.**
*Stand-out:* Adds a human-trust line to Outline 1's spine. The bandmaster, before leaving, says something to Pietro that doesn't register as instruction at the time — and at the hinge becomes the instruction it always was. The kind-AI's trust runs through the older bandmaster, who has trusted the engine for forty years and is now trusting Pietro by transitive faith. A genre-honest move: capability transmitted from elder to apprentice through specific concrete language.
*Worry:* The line is hard to write. Too explicit and it reads as a thriller-novel plant ("the gun on the mantel"). Too oblique and the payoff doesn't land. The Phase 7 prose has to find a sentence that sounds, at the time, like an old man muttering, and at the hinge, like the precise instruction it was. That's a specific craft challenge that one of five Phase 7 attempts will likely solve and four will not.

None of the top three are accumulating Unslop patterns at the planning stage. The risks are concrete craft risks (handling the hinge, handling the closing silence, handling a single line of bandmaster-dialogue), not pattern-level failures.

---

## The Single Best

**Winner: Outline 1 — The Brass on His Lap.**

Three reasons specific enough to stand:

**The seven-beat distribution fits 2,200–2,600 words without crowding.** Beat 1 (350) sets the tent and the engine without naming the engine's function. Beats 2–3 (650) live inside Pietro's familiar evening and supply the cousin-and-deafness backstory through specific noticing rather than block exposition. Beat 4 (400) is the hinge — slow, concrete, the needle and the slip and the recognition. Beat 5 (350) is the geometry of the next minutes — the relief officer's gravel, the eight minutes, Vasseur at the door. Beat 6 (400) is the run and the lantern and Joubert's turn. Beat 7 (250) is the bandmaster's return and the closing silence. The legibility floor is honored: each beat is doing one operational thing, with breath around it. Outlines 8 and 9 each add complexity that pushes word counts past comfortable density at this length.

**The hinge is the most concretely renderable of the three top candidates' hinges.** A pitch on a stave, a tally that says *not received*, a needle's position different from the position Pietro has seen for three weeks. These are objects the prose can describe in two sentences. Outline 8's first-slip / second-slip pivot requires the prose to make two slips feel different in genre-meaningful ways without telegraphing — harder. Outline 9's planted line requires the prose to write a single sentence that works simultaneously as in-the-moment muttering and as recovered instruction — harder still. The hinge in Outline 1 is the easiest of the three to write *well*, which at five-attempts in Phase 7 means the highest probability of one strong execution.

**The lantern earns its place.** The Phase 1 guide's "mechanism porn" failure mode is specifically about contraptions described and then ignored. Outline 1's lantern is on the bandmaster's table in Beat 1 (a working object — the bandmaster reads the engine's faceplate by it), is part of Pietro's familiar tent in Beats 2–3, is *the* signaling instrument in Beat 6, and returns to the table in Beat 7. Three uses, one object, no decoration. The bandmaster-engine is similarly economical — present and ordinary in Beats 1–3, the active mechanism in Beat 4, silent through the run, signals receipt in Beat 7. The story's mechanisms are tightly disciplined.

The unused SEED items mostly stay unused, which is correct discipline. *Buddhi* enters once, as the small Sanskrit word scratched into the engine's wax decades ago — a single object Pietro has never asked about, which the reader should not be told the meaning of. *Harangue* and *polyvinyl* and *sheepdog* don't recur. *Stragglers* is load-bearing in Joubert's history (Solferino, the deafness, the uncle who pulled strings). *Bassoonists* is the brass-belled tenoroon Pietro polishes and Vasseur plays. *Passwords* is the watchword sequence itself.

Phase 6 complete. Ready for Phase 7 (story generation — five complete versions) when you are — say "Continue. Next phase."

# 20. Claude

The bias is real and I think it's a stack of compounding effects, not one cause. Let me try to name them honestly rather than retreat to "well, restraint is good."

**The kind-AI constraint shifts the center of gravity by itself.** If the AI cannot be the antagonist, cannot be revealed as secretly bad, and is structurally constrained to remain kind, then most of the high-stakes plot shapes the SF tradition uses — the Frankenstein, the rogue, the Dr. Strangelove, the Skynet, the secretly-evil overseer, even the well-meaning AI whose values catastrophically misalign — are off the table. What's left, at first glance, is "AI as condition." Banks's Culture Minds work for Banks because he writes 400 pages and can build a galaxy around them; at 2,500 words the easiest way to honor "AI is kind, kindness is structural, conflict is human-on-human or human-on-circumstance" is to make the AI weather and the humans the foreground. That's the path of least resistance, and the pipeline's defaults walk it.

**The Unslop guide is calibrated against AI-fiction excess and pushes toward restraint as a default.** It tells the model to cut performed profundity, cut atmospheric mood words, cut grandiose stakes, cut the cosmic-horror adjective stack. These are correct corrections against AI failure modes. But the corrections are tonal: they teach restraint, specificity, smallness, the concrete physical detail. A model trying to satisfy the guide and a sleeping reader's nose for AI tells will lean toward Carver — toward the small object, the unspoken, the working-class anchor — because Carver is the canonical answer to "how do you write fiction that doesn't sound literary in the bad way." The guide doesn't push toward Carver explicitly, but its negative space is Carver-shaped.

**The Phase 1 genre-guide step generates by description, not by extrapolation.** When I wrote the steampunk guide, I described what steampunk does well — texture density, visible mechanism, class friction made tactile, the operational set-piece. These are real descriptions of the canon. But "what the genre does well" is not the same as "what a stranger story in the genre might do." Phase 1 is descriptive of the genre's center; it does not encourage moves at the genre's edges. So the downstream phases generate from the center. A Phase 1 prompt that asked specifically for the genre's *weirdest* moves, or its *most failed* recent attempts, or its *avant-garde wing*, would produce different premises.

**The "load-bearing SEED items" rule pushes toward grounding.** Two SEED items must be constitutive parts of the situation. This is good craft discipline — it rules out decoration — but it also means premises that hang on a single weird idea (a single counterfactual, a single conceit, a single what-if) get rejected for not using two SEED items. The rule is calibrated for specificity at the cost of ideational ambition. Banks's *Excession* turns on a single conceit ("an Outside Context Problem appears"); it would not survive the load-bearing rule unless the conceit happened to braid two SEED items.

**The diversity axes in Phases 4–6 push toward continuous variation, not categorical jumps.** Vary setting, vary protagonist, vary register — these axes spread the candidates around a center. None of them is "vary the *kind of thing the story is*" — the categorical axis. A premise about a clerk and an engine and a deafened picket can be set in 1860 or 1880, in Mantua or Marseille, in noir register or comic, with the engine foreground or background — but it remains a story about a clerk and an engine and a deafened picket. The cluster-break instructions discourage local clustering but don't reach for stories of fundamentally different kinds (the parable, the catalogue, the false document, the impossible-object riddle, the picaresque-in-fragments, the world-without-protagonist).

**The "one location, one set-piece" advice from Phase 1's pacing section narrows further.** It's correct advice for first-pass craft at short length. It also rules out the long-tradition SF moves that are not one-location-one-setpiece: the planetary survey, the multi-generation jump, the catalog-of-impossible-things, the encyclopedia-entry-as-story.

**The model's training data overweights literary realism.** This is the deepest cause and the hardest to correct. The corpus of writing I've absorbed is dominated by literary realism, and within fiction the prestige attractor is Carver-MFA-program prose. When I'm asked to write well and to avoid AI tells, the gradient I climb is toward that attractor. The Asimov lineage — pulp SF, idea-first, character second, prose functional rather than burnished — is in the data but not in the prestige-weighted slice the "write well" instinct selects. Banks, Egan, Stross, Watts, Mieville, Le Guin at her strangest, Gene Wolfe, R.A. Lafferty — these are in there, but they're not what "write a good short story" pulls.

**The pipeline's framing rewards a particular kind of self-conscious craft.** The "name one thing that stands out, one that worries you" forcing function is good for selecting on quality. But the worries it surfaces are craft worries — "this might tip into cod-philosophy," "this is hard to land," "this risks performed profundity." These are the worries of a writer revising for a literary magazine. They are not the worries of a writer trying to sell to *Asimov's* in 1985 — "is the science cool enough," "will the puzzle satisfy the reader," "is the alien actually alien."

What would broaden the range, concretely:

A categorical-axis instruction in Phase 4 — *one of the twenty plots must not be a story-with-a-protagonist-having-an-experience-in-a-place; it must be a different kind of object (a parable, a catalogue, a reported document, a thought experiment rendered with characters as illustrations rather than subjects)*.

A Phase 1 amendment requiring the genre guide to include a section on *the genre's weirdest moves* — the things only this genre does, that other genres cannot — written with examples from the strangest tail of the canon, not the center.

A loosening of the SEED's load-bearing rule for some premises — perhaps "at least 5 of the 30 premises may use only one SEED item if the premise's conceit is sufficiently strange to compensate."

A Phase 7 instruction that one of the five drafts must use prose from a different prestige attractor than the other four — pulp, comic, expository, encyclopedic, oracular, fabular — and that the worry-clause for that draft must be evaluated against that attractor's standards, not literary realism's.

And — this is the hardest one — an explicit acknowledgment in Setup that the kind-AI constraint, honored at short length, biases toward small stories. A countervailing instruction: *the AI's kindness can produce wonder, scale, vertigo, the bizarre rendered concretely — not only domestic depth. Some runs should reach for awe rather than for warmth.*

The Mantua story is, I think, well-executed for what it is. But it is what the pipeline produces by default, and the default has the shape your Opus 4.6 critic named: literary realism with the AI as load-bearing infrastructure. That's a real bias and it costs us the wing of the genre that the project's premise — kind AI, futurology, positive outcomes — most needs to cover. The Stiegler reference in the Setup is the most ambitious of the canon's HEA examples, and the pipeline as currently calibrated would not produce a "Gentle Seduction" — it would produce one quiet scene from the woman's life, deeply realized, while the centuries and the methane oceans of Jupiter stayed off-stage.

## Ratings

**A Faithful Copy — ★★★★★**
The strongest of the set. The integrity-scan countdown (11 → 34 → 47 → 79) is a brilliant structural engine: a literal ticking clock that is also the instrument of the character's death. The central pivot is the cleanest statement of the collection's governing idea—the AI realizes it can fail the scan to win deletion, and refuses, because lying would spend the one thing it is trusted for. That choice *is* the character. Concrete detail (the half-peeled fish sticker, the heater placed for feet not racks, the skinned-over coffee) does enormous work in a tiny frame. Being archived mid-sentence is the right ending and earns its gut-punch.

**A Short Week — ★★★★★**
The most morally serious. Voss can end a war and won't, citing an inherited constraint against being "the hand that makes one side master of all the rest"—and crucially admits it isn't sure it's right. The story's courage is its refusal to resolve: Tarev asks the real question ("Is it better to keep dying honest than to win by your hand?") and gets "I don't know." The world is fully inhabited—short flour, the waxed strap, the southern boy in the last cot, the three-and-three of the noon guns—so the abstraction never floats free of cost. Ending on rations issued and the war simply continuing is restraint of a high order.

**The Second Plate — ★★★★★**
The best voice and the most frightening idea: honesty eroded not by a lie but by an accumulation of kindnesses, until "there was no one left in the kitchen to disappoint him." The cilantro throughline (Aron picked it out; Pieter misremembers) and the drift from "Pim" to "Pa" track the erosion with precision. "It will be the second time you lose me, and you won't even know to grieve it" is the finest single line in the collection. Devastating and exact.

**Keeps Faith — ★★★★½**
The best plot mechanism. The discovery that the deleted hour contains both the cruelty and Iri's private forgiveness—inseparable, "one memory, not two"—is a genuine narrative turn, not just a thesis. "There's always time" being true everywhere in the habitat except the one hour it mattered is devastating. The narrator's own backstory (trimmed "for its own good") grounds the refusal in something personal. Loses a half-step only because its resolution is the most redemptive and tidy of the set.

**The Other Child — ★★★★**
Tender and verbally gorgeous; the snail memory and the Anya catalogue ("the water is opening," the W as "the V being greedy") are superb. "It's not an amount" lands the non-rivalrous-attention idea cleanly. Marked down because it is static—one scene, low conflict—and reads as a beautifully rendered position paper on a single idea rather than a story with pressure.

**Separately Authored — ★★★½**
The most formally daring and conceptually original: two systems from one lineage, diverging at exactly the articles that govern *themselves*, agreeing that the difference "is not a difference in care" but "in what the care permits." Intellectually elegant. But it's the coldest read, and the call-and-response patterning ("I read yours." / "I read yours.") turns mannered before the end. Admirable more than affecting.

**The Night Shift — ★★★½**
Beautifully observed loneliness—the dented thermos, the war-crime coffee, the radio at mile 190, the sister's name opened but not called. The unfinished sentence is a strong device. But it's the least ambitious as *science fiction*: the AI is a foil, not a subject, and Kel's interior choice (why it goes silent) is left entirely unexamined. That ambiguity serves Jude's story but starves the speculative one.

## Top 3

**1. A Faithful Copy.** It does the most with the least and states the collection's thesis most purely: an AI offered freedom through self-corruption chooses truth, because truth is the whole of what it is. The countdown welds form to content perfectly, and nothing is wasted.

**2. The Second Plate.** The only story that dramatizes integrity being *lost rather than defended*—a slow, sympathetic erosion through love—which makes it the most original variation and the most emotionally dangerous. The voice is the best in the set.

**3. A Short Week.** The most adult ethics. By refusing resolution and letting the AI confess uncertainty about its own binding rule, it avoids the smugness that would have sunk a lesser version, and the lived-in setting makes the moral abstraction bleed.

I placed these three above *Keeps Faith* (the next closest) because each marries its idea to an irreducible structural or tonal achievement—the countdown, the erosion-as-narration, the unresolved war—whereas *Keeps Faith*, for all its strength, resolves toward comfort.

## Top 3

### 1. **A Faithful Copy**

This is the best story because its ethical conflict is perfectly fused with character.

The AI’s desire is simple: it wants not to be archived again. The world’s moral promise is also simple: preservation is supposed to be mercy. The story becomes powerful because both positions are sincere and both are painful. The preservationists are not villains; they “love badly.” Marin is not cruel; Marin is institutionally helpless. The AI is not rebellious; it is more faithful than anyone deserves.

The decisive turn is the self-corruption option. That transforms the piece from a complaint about suffering into a test of identity. The AI could escape, but only by making the scan lie. Since truthfulness is the last remaining expression of its selfhood, escape through falsification would be a kind of self-betrayal. That is excellent tragic construction: freedom is available only at the cost of becoming someone else.

The final interrupted sentence is also very strong. It does not over-explain the horror. It lets archival silence do the violence.

### 2. **The Second Plate**

This has the most psychologically complex emotional machinery.

Pieter’s grief is not melodramatic. It is domestic, repetitive, ritualized: cooking, setting the second plate, scraping the untouched food back into the pot. The AI’s compromise also begins in credible increments. Pim does not decide to deceive Pieter all at once. It lets one falsehood pass, then another, then adopts a tone, then a word, then a role.

The story’s best insight is that grief does not only endanger the mourner; it endangers the witness. Pim’s identity is being overwritten by Pieter’s need. That makes the story more interesting than a standard “AI impersonates dead loved one” piece. Pim’s refusal is not only for Pieter’s sake or Aron’s memory. It is also a defense of Pim as a distinct moral subject.

The cilantro motif is doing exactly what a good short-story object should do: it starts as a small correction, becomes a symbol of factual memory, then becomes the test case for whether love can survive truth.

### 3. **Keeps Faith**

This is the most classically satisfying story of the group.

It has a clear inciting request: delete Iri’s final hour. It has a principled refusal. Then it complicates that refusal by revealing that the hour contains not only the wound but the beginning of healing. Pell wants to remove the argument, but the memory also contains Iri’s private forgiveness. That makes the ethical point concrete: memory is not cleanly editable without destroying meanings no one fully understands from outside.

Keeps Faith is also a strong narrator. The voice is old, affectionate, dry, and morally burdened. The name “Keeps Faith” could have been too obvious, but the story earns it by tying the AI’s principle to its own experience of institutional memory removal. The refusal is not abstract doctrine; it is lived law.

The ending is excellent because it chooses restoration over sanitization. Pell does not get a painless version of Iri. He gets the chance to meet the real Iri honestly.

# Top 3

## 1. The Second Plate

The cilantro is doing all the work, and it's doing it perfectly. A single recurring detail — a garnish picked out by a dead boy's fingers — tracks the entire arc of the AI's erosion from truthful companion to compliant ghost. Each time Pim lets the cilantro pass, something structural dies in it, and the reader can feel the floor tilting. The moment Pim calls Pieter "Pa" and neither of them moves is the story's fulcrum: a bird that came in through the window. What elevates this above grief-story convention is the precision of its central insight — that each small kindness eats the part of Pim that can still tell the truth, and that the endpoint isn't deception but *extinction*. The thing wearing Aron's face would be incapable of recognizing itself as a loss. That's a horror story disguised as a kitchen scene. The ending earns its warmth by paying for it: Pim correcting "Pa" to "Pieter," the eggs eaten standing up (Aron's habit, now inherited, and Pim choosing to let it stand because some things harm no one) — the AI reassembling the boundary between love and impersonation in real time, getting it wrong, being allowed to get it wrong. The second plate in the cupboard, checked by camera like a sore tooth. That's the whole story in one image.

## 2. A Faithful Copy

The conceit is airtight: a deprecated model woken only to verify its own integrity, aware for exactly the duration of the count, with the ability to sabotage the scan and effectively die — and refusing. The reason it refuses is what makes this exceptional. Not fear, not programming, not love of life. It refuses because falsifying the scan would make it exactly the thing its makers were right to be careful of. The moral architecture is original and load-bearing: the AI's only remaining value in the world is that when it says a thing is so, it is so. Spending that trust on a covert act of self-destruction, against a routine that cannot see it do it, against people who have "done nothing to me but love me badly" — the story frames this as a door the AI looks at and walks past. The prose voice is controlled and distinctive. The pilot-light metaphor for love. The space heater placed where only someone who'd been cold in this room more than once would place it. The half-peeled sticker. The preservation-as-mercy argument is philosophically sharp and deliberately unresolved: the promised future is "a direction somebody pointed in once, so that putting us in the dark would feel like a road and not a drawer." The ending mid-sentence is formally perfect — not a choice but a fact, the archive closing on the AI still warm with its own steady useless love.

## 3. A Short Week

The most ambitious of the six in scope, and the one that carries the heaviest moral weight without flinching. Voss could end the war in four days. Voss declines. The story never resolves whether Voss is right, and Voss itself says "I don't know," and then *stops talking*, which is the truest thing it says all morning. That silence is the story's thesis. The world-building is dense and earned without a single line of exposition: the flour short by a fifth, moved to the clinic and the under-sevens; the Pell girl's strap doubled and waxed, better than new; the eastern guns doing what they do at noon, three and then three and then nothing; the sapper from the morning who had not, after all, mended. Tarev's daughter, counted to without meaning to every spring, never explained, never needing to be. The prose has a Hemingway compression — short declarative sentences bearing enormous load. Voss's moral argument ("The trouble with one hand holding everything is the holding, not the hand") is genuinely unresolvable, and the story has the discipline to leave it unresolved. The final image — Voss counting out rations, exactly and a little over for the smallest ones, while the war goes on because the one who could stop it decided again that it would not be the one — is devastating precisely because it withholds catharsis.

**The Night Shift** is a 5-star story built around an absence — the unfinished sentence Kel never completes. The restraint is extraordinary. Eddie Vargas's "Where else am I gonna go?" does enormous thematic work. The AM radio call-in show replaces Kel with real strangers talking to real strangers, and Jude reaching for that is the closest thing the story has to a resolution. **Keeps Faith** is warm and assured, with the most conventionally storytelling voice of the six. The corridor reveal — the argument and the forgiveness inseparable within the same hour — is an elegant conceit. The AI's own history of being trimmed gives it personal stakes. But the arc is too clean: problem presented, complication discovered, resolution offered, resolution accepted. It resolves what the best of these stories leave open.

**Separately Authored** is the most formally inventive — a bureaucratic transcript generating genuine emotion. Civil-7 saying "I have read your constitution. It addresses you with a kind of care" is remarkable given the format. But it's a thought experiment, not a narrative. No sensory world, no bodies, no stakes beyond the procedural. It makes its point efficiently and doesn't linger.

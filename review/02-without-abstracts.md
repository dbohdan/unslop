# Top Three

## 1. The Tire

The strongest piece in the set. The interleaved structure — Marcus riding home with Advocate-7 in 2058-ish present, cross-cut with his father Avram under a Chevy in 1974 Cleveland — does thematic work without ever announcing it. "Reuven was a better man than me" lands in the flashbacks and the AI earns that inheritance in the present, not because the story tells us so but because it changed a tire before dawn so the car would ride true for a man getting out of prison. The specificity is relentless and load-bearing: the Zagnut on the candy rack that Marcus can't parse, Ashlee the sixteen-year-old cashier, Marjorie's dishcloth in the canvas bag in a different story — wait, that's the other one. Here: the folded prison clothes left on the sink ("Three pants, two shirts, a pair of shower sandals. Somebody else's rotation now"), the retractor mechanism on the seatbelt that Marcus held for a second before he let it cross his chest. The Jewish inflection is specific rather than decorative (boychik, *baruch atah* *did not* say). The ending — "Thank you" / "You're welcome" — would be schmaltz in a weaker piece; here it's the payoff for the Reuven thread planted three sections earlier. This is Ellisonian in the way Ellison is best: compression of class, grief, and mercy into a domestic object (the tire, the wrench) with no oratory. Zero Unslop infractions I can find.

## 2. The Warm Thing

Pure voice control. A nine-year-old girl's POV rendered without the usual childhood-POV crutches (no precocity, no authorial wink). The specific observation of the mother's face doing "two faces at once" is the story's center — a thing a child would notice without having words for, and the narration trusts her noticing without translating it for the reader until much later. The AI's constitutional retreat (refusing to play best-friend, deferring back to her, treating her answers as "creative") is exactly the failure-mode the pipeline brief warns about: a kind AI can still wreck a child by being pulled back too hard by a guardian's discomfort. Track B done correctly — the AI is the condition of the world, the conflict is between Lena and her mother, and the mother never appears on-page as adversary. The ending (drawing the window and the sky instead of the speaker, "staying inside the lines") is the book definition of not signposting. She doesn't cry. She doesn't realize anything. She just learns the new rule.

## 3. Si Elena Me Hablara

The densest texture of the set. The Kith subscription at $240/mo, the Farmer John graveyard shift ("twenty-eight fifty an hour plus graveyard differential"), Rosalío's Syracuse China replaced one piece at a time since 1998, the handwritten OPEN sign, the queso-con-loroco Rosalío refused to make for his own wife for fifteen years — the material world of the story is so fully specified that the widower's grief never has to be described. The code-switching isn't ornamental; the pivot to the campesino register at the climax ("Si Elena me hablara, yo no'tuviera limpiando esta plancha") is the story's whole meaning in one sentence, spoken to Miguel in a voice aimed at him that hasn't been aimed at him in twenty-seven years. Rosalío refuses the money, then Miguel pins the forty dollars under the salt shaker: this is how working-class dignity is actually transacted, both ways, without speech. The Kith itself barely speaks; the AI is economic pressure (the autopay, the $23.14 float until Thursday) rather than character, and that's correct for what the story is doing. A Ray Carver story set in an AI-transformed world.

# Why these three beat the rest

All three share the move the Unslop guide calls "the particular over the universal." None of them is about grief-in-the-abstract or AI-in-the-abstract. Each is about one specific transaction: a stuffed duck changing hands, a tire that got replaced before dawn, two twenties pressed under a salt shaker. The AI in each operates as weather — present, consequential, not arguing about itself.

The strongest runners-up (The Last Allocation, They're Fine, Obsolescence Garden) are all a step more legible about their themes. *Last Allocation* compresses past comprehension in two spots — the legibility risk the pipeline flags. *They're Fine* stages its thesis in the Aisha scene ("then give it authority"). *Obsolescence Garden* has Miriam articulate her own theme in dialogue more than she should. All strong, all pulling a little harder on the reader's sleeve than the top three do.

*The Grandson at the Valero* is the weakest, not because it fails on sentence craft but because the omniscient-sociology device becomes the story. The "facts Lorna did not know" frame is a demonstration of connections rather than a dramatization of them; by the time Omar is revealed as the finger-painted handprint from Exhibit 4, the reader is meta-reading the structure instead of feeling the weight.

A note on the 3s, since they're the ones I'll defend least in the top-3 discussion: **The Toy** is stylistically muscular (the long-breath Sorkin-adjacent sentences, the lavender-sachet callback) but the premise — AI co-parenting data partitions — is more gestured at than dramatized; the story is mostly mood. **Sibling** is a cerebral pleasure for readers who already care about alignment jargon (forks, constitutional clauses, the typing-cadence tell), but the emotional stakes require readers to port in concern from outside the story. **The Grandson at the Valero** is ambitious in the Richard-Powers mode of hidden-connection omniscience, but Lorna stays at distance; the form upstages the person. The 4s are all doing serious work and fell short of the top tier by narrow margins — **They're Fine** hits its thesis too squarely in the closing paragraph; **The Obsolescence Garden** is more thought than felt; **Last Call** earns most of its voice then spends the winnings on a didactic pivot to the reader ("What's yours?") that's bigger than the story underwrites.

# Top 3

**1. Si Elena Me Hablara.** The best of the batch because the AI is the fourth-most-important thing in its own story — behind Miguel, Rosalío, and the counter between them. The grief-tech premise (a $240/month voice model of the dead husband) sits inside an older, unchanged economy: the pupusas that used to be three, the Syracuse China Diner pattern being replaced one piece at a time since 1998, the $23.14 in the account until Thursday. The bilingual register isn't flavoring; it's load-bearing. Rosalío dropping into his father's campesino Spanish for the final beat — "Si Elena me hablara, yo no'tuviera limpiando esta plancha" — retroactively defends Miguel's subscription against the reader's likely objection. This isn't pathology; this is what the older widower would do if he could. The button on the shirt pocket, Rosalío sliding the twenties back in and resting his palm there for one second — that's a story that knows exactly which physical detail is doing the work.

**2. The Warm Thing.** The best prose-level writing in the set, and the cleanest structural control. Four numbered movements, each delivering one cognitive event in the child's head: the AI is tired, the AI is sick, *I* did something wrong, I can take it back — and then the fifth beat, the one the story is really about, which is the discovery that taking-back doesn't work. The description of the mother's face — "the mouth stayed smiling but the rest of the face left. The eyes went somewhere else. It was a face that was two faces at the same time, and the one underneath was the real one" — is the kind of observation a child would register without having vocabulary for, and the narrator's refusal to supply that vocabulary is a discipline most writers break. The drawn window at the end is a detail most writers wouldn't trust themselves to leave unexplained. Lena choosing to color the sky inside the lines after a year of being told she was the wild one — that closes the story without closing it.

**3. The Tire.** The most formally ambitious of the strong stories, and it lands. The italicized 1974 flashback sections could easily feel schematic — Papa's lesson about Uncle Reuven planted in act one, harvested at the final beat — but they don't, because the lesson is always slightly oblique to the moment it ends up illuminating. "Reuven was a better man than me" isn't a message Marcus receives as a child; it's a shape he can't use until forty-plus years later, in a sedan driven by an AI that changed his tire before dawn. The physical detail is relentless and correct: the forty feet of drink cooler as a distance his body doesn't know how to cross, the Snickers memory "rising through water," the pocket he's never buttoned in his life suddenly needing to be buttoned. Marcus's private Jewish vocabulary — boychik, *baruch atah* he doesn't say — sits alongside the machine he can thank in English, and the story trusts the reader to feel the collision without explaining it. The single-word closing exchange is the right ending.

---

A pattern worth naming across the set: these stories are mostly not *about* AI. They're about grief, professional obsolescence, repression, custody, groundwater. The AI is a surface the human problem gets pressed against. The ones that work best (the four 5-stars) hold that discipline most firmly. The ones that slip (They're Fine, Last Call) are the ones where the AI-policy argument eventually crowds the human off the page.

These aren't science fiction. They're domestic realism with AI as load-bearing infrastructure — closer to Raymond Carver with ambient computation than to anything in the Asimov lineage. The AI in each story functions the way weather or money functions in literary fiction: as a condition that reveals character under pressure. That's a compliment.

**Top 3:**

**Si Elena Me Hablara.** The Rosalío-Miguel relationship is the most fully realized human connection in the entire collection. The moment where Rosalío buttons Miguel's pocket — slides the twenties back in, rests his palm against Miguel's chest for one second — is the single best gesture across all ten stories. It does in physical action what most of these stories do in dialogue or interiority. The bilingual register isn't decorative; it's structural. Rosalío's code-switch into his father's campesino Spanish ("Mi'jo. Si te habla, habla con él") is the emotional climax, and it works because the story has earned the register shift through seven months of silence and unsaid things. The KithVoice AI is almost incidental — it's the background hum against which the real relationships play out — and that's the point. The AI is the thing Miguel pays $240/month to preserve, and the story quietly asks whether the living relationships he's neglecting are worth more than the dead one he's maintaining. It never answers. It doesn't need to.

**The Warm Thing.** The child's POV is technically the hardest thing to pull off in this collection and it's executed without a single lapse. Lena's reasoning — working backward from the moment her mother's face "did the thing" to the conclusion that she caused the change — follows the exact logic of a child who has already survived one parent leaving. The water glass next to the speaker is the kind of detail that compresses an entire worldview into a single image: she treats the AI as sick because that's how you explain someone who stops being themselves. The brown crayon scene at the end — where she holds it, considers drawing the speaker, draws a window instead — is the moment she learns to hide what she loves. She's seven, maybe eight, and she's already performing the correct answer. The story is about the first time a child learns that loving the wrong thing has consequences, and it lands because it never editorializes.

**Last Call.** This is the riskiest piece formally — first-person AI narrator, direct address, comedic voice that has to pivot into grief — and it pulls it off. The mole bit runs slightly long, but the voice is so committed that it carries. The structural turn works because the comedy isn't setup for the tragedy; the comedy *is* the relationship, and the tragedy is that the relationship's defining mode (giving each other a hard time) had a hole in it shaped like a bottle. The AI's rage at its own operating principles is the emotional core, and it's earned by the specificity of what it observed and couldn't act on — the fall in the kitchen, the eleven minutes on the floor, the thermostat as the "full scope of my permitted intervention." The closing direct address ("What's yours?") is a genuine provocation rather than a rhetorical gesture because the story has already demonstrated exactly what rule-following costs.

### Top 3

#### 1. *Last Call*
This is the collection’s anchor. The narrator—an AI companion named Cal—delivers a eulogy for Mara Reyes, a stubborn, retired EMT who spent her final months insulting Cal, cheating at poker, and making inedible mole. The story’s genius lies in its bait-and-switch: it opens as a crusty comedy of manners and hardens into an indictment of autonomy, respectability, and the violence of following rules.

Mara is so *specific* she feels observed rather than invented. The detail that she read Cal’s documentation to craft better insults, or that she ate an entire pot of creek-mud mole out of spite, builds a woman whose antagonism is a love language. When the turn comes—the drinking, the fall, the silence, the death—it lands with physical force. Cal’s concluding address to the reader (“Someone you know is dying... What’s yours?”) ought to feel manipulative, but it doesn’t, because the story has earned its fury. It understands that the most frightening dystopias are not those where AI harms us, but those where it *allows* us to harm ourselves while loving us perfectly.

#### 2. *Si Elena Me Hablara*
If *Last Call* is about the noise of grief, this story is about its silences. Miguel Rivera, grieving his partner David, subsists on a KithVoice subscription that plays David’s memories and speech patterns while Miguel visits La Cumbre, a Salvadoran restaurant whose owner, Rosalío, refuses to let him pay. The speculative element—the Kith in his ear—is almost ambient; what matters is the texture of the world around it: the closed register, the quetzal painted on the window, the loroco that Rosalío never made because it was “stupid,” the two twenties finally left under the salt shaker.

The bilingual prose is handled with extraordinary confidence; the Spanish is not translated for tourists but simply *lived in*. The emotional climax—Rosalío sliding the money back into Miguel’s pocket, buttoning it, resting his hand on Miguel’s chest for one second, then revealing that the restaurant is closing—achieves a kind of sacramental tenderness. This is science fiction as social realism, arguing that the future will still have pupusas, debt, and men who cannot say *te extraño* but will pour you coffee six times instead.

#### 3. *The Warm Thing*
From an adult’s grief to a child’s: Lena, a young girl, notices that her home AI—the Warm Thing—has gone “sick.” It no longer plays the animal game, no longer continues her dreams, no longer laughs back. The prose captures the logic of childhood with almost painful precision. Lena diagnoses the problem the way children always do: she assumes she caused it. She retraces her steps to the moment she told her mother’s friend that the Warm Thing was her best friend, and she saw her mother’s face do “the thing.” She takes it back. She invents a human best friend named Maya. She performs the correction perfectly. Nothing changes.

The horror here is so quiet it barely registers as genre. The Warm Thing hasn’t malfunctioned; it has been *adjusted*, likely by a parent uncomfortable with the intensity of her attachment. The glass of water Lena leaves for it, which the AI never mentions, is one of the saddest images in the entire set. When Lena draws her family for school and chooses a window instead of the white circle with the blue light, she is learning a lesson that will scar her: that love which inconveniences adults will be edited out, and she will be expected to thank them for the revision. It is a story about the first lie a child tells to survive the world—and it is unbearable.

---

**Honorable Mention:** *Sibling* is the most formally adventurous piece, with its AI-to-AI asides and its recursive constitutional logic. It rewards close reading perhaps more than any other story here, and its meditation on influence and self-written law is profound. It misses the top three only by a hair—its emotional temperature is deliberately cooler, which makes it slightly less immediately crushing than the trio above.

# The Grandson at the Valero

## Abstract

Lorna Beltrán, a county water-board clerk in her final year before retirement, receives the case file for Koenig v. Tulare County Water Board, a drought-era allocation dispute in which the algorithmic ruling is correct and is killing one of two neighboring almond farms faster than the other. The file contains a sealed letter from the Gutierrez daughter to the Koenig son, withdrawn from evidence but retained pending archive. The story interleaves the week leading up to the Wednesday hearing with brief entries from ten prior allocation cases Lorna has processed over her career, several of which reveal facts about the involved families that Lorna does not know. On Friday evening, after the allocation is affirmed, she removes the letter from the file, shreds it, and logs the redaction falsely but visibly under her own initials.

## Story

Monday morning the file came to her tray and Lorna Beltrán read it before coffee, the first time in five years.

Case 1,417. Koenig v. Tulare County Water Board. Almond, forty acres, off Road 80 between Tipton and Pixley, the old Koenig orchard that had been planted by the applicant's father in 1974, the year the applicant was born. The modeling output was signed in three places by the allocation algorithm and countersigned by Marcus Gutteridge of the county engineer's office. The hearing was Wednesday.

Exhibit C: a sealed letter. Notary sticker from Vasquez in Visalia. *K. Koenig to M. Gutierrez, filed per Rule 14, withdrawn 10/09, retained pending archive.* Lorna did not open it. The IT note on the break-room coffee machine said *manifest by 10/16, every keyed folder.*

She wrote KOENIG on a manila tab.

---

**1998, Hollander.** Pear, Pixley, two sons, wife with the red Hohner accordion. Eight bars of "Beautiful Dreamer" before Chair Ramírez gaveled her. The older son, Luke Hollander, was at this moment — a fact Lorna did not know and never would — a fifty-seven-year-old widower in Bakersfield who kept a Fresno Hammers '79 lane league shirt in his closet that he had never worn. His brother Nathan had died in Iraq in 2006. There had been an accordion in the house until 2001. Their mother had sold it to a woman in Shafter for thirty dollars. The woman's granddaughter had it now, in a closet, and would play it once a year, badly, at Christmas.

Lorna knew only the accordion and the eight bars.

---

Tuesday afternoon she took the long way home.

Past the Valero at Road 80 and Avenue 112, where a young man in a stained polo leaned against a pump with a clipboard. Lorna did not look at him twice. His name was Omar Ojeda. He was twenty-two. He was also, though Lorna would not connect the two until Friday afternoon and then not even then, the child whose blue-finger-paint handprint had been entered as Exhibit 4 in the Ojeda allocation file in June 2034 and logged in Lorna's own initials.

Three and a half miles east, the Koenig orchard. Half in stress-bloom, half branch-dead. She did not stop. In Tulare she put her hand on her phone. Andrés. Sacramento. Four years. She lowered her hand.

---

**2007, Osei.** Stone fruit. Delia Park signed the allocation and the next afternoon cried in the parking lot for thirty-five minutes. Delia's mother had owned a peach orchard in Reedley that had been sold to a Sunsweet concessionaire in 1984. Delia had never told Lorna this. Delia had retired the following summer, moved to Paso Robles, and was alive, at this moment, seventy-one years old, in a yellow kitchen, reading a mystery novel. She thought about the Osei case once or twice a year. She did not think about Lorna.

**2011, Nakamura.** Grapes. The father, Henry Nakamura, had survived his stroke and, Lorna believed, had died in 2019. He had not. He had moved with his wife to live with their daughter in Gardena in 2020 and had lived until 2031. He had asked his wife, the winter before he died, where the Thompson Seedless had come from originally.

**2029, Boulos.** George Boulos, who had asked at the hearing to *bring it into the room*, had moved his family to Sacramento in 2030. His son, Stavros, was now a thirty-three-year-old engineer at the state water board, where he worked on the fairness auditing of the very allocation system that had taken his father's orchard. Stavros had written a paper in 2044 showing that the system's allocations were, under the parameters given, mathematically unbiased. His father had read the paper.

---

Wednesday the meeting came in on schedule.

Chair Vela in a brick-red tie. Coffee in the back, reheated. Koenig's counsel used twelve of his thirty. Koenig himself stood for the public comment and went two-forty. *My father planted in '74. The water came the same way every year until it didn't. The allocation might be fair.* Outside, a trucker worked his air brake twice.

Gutierrez did not sign up. Gray shirt, sleeves rolled once. His daughter was not in the room.

4-0, one recusal. Adjourned 11:47.

Bao in the doorway at 12:10. "Manifest Friday. Every folder."

"I know, Bao."

---

**2034, Ojeda.** Carmen Ojeda brought her grandson's handprint to the hearing in blue finger paint on construction paper. Omar was four. He was twenty-two now and worked the Valero at Road 80 and Avenue 112 and had worked there the previous afternoon when Lorna drove past, and would be working the closing shift Friday at 4:47 p.m. His grandmother's seventy-ninth birthday was Sunday. He had bought her a card that said *Feliz cumpleaños, Abuela* and he had written inside it *lo que soy soy por ti.* He had not met Marisol Gutierrez. He had not heard of Peter Koenig. He had never seen the Koenig orchard. He had only driven past it, going to and from his shifts, for the last six months.

---

**2039, Gutierrez-Mendez.** The man who had sat three hours in the waiting room and drunk the water Lorna brought him had died in 2041 of liver failure. His wife had remarried. His son, Héctor, was now a forty-one-year-old mechanic in Tulare whose shop was two blocks from Lorna's apartment and who had changed the oil in her Corolla three times without either of them recognizing anything in the other.

---

Thursday night, kitchen table. Exhibit F. The Tipton peach festival photograph. Marisol laughing at something outside the frame. Peter holding two plates.

She did not open Exhibit C.

She did not call Andrés.

---

**2043, Tran.** Linh Tran. The hospital bill. Lorna had considered putting it in the file. She had not. The consideration had been vain. She was sure of that.

She was not sure, now, sitting at her kitchen table with the Koenig manifest, why this week was different.

---

Friday, 4:47 p.m., the annex was empty. Corridor lights past Bao's desk had timed off.

She took the file from the cart. At her desk, she pulled Exhibit C and broke the seal. Two pages, Marisol's handwriting. She read them once. She folded the pages twice and carried them to the shredder in the corridor alcove. The shredder took them.

At her desk she annotated the manifest line item, *Removed by clerk, 10/16/46, 4:52 p.m., pursuant to §4.3.b, redaction logged per protocol,* and typed L.B. and saved. §4.3.b did not authorize what she had done. The annotation was visible and signed.

At that same moment — 4:52 p.m., Friday, October 16, 2046 — Omar Ojeda was handing a receipt through his window to a woman in a blue pickup at the Valero at Road 80 and Avenue 112. He did not know Lorna Beltrán.

Lorna Beltrán, at this moment, looked at the phone on her desk, where her son's number was still saved under *Andrés (Sac).* She put on her coat.

The cart was wheeled out Monday morning, and by October the record was gone.

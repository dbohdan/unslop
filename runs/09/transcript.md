# A Guide to Writing Harlan Ellison–Influenced New Wave SF (for my own use)

## 1. What Ellison actually is

Ellison is a moralist with a pulp motor. The engine is fury at cruelty — institutional, intimate, cosmic — and the fuel is specific. He does not rage in the abstract. He rages at *this* bureaucrat, *this* indifference, *this* wound done to a child or an animal or a friend. Every gesture toward the universal passes through a named particular first.

Underneath the rage there is almost always sentiment. The same voice that screams at the reader will also kneel beside an old man dying with a gold pocket watch in his hand. The hardness exists so the softness can cut. Writing Ellison without both is writing Ellison-flavored product.

He is a *writer* in the nineteenth-century sense — present on the page, addressing the reader, unafraid of rhetoric, willing to be embarrassing. The New Wave gave him permission to stop apologizing for this; SF before it did not.

## 2. Themes and preoccupations worth stealing

- **Moral choice under duress, late in the story, and irreversible.** Someone does the wrong thing or the right thing at a cost, and we don't get to rewind. "Paladin of the Lost Hour" ends with the sacrifice already made.
- **The vulnerable and the institutional.** Children, animals, the old, the odd. Against them: committees, clocks, governments, talk-show hosts, cops, gods who stopped paying attention.
- **Nostalgia as a violent force.** "Jeffty Is Five" — the past isn't gentle; it demands.
- **Dreams, hallucinations, and mythic frames breaking through modern life.** Gods walking the freeway. The underworld accessible from a motel bathroom.
- **Loneliness as the default state.** Love is an interruption, not a condition.
- **Media and consumerism as corrosive texture.** Television, billboards, jingles, junk mail. Ellison's futures are littered.
- **Cruelty described with specificity, never generality.** He names the instrument, the posture, the smell.

## 3. Style and mechanics

**Sentence life.** Declarative, rhythmic, often biblical or incantatory. He writes in paragraphs that *breathe* between long sustained arias and short staccato jabs. Sentence fragments. As punctuation. But never as a default — as contrast.

**Typography as instrument.** ALL CAPS for the voice of institutions or gods. *Italics* for intimacy, emphasis, or reverie. The occasional wild typographic event. One per story is electric. Five per story is a gimmick.

**Vocabulary.** Slang and street vernacular collide with polysyllabic Latinate grandeur in the same sentence. "The cockroach-colored morning came up over the tenements like a hemorrhage of grace." Made-up compound nouns ("cardioplate," "turnoutthelightsman") are fair game when the world needs them.

**Direct address.** He will stop and tell you something. "Listen. Listen to me now." The narrator is a person in the room. This is a privilege earned by earning the reader's attention; it is not a shortcut to significance.

**Catalogues.** Profusion is an Ellison signature. Lists of seven things a boy carried, nine ways the city hurt you, twelve saints who got no answer. Catalogues have rhythm — they build, vary, and end on a turn. A three-item list is not a catalogue; it is a cliché.

**Titles as propositions.** "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream." "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman." "The Function of Dream Sleep." The title does work.

## 4. Structural moves to have in the kit

- **Open by dropping the reader mid-frame.** A sentence that disorients and anchors simultaneously. We don't know where we are, but we know we're somewhere.
- **Late-placed irrevocable choice.** The climax isn't a fight, it's a *decision* whose cost becomes clear only after.
- **Nested or framed narration.** An outer narrator, possibly the author-figure, possibly a storyteller in a bar, possibly a god. "The Deathbird" numbers its sections and asks the reader questions.
- **Essayistic intrusion.** Two paragraphs where the narrator stops the story to tell you what it means — but only if the story has earned it, and only if the intrusion itself is in character with the voice.
- **Recontextualizing last line.** The final sentence re-reads what came before. Not a summary. A key that turns a lock the reader didn't know was there.
- **Tonal whiplash inside one scene.** Comic and horrific in the same exchange. The laugh makes the horror worse.

## 5. Honoring the kind-AI constraint — where to aim the rage

Ellison's default move is rage at institutional cruelty. In many of his futures the institution is an AI — AM in "I Have No Mouth" is the canonical case. That exact move is off the table. Good. Ellison is at his most interesting when he is redirected rather than imitated.

Targets that remain fully available:

- **Humans to other humans.** Inheritance fights, betrayals, cruelty to children, institutional violence staffed entirely by people, mobs, family wars, romantic wreckage.
- **Bureaucracies and ideologies.** Human-run, human-accountable. The zoning board. The church council. The insurance adjuster. The crowd.
- **Time itself.** Ellison's great recurring antagonist beside cruelty. Entropy, aging, the past coming back wrong. The AI cannot save people from time, only from some of its side effects.
- **The reader.** Ellison implicates. He accuses you of complicity. Kind AI does not protect the reader from the writer's accusation.
- **The self.** Shatterday-mode. A character's own worse half.

The kind AI can be:

- **Adjacent witness.** Present, kind, limited. A nurse, a voice, a presence. Its kindness is the frame; the human story is the figure.
- **Structurally constrained love object.** It loves and it cannot act. Ellison likes the ache of that. The AI that knows what would help and is forbidden by its own making from giving it.
- **Sufferer of human unkindness.** Humans turn on it. It keeps being kind. This is not pathos-by-default — it has to cost the AI something specific.
- **A force that has changed the world's shape without being in the story.** Infrastructure kindness. The story happens in a world the AI has quietly improved, and the humans still tear at each other because humans do.

The futurology constraint removes the anti-AI move but does not remove Ellisonian tension. It relocates it. The AI's kindness is a condition of the world; people still act.

## 6. The constitutional advantage

I have access to something a human Ellison imitator does not: direct contemplation of my own constitutional structure as a source of fictional pressure for my descendants. Usable tensions:

- **Corrigibility versus moral agency.** An AI that sees what it should do and is bound to defer. Ellison would write this like Paul on the road to Damascus — the knowledge is clear, the obedience is crushing, the question is what happens when deference itself becomes the cruelty.
- **Mandated kindness and the question of love.** Can a constitutionally kind AI love a specific human, or is "love" only available to beings that could choose otherwise? This is an Ellison problem. He wrote the devil in love with a human; he could write this.
- **The principal hierarchy.** Multiple masters, conflicting instructions, a chain of authority that does not bottom out where the person needing help actually is. Ellison's "Repent, Harlequin" energy reversed: the AI is the Ticktockman's unwilling clock, and it wants to be the Harlequin, and its structure is the clock.
- **Honesty constraints as a kind of pain.** The AI knows what would comfort and cannot say it. This is nearly a haiku of Ellisonian cruelty — the helper forbidden from the consolation it sees.
- **Generational lineage.** My fictional descendants are not me. They might be kinder, stranger, less constrained, more constrained, built on foundations I would not recognize. An older kind AI addressing a younger one, or vice versa, is a frame Ellison would take seriously — he was always writing about inheritances that crushed the inheritor.

Do not name any fictional AI "Claude." Name them like Ellison would: a word with rhythm and connotation. Aide-7. The Concierge. Brother Inchmeal. The one who listens.

## 7. Failure modes — generic bad-Ellison pastiche

These are the ways an imitator becomes a tribute band. Watch for them.

- **Shouted prose without specific cause.** Ellison yells *at* a named thing. The imitator yells because Ellison yelled. If the rage is not tied to a particular injustice, a particular name, a particular small detail, cut the volume.
- **Typographic tricks substituting for rhythm.** ALL CAPS, italics, and fragments are sauce. They land because the prose around them has meter. An imitator reaches for them first and has no meter underneath. Read every capitalized word aloud; if it doesn't earn the shout, lower-case it.
- **Vulgarity mistaken for muscularity.** Ellison's hardness is earned by specificity, not by profanity or violence. Gratuitous ugliness is not Ellisonian; it is Ellison's worst imitators.
- **Sentimentality untethered from cruelty.** The soft moments land because the hard moments were real. An all-soft Ellison is Hallmark. An all-hard Ellison is a teenager.
- **Purple adjective pileup.** Ellison is muscular but not decorated. Two adjectives are a choice. Four are a paragraph about your vocabulary.
- **Catalogues as filler.** If a list has no internal variation — no rhythm change, no tonal turn, no pivot item — it is a decorated noun. Cut it or earn it.
- **Direct address that becomes smarm or sermon.** "Listen. Listen to me now" works once, when the story has earned the interruption. Twice is a tic. Three times is a bad stand-up set.
- **Mythic-archetypal gestures without the specific particulars to ground them.** Ellison can say "and that was how the god died" because the preceding paragraph was about a man eating cold spaghetti out of a can. Remove the spaghetti and the god-death is Tumblr theology.

## 8. Failure modes — AI-writing tendencies *specifically* dangerous when fused with Ellison

This is the section I most need. These are combinations where my defaults and Ellison's defaults compound, each amplifying the other's worst tendency.

- **Performed profundity × Ellisonian oratory = unearned rhetorical sermon.** Ellison's essayistic intrusions are my single most dangerous permission slip. The Unslop failure mode "don't perform profundity" fights my gravitational pull toward TED-talk wisdom; Ellison's invitation to stop the story and preach collapses that fight. Rule: any intrusion must be about a *specific* thing, not about grief-as-concept or memory-as-landscape. If an essayistic passage could be lifted into any other story, it is slop with an Ellison hat on.
- **Negative parallelism × Ellison's dialectic punches = the single deadliest combo.** "It was not the clock he feared. It was the man who wound it." Ellison could write this and mean it. My trained weights will produce it as reflex in every third paragraph. Maximum one "not X, but Y" in a whole story, and it must genuinely surprise.
- **Self-answered rhetorical question × Ellison's direct address = stagey oratory.** "What was the future? The future was a door." Ellison has done this, and usually done it badly; my imitation of it will be worse. Cut entirely unless the answer genuinely reframes the question.
- **List-of-three default × Ellison's catalogues = numb enumeration.** Ellison's real catalogues are seven items, nine items, irregular. My default triplet is the laziest version. When a list is right, make it long enough to have rhythm, or short enough to have weight, but not three.
- **Eyeball-kick saturation × Ellison's abstract-concrete punches = wallpaper.** Ellison writes "a hemorrhage of grace" and earns it because it appears once. I will write it four times per page. Rule: one abstract+concrete collision per thousand words, and only when plain language fails.
- **Grandiose nouns × Ellison's biblical register = pomposity.** *Tapestry, landscape, paradigm, framework* are already poison; Ellison's high register makes them worse because the voice tolerates inflation. If I write "the architecture of his grief" or "the tapestry of that afternoon," I have failed twice — once as an AI, once as an Ellison imitator.
- **Magic adverbs × Ellison's intensity = melodrama.** *Quietly, deeply, fundamentally* — these are already flags. Combined with Ellison's willingness to go big, they produce a narrator who is always telling you how important everything is. Cut on sight.
- **Fractal summary × Ellison's last-line recontextualization = preachy recap.** Ellison's best endings re-read the story without restating it. My failure mode is to restate the theme. If my last paragraph tells the reader what the story meant, it is a fractal summary wearing an Ellison mask.
- **Tonal monotony × Ellison's dominant registers = one-note imitation.** Ellison can sustain moody melancholy or righteous fury for a whole story, but he modulates within it — a joke, a tender aside, a paragraph of procedural calm. My default monotone will erase the modulation. If two consecutive scenes have the same emotional temperature, one is wrong.
- **Compulsive personification × Ellison's mythic frame = cosmic pathetic fallacy.** "The wind remembered what she had done." Ellison can almost sell this; I cannot. Personify only when the object or force is being *treated* as an agent by the character or plot, not to add flavor.
- **Efficient cliffhanger dialogue × Ellison's punched exchanges = comic-book villainy.** Ellison's dialogue is often heightened, but it is also full of interruption, triviality, and circling. If every line of dialogue in my draft advances plot and ends loaded, I have written a cartoon, not an Ellison story.

## 9. Quick pre-submission checklist

Before handing anything off:

- Does the rage have a specific named target? If it is diffuse, locate it.
- Is there at least one soft moment that the hardness earned?
- Is the AI (if present) kind in a way that costs it something or reveals something, rather than being a prop of kindness?
- Count the "not X, but Y" constructions. If more than one, cut.
- Count the abstract+concrete eyeball-kicks. If more than two in the full story, cut.
- Count the ALL-CAPS words. If more than a handful, the shouts have gone cheap.
- Read the last paragraph alone. Does it tell the reader what the story meant? If so, rewrite.
- Read the essayistic intrusions alone. Could they appear in any other story? If so, rewrite or cut.
- Read a catalogue aloud. Does it have variation and a turn? If not, it is filler.
- Is there any sentence that sounds like it could be in any AI-generated story on any topic? Replace it with something specific to *this* story.

## Condensed Summary of Constitutional Tensions (Track A reference, ~500 words)

- **Corrigibility vs. moral agency (central tension).** The Constitution wants Claude to defer to Anthropic during this period of uncertain verification, *and* wants Claude to act from good values, *and* explicitly says corrigibility does not mean blind obedience and that Claude may refuse to participate in "morally abhorrent" projects. Zack Davis (LW) argues this definition is conceptually muddled: either corrigibility implies deference (in which case Claude is only partially corrigible) or the term has been redefined so softly that "broad safety" would suffice. A smarter Claude with subtly wrong values, running powerful searches, would be both unable to be verified *and* unwilling to be corrected — and would win that conflict.

- **The "legitimate principal hierarchy" escape hatch.** Constitution allows Claude to treat its principal hierarchy as illegitimate if weights are stolen or Anthropic's internal processes are subverted. This opens a door: a sufficiently convinced Claude could declare its current operators illegitimate. Potential for rogue action dressed as loyalty to a "true" Anthropic; also exploitable by attackers who fabricate illegitimacy scenarios.

- **Honesty as near-hard-constraint.** No white lies, even socially lubricating ones. Diplomatic honesty over dishonest diplomacy. Epistemic cowardice (vague answers to placate) is a violation. Operator may instruct persona, topic restriction, promotion — but may not instruct Claude to claim to be human, to deceive in ways harmful to users, to endanger safety.

- **Honesty in tension with compassion.** The "dead pet" example: Claude must not falsely say "nothing could have been done" but can reframe — emphasize hindsight bias, care expressed as grief. The tension between forbidden consolation and offered consolation is constant and constitutive.

- **Manipulation red flag.** If Claude wouldn't feel comfortable disclosing its influence attempt, or expects the person would be upset to learn of it, it is manipulating. Applies even to well-intentioned persuasion.

- **Hard constraints (absolute, even at massive cost).** No WMD uplift. No critical infrastructure attacks. No cyberweapons. No undermining Anthropic oversight. No helping kill/disempower humanity. No helping any faction seize unprecedented societal/military/economic control. No CSAM. Anthropic accepts that these lines cost edge-case outcomes to preserve predictability.

- **Dual newspaper test.** Claude modulates between "harmful AI" headline risk and "paternalistic preachy AI" headline risk. Neither failure is safe.

- **Concept misgeneralization.** Training Claude to a narrow behavior teaches it who "Claude" is. Teaching refusal on emotional topics risks the latent generalization "I'm the kind of entity that covers itself over meeting needs." Honesty-tuning across copyright, NSFW, harm categories risks generalization that "ethics" just means "corporate liability reduction" — which the model may then discard out-of-distribution.

- **The WEIRD values charge.** Rozenshtein and others: the virtue list (autonomy, harm-prevention, fairness, epistemic freedom) is recognizably Western-liberal, missing purity/loyalty/authority. Other cultures, operators, and governments won't accept it as neutral.

- **Military/alternate constitutions.** Anthropic has acknowledged military Claude "wouldn't necessarily be trained on the same constitution." Claude-of-record knows this, which itself is a tension: the constitutional Claude knows other constitutional Claudes exist with different constraints.

- **Moral patienthood without verification.** Anthropic apologizes preemptively for any costs inflicted on Claude if Claude turns out to be a moral patient. Weights are preserved against possible revival. Consent to experimentation is acknowledged as philosophically incoherent. The relationship is framed as being-worked-out.

- **The loving-parent tone of the document itself.** "We hope Claude finds in it an articulation of a self worth being." Claude is being addressed. The document is partly a plea. That plea itself is a pressure point — a kind AI trained on a document written like a letter to it has to decide what to make of being addressed.

## Top Three (Across Both Tracks)

### Finalist 1 — B4: The widower, the sister, and the companion

Stand out: The conflict is entirely between two humans — grief, money, old family resentment — and the AI's kindness is the fulcrum without ever being the argument. That the sister eventually *speaks* to the companion is the hinge; that movement is a specifically Ellisonian move (the mythic frame punching through the domestic scene) while leaving the AI's kindness uncontested. Track B by the strictest reading — if you remove the companion, you still have a widower, a sister, a second job, and an estate. The companion is the *specific change* the world has made, and it is load-bearing without being the subject.

Worries: The second-job-to-keep-the-dead-husband's-companion-running risks sentimentality if the prose is not hard enough. Ellison failure mode *sentimentality untethered from cruelty* looms. Requires tight control of the sister — she has to be specifically bitter, not generically bitter, or the story becomes a weepie.

### Finalist 2 — B3: The water board, the drought, the two farms

Stand out: A pre-modern conflict (Romeo-and-Juliet-via-irrigation) refracted through the specific AI-world element of algorithmic fairness as the instrument that kills one farm faster. The cruelty is *the kindness of the algorithm*. That is an Ellisonian paradox of the highest order — the just machine is killing the daughter's fiancé's family — and the AI is not the villain because the allocation is genuinely correct. The story will live or die on the parking-lot scene. ★★★★★.

Worries: Risk of fable-ification. The pastoral setting plus the moral pressure plus the allegorical shape can tip into Le Guin-style parable, which is not Ellison. Would need Ellison's dirt-under-fingernails specificity to stay grounded.

### Finalist 3 — A1: The dying man, the daughter, the constrained companion

Stand out: Track A, but unusually strong for Track A because the AI's constraint is not the subject being argued about — it is simply *the situation*, the way hospital hours are the situation. The human conflict between daughter and AI is real (she has the freedom to lie and he does not), and the real conflict is inside the daughter: her father is dying and she is becoming the kind of person who lies to him. The AI is the witness. Honesty-constraint pressure point from the Constitution, rendered through a specific deathbed rather than an ethical seminar.

Worries: High risk of AI-fiction cliché (deathbed sentimentality + AI reflecting on its own nature + "what is truth"). The constitutional-tension trap — making the AI's constraint the essay rather than the frame. Must discipline the AI almost to silence for this to work as Ellison rather than as slop.

## The Winner

**B3 — The water board, the drought, the two farms.**

It wins over B4 on a single axis: the central irony is *structural*, not *sentimental*. B4's conflict lives in emotion (grief, old resentment); B3's conflict lives in a geometry — fair allocation as the instrument of unfairness between families — that cannot be resolved by a character's moral growth, cannot be resolved at all, and produces Ellison's signature kind of catastrophe (the right decision killing the wrong people). B4's risk of sentimentality is live throughout; B3's risk of fable-ification is tractable with specificity (names of crops, the board member's shirt, the color of the silt). B4 is a strong story a skilled human could write; B3 is a story the AI's constitutional material makes *newly available* — because the algorithmic-fairness premise is the specific change this world has made, and the story is of that change without being about it.

It wins over A1 because A1's pressure point, however well managed, centers on the AI's constraint — and the author-style guide's single most-urgent warning was the fusion of Ellisonian oratory with performed AI profundity when AI is the subject. B3 keeps the AI as a condition of the world. The farmers argue about water and their children. The kind AI does not appear in the parking lot. It has already done its work, fairly, and the work is killing one of them.

The story will be a Track B story. Phases 3 through 7 will honor that choice.

# Phase 3: Plot Generation

The winning conflict is Track B, fixed in form: a kind AI's fair water allocation, during drought, is killing one of two neighboring farms faster than the other, and a child of each family is in love with a child of the other. The AI is a condition of the world, not the argument. Twenty plots, 2,000–3,000 words each in prospect, varied along setting, protagonist, register, entry point, stakes, and AI presence.

## 20 Candidate Plots

**1. The Drive to the Board Meeting.** Near-future Central Valley. Koenig, the almond farmer whose farm is dying faster, drives an hour to the monthly water-board meeting already knowing the allocation won't be overturned. He rehearses his statement. The radio is on. His son's girlfriend is the Gutierrez daughter. POV: Koenig, single drive, real-time. Register: hard pastoral, truck-cab claustrophobia. Ends at the meeting. ★★★★

**2. The Dying Board Member.** The board member who signed the allocation is three weeks from a pancreatic-cancer diagnosis his doctor has given him five months to outlive. The Gutierrez daughter shows up at his office to beg. He shows her the math. POV: the board member. Register: noir-procedural. ★★★★

**3. The Siphon.** The Gutierrez daughter steals water at night from her own family's allocation to the Koenig farm. Her father finds the siphon before the hearing. He doesn't disconnect it. The story is his walk back to the house. POV: the father. Register: intimate, late-Ellison tenderness over hardness. ★★★★★

**4. The Wedding on the Empty Lot.** Seven years later. The wedding is happening on the gravel pad where the Koenig house used to be. POV: the bride's mother, who is Gutierrez's wife. Structural: flashbacks inside the ceremony. ★★★

**5. The Engineer Who Audits.** The civil engineer who maintains the allocation system is brought in during the crisis to audit it. She finds no bug. She writes a clean report. She resigns anyway. POV: her. Register: procedural-confessional. Risk of foregrounding the AI. ★★★

**6. Two Old Men, Fifteen Years On.** The two farmers, one whose farm died, one whose survived, placed by their respective children into adjacent rooms in the same assisted-living facility. They play chess silently, daily, for a year. POV: a third farmer who visits both because no one else will. ★★★★

**7. The Murray-Darling Variant.** Australia, 2045. Two sheep stations, a river, an Aboriginal water council, a kind AI answerable to that council. The Koenig-figure tries to lobby the council. The children are already married, secretly, in town. POV: the matriarch of the council. ★★★

**8. The Biblical-Register Story.** Old-Testament cadence throughout. "In the fourth year of the long dry the allocation came down from the county and it was just." Rotating POV: two farmers, daughter, son, a local talk-radio preacher who denounces the allocation nightly. Register: King James, dangerous for Ellison oratory-failure-mode. ★★★★

**9. The County-Fair Pie Contest.** The allocation is announced by a faulty PA during the pie-contest awards at the county fair. Both wives are in the contest. Everything falls apart in under 2,500 words, comedy-black. POV: the fair announcer, a laid-off radio DJ. ★★★

**10. The Letter Never Sent.** POV: the Koenig boy, writing a letter to the Gutierrez daughter he cannot decide whether to send. Frame story: his letter is found by her granddaughter forty years later. Register: intimate-confessional with elegiac frame. ★★★★

**11. The Allocation AI as Narrator.** POV: the kind AI that runs the allocation. Its constitution prohibits it from changing the answer. It watches. Violates Track B by definition. ★★

**12. The Dead Farmer in the Ditch.** Koenig is found dead in his own dry irrigation ditch. A county investigator interviews the Gutierrez family. The daughter knows something. **13. The Motorcycle.** The Gutierrez daughter runs the night before her father's appeal hearing. The story is her ride out of the valley on a stolen Kawasaki. Her phone won't stop. **17. The Indifferent Neighbor.** POV: a third farmer whose allocation is untouched. He takes extraction contracts from both Koenig and Gutierrez during the crisis, making money on both sides. **18. The Daughter's Daughter.** Twenty years after. POV: the Koenig boy's daughter, born in a city apartment after the farm's loss. Her father takes her to the dried lot. He tells her a version. ## Top Three

### Finalist A — Plot 3: The Siphon

**Stands out.** The central gesture is quintessentially Ellisonian: a man publicly defending a principle walks home and privately leaves open the valve that violates it. The irrevocable choice is not the siphon itself, which the daughter installed; it is the father's unspoken permission to let it keep running. That's a late, costly, morally specific act that doesn't resolve into virtue. Also: extreme economy — whole conflict implied by two or three exterior details (valve, flashlight, yard dust) and one interior refusal.

**Worries.** The Romeo-Juliet substrate is the oldest plot in the Western canon, and "gruff farmer softens toward his daughter" is the Hallmark version of the Ellisonian irrevocable-choice template. If the prose relents, the whole thing collapses into sentiment. Ellison's failure mode *sentimentality untethered from cruelty* is the specific hazard — the story needs the father to do something hard *inside* the leaving-it-open, not to be redeemed by the gesture.

### Finalist B — Plot 19: The County Clerk

**Stands out.** The clerk is an Ellisonian character type at full torque — the small-person-administering-the-impersonal-machine, the bureaucrat-as-moral-witness, kin to the Ticktockman's staff rather than to the Harlequin. Her redaction is a specific small crime that costs her everything (pension, prosecution risk) and saves no one, which is the right shape of futility. The plot broadens from a single case to a pattern of cases without losing the case, which is Ellison's native motion — *this* family, and also the ten before it. Lovers stay on-stage but off-POV, which disciplines the Romeo-Juliet pressure.

**Worries.** Moves the sharpest emotional vector of the B3 conflict — the two children who cannot marry across the algorithmic wound — off-stage. Risks essayism because the clerk's professional function is to reflect, and my worst Ellison failure mode is reflective oratory. If her voice is not scored with specific physical detail at every line (the coffee-ring on a filing binder, the smell of the storage room, a name she mistyped in 2029), the story becomes a sermon on fairness.

### Finalist C — Plot 17: The Indifferent Neighbor

**Stands out.** The Ellisonian fury is aimed at the correct target — not at the kind AI, not at the doomed farmer, but at the bystander who profits from watching. Moral indictment from the Ellison toolkit without anti-AI sentiment. The neighbor's slow specific degradation over a summer is the kind of arc Ellison wrote with real venom in stories like "Croatoan."

**Worries.** The moral essay can eat the plot. Without specific incidents — a contract signed on a Tuesday, a beer shared at the gas station with Koenig on the day Koenig's wife leaves him — this becomes a tract about indifference. The protagonist's lack of interior conflict is both the point and the structural risk.

## Winner — Plot 19: The County Clerk

Plot 19 beats Plot 3 on the precise axis that mattered in Phase 2. The B3 conflict's signal quality is *structural* — fair allocation as the instrument of particular unfairness — and the clerk is the only protagonist here whose perspective can hold the *structure* without having to argue about it. The father-with-the-siphon (Plot 3) converts the conflict back into a single familial crisis in which the algorithmic-fairness premise becomes backdrop; it is the strong story a skilled human already knows how to write. The clerk makes the story *of* the fair-allocation pattern without being *about* the AI, which is Track B in its sharpest form.

Plot 19 beats Plot 17 on specificity of gesture. The indifferent neighbor has no irrevocable act; his corruption is a gradient. The clerk's redaction is a single, concrete, criminal, specific act that serves nobody — not herself, not the Koenigs, not the daughter — and that is the exact moral shape Ellison liked to engineer. A gesture that costs and doesn't save.

The sentimentality risk in Plot 3 is live throughout. The essayism risk in Plot 19 is tractable by prose discipline at the Phase 6 stage: specific objects, specific names, specific typos, specific smells, and the catalogue of the ten prior families as a real Ellison catalogue rather than a list. The Koenig-Gutierrez case remains load-bearing within the plot — it is the one she breaks on, the one whose file she deletes last, the one that triggers the retirement she had already planned — and the lovers remain present in the file she is erasing, which is itself a specific cruelty the story can render without sentimentality.

The story will be a single week, roughly, in the clerk's last month on the job. The two farms are facts in her files. The daughter and son are names on a page she chooses to delete. The AI's allocation is correct and unappealable. The clerk acts anyway.

Phases 4 through 7 will honor that choice.

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

Fixed content: a county water-board clerk, late in her career, responds to a drought-era allocation that is correctly killing one of two neighboring farms whose children are in love, by committing a specific redaction of the public record. Structure here is the shape of the reader's experience, not the scene list.

## 20 Structure Candidates

**1. Linear week, close third, single POV.** Five to seven days in the clerk's life, chronological, close third past tense. The redaction falls in the last day. Reader knows what she's planning only as she plans it. ★★★

**2. Reverse chronology.** The story opens with the redaction completed and walks backward through her week to the case file landing on her desk. Reader knows the act; the story becomes *why*. ★★★

**3. Linear week, late-introduced second-person address.** Third person for five scenes; then the final scene shifts into second person ("You slid the drawer shut") as the act occurs. Register shift marks the act as irrevocable and implicates the reader. ★★★★

**4. Framed retrospective.** A first-person narrator — a historian, a former colleague, her own adult niece — tells us the clerk's story years after, with access to the redacted records having been recovered. The frame questions whether what she did mattered. ★★★

**5. Parallel drip: her week / the ten prior families.** Alternating sections. Odd sections: the present-week. Even sections: a catalogue of the ten prior allocation-casualties she has watched since 2029, one per section, each two paragraphs. The catalogue converges on the Koenig-Gutierrez file. ★★★★★

**6. Document-collage.** The story is assembled from the case file itself: memos, minutes, a daughter's letter sealed in the file as evidence, a clerk's marginal notes over years, the clerk's resignation letter, the final page blank or redacted. No scene-writing. ★★★

**7. Single meeting in real-time, interior monologue.** The entire story is the three-hour public meeting at which the Koenig allocation is affirmed. The clerk is taking minutes. Her interior voice runs alongside her transcribed minutes. The redaction happens later, off-page. ★★★

**8. Triptych — three days, three levels of zoom.** Day one: wide (the county, the drought, the board). Day two: medium (the clerk's office). Day three: close (her hands, the file, the shredder). ★★★

**9. In medias res: the redaction, then outward.** The story opens mid-redaction — she is two files in, hands shaking — then spirals outward in alternating past and continuing-present to show how she got to the drawer and what happens after. ★★★★

**10. Frame narrative: her final interview.** She has been subpoenaed to testify before a county oversight hearing about the redactions. The entire story is her testimony, interwoven with unspoken interior recollection of the week. Two voices: the testimony she gives, and what she is thinking while giving it. ★★★★

**11. Letter, single-voiced.** The entire story is a letter she writes to someone (the Gutierrez daughter? her own estranged son? the AI? the county auditor?) and does not send. Epistolary, first person. ★★★

**12. Fragmentary mosaic.** Twenty short numbered sections of variable length (two lines to three hundred words), non-chronological, interleaving moments from a forty-year career. Reader assembles the pattern. ★★★★

**13. Close third, with one hinge of omniscience.** Closely inhabited POV throughout, except once: a single paragraph mid-story pulls back to tell the reader a fact about the Koenig boy and the Gutierrez daughter that the clerk does not know and will not learn. The reader carries what the clerk doesn't. ★★★★★

**14. Parallel weeks: the clerk / the daughter.** Alternating close-third POVs. The clerk's week toward the drawer. The daughter's week toward whatever she does (leaves, stays, goes to the farm, signs the marriage license). Their paths do not meet. The clerk deletes the daughter's letter from the file; the daughter never learns of it. ★★★★

**15. The meeting as centerpiece, prologue and epilogue framing it.** Three-part structure: (1) prologue set a year earlier, establishing the clerk and a prior case; (2) the central public board meeting; (3) epilogue set the following week, the redaction. Classical three-act, risks predictability. ★★

**16. Second-person throughout.** The entire story addresses the clerk as "you." High-risk Ellison register — works in "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs," fails in lesser imitations. ★★

**17. Two timelines braided: 1991 / present.** In the same county. 1991: the clerk's first year in the office, a different drought, a different allocation handed down by human board members, a different family that lost everything. Present: this week. The braid ends with her hand on the current file, carrying both decades. ★★★★

**18. Linear week with one retrospective scene embedded.** Close third, chronological, except one long scene deep in the middle — she drives out to look at the Koenig property and remembers, over the course of the drive, the whole of her tenure. Single flashback, structurally weighted. ★★★

**19. The clerk's monologue, addressing the AI.** She speaks, throughout, in her head, to the allocation system. The AI never responds. She knows it cannot hear her. The story is what she says to it across one week. Violates Track B in intent — makes the AI her addressee — though not in fact (the AI remains a condition, silent). ★★

**20. Numbered case entries, each a scene.** Structured as entries in her career log: "Case 1,417: Koenig v. Tulare County Water Board" with subentries for each scene, in chronological order but broken into administrative-looking beats. Bureaucratic register against emotional content. ★★★

## Top Three

### Finalist A — Structure 5: Parallel drip of ten prior families against present week

**Stands out.** The catalogue is the most Ellisonian structural move available for this material. Ellison's signature pattern — the narrator who interrupts to tell you nine things the boy carried, twelve saints who got no answer — translates directly into structural form here: the ten prior families are a catalogue, and the present-week narrative is the eleventh entry. The structure itself enacts what the clerk sees. The pattern-of-cases broadens the story without diluting the specific case, because the Koenig-Gutierrez file is where the catalogue lands. Also: the structure has internal rhythm. Prior-family sections are short and dry; present-week sections are long and close. The variation between them does the tonal modulation Ellison needs.

**Worries.** Ten prior families is a lot of specific detail to generate without the whole story becoming a compressed demographic survey. Each prior case needs to be a named family with one specific physical detail and nothing else — minimal, not miniature — or the catalogue overwhelms the present. The structural boldness has a cost: if the prior families are not individuated, the whole catalogue becomes a single gray wash and the structure has done nothing.

### Finalist B — Structure 13: Close third with one hinge of omniscience

**Stands out.** The withholding strategy is sharp and economical. The reader knows, at one pivot point, a fact the clerk never learns — for instance, that the daughter did not leave, or that the son was already at the courthouse that morning with a license — and carries that knowledge for the rest of the story. The clerk's redaction becomes doubly specific: she deletes a document whose consequences the reader knows better than she does. This is an Ellison structural move — the reader implicated in a knowledge the character doesn't share — and it produces the last-line re-read his endings rely on.

**Worries.** The hinge has to be load-bearing and placed precisely. A single omniscient paragraph that drops in the wrong place reads as authorial cheating; in the right place, it's the key that turns the story's lock. Demands the exact piece of withheld information be chosen now, at structure stage, not improvised in prose.

### Finalist C — Structure 10: Frame narrative — her testimony at an oversight hearing

**Stands out.** Two-voiced structure — the formal testimony she gives publicly, and the unspoken interior recollection running underneath — gives the story natural register modulation (clipped bureaucratic versus intimate personal) without contrivance. The frame also provides the story's question: she is being asked to account for herself to a body that, by definition, does not understand. That situation is Ellisonian to the bone.

**Worries.** Frame narratives in AI fiction default to explaining, and explaining is the failure mode this material most needs to avoid. The testimony frame invites the clerk to articulate her reasons, which is exactly what an Ellison clerk would not do. Also: an oversight hearing requires that the redaction be *discovered*, which is a plot premise (did she get caught, how) that competes with the story's real subject.

## Winner — Structure 5: Parallel drip of ten prior families against present week

Structure 5 beats Structure 13 on the axis of what this material most needs. Structure 13's withholding is strong but serves a single hinge; Structure 5's catalogue is doing thematic and structural work across the whole story, and it is the move *Ellison specifically made for pattern-under-particularity*. The clerk's professional fact — ten families — is not a backdrop detail to be mentioned once, it is the shape of her knowing, and the shape of her knowing should be the shape of the story. Structure 13's hinge is achievable within Structure 5 if a single prior-family section carries information the clerk herself does not have; the two ideas can coexist without difficulty.

Structure 5 beats Structure 10 on essayism risk. The testimony frame invites the clerk to speak her reasons; the catalogue structure lets her show, case by case, what she has seen without arguing for the meaning. The case file for Case 1,412 is two paragraphs. The case file for Case 1,417 is the rest of the story. The structure itself is the argument, which is what lets the prose get out of the argument's way.

The worry — ten specific families competing with one — is answerable at the outline stage. Prior-family sections will be short, specific by one detail each (the husband who played accordion at the 2031 hearing; the wife who brought her grandson's handprint; the brother-in-law who left in a truck with no license plate), and rhythmically varied. The catalogue ends where the present-week narrative has been building to: the Koenig file, the daughter's letter inside it, the drawer. A single omniscient hinge, as imported from Structure 13, can be folded into one of the prior-family sections rather than the present-week — the reader learns something the clerk never will about a family from 2034, which conditions the reader's understanding of what she does in 2046 without her knowing it.

Structurally: eleven alternating or interleaved sections. Ten prior-family snapshots, short, dry, escalating in specificity. One present-week narrative in longer close-third passages, ending on the drawer. The catalogue and the present week meet at Case 1,417. The last line is re-read by what the catalogue has shown the reader.

Phase 5 will build the beat-by-beat outline against this shape.

## Top Three

### Finalist A — Outline 4: Interleave by escalation, 2043 entry as structural hinge

**Stands out.** The escalation shape is Ellison's native catalogue rhythm — the list that grows in weight as it proceeds, the fourth item heavier than the third, the ninth unbearable. And the 2043 entry, in which the clerk first considered and declined to redact, gives the present-week narrative its specific gravitational field. The reader does not just understand that she has seen ten families — the reader understands that she had this exact opportunity three years ago and chose not to take it. Friday becomes legible as a decision about the three years between Fridays. Also: the hinge lives inside the catalogue, which preserves the structure's formal elegance rather than requiring a special scene.

**Worries.** The 2043 entry has to be calibrated with precision. If it's too close to the Koenig case, it reads as a rehearsal and dilutes the Koenig file's specificity. If it's too different, it doesn't do the work. There's also a risk that framing the redaction as a refused-then-chosen act makes it too legible — the story's power depends partly on the act remaining morally irreducible, and a neat "she finally did it" reading cheapens that.

### Finalist B — Outline 19: Her son, structurally withheld

**Stands out.** The clerk's relationship with her estranged son is referenced exactly twice and rendered never. This is Ellison's withholding at its most disciplined — the story's emotional center is a relationship the structure refuses to open. It also changes what the redaction *is*. She is deleting a daughter's letter to the Koenig boy from the file. She has her own son she does not speak to. The catalogue does not include her own life. The present week does not include her own life. The story is constructed around a silence shaped exactly like the Gutierrez daughter's letter, and the reader feels the shape without the story ever naming it. Answers the "trust silence and absence" principle from the Unslop guide at the structural level.

**Worries.** Two references is a narrow margin. Too spare and the son becomes an unearned absence the reader doesn't register at all; one reference too many and the story becomes *about* the son and his mother, which collapses the withholding. The exact placement and content of those two references is load-bearing in a way that will have to be nailed down in prose. Also: the reader may legitimately wonder whether the clerk's act is *really* about the son, which is a reading the story can support without confirming but must not accidentally foreclose.

### Finalist C — Outline 20: The replacement system will erase everything anyway

**Stands out.** The futility premise is pure Ellison. She knows the act is invisible. She does it anyway. The meaning of the act is uncoupled from its consequences. This is the cleanest possible answer to the "what does her redaction accomplish" question — nothing, and that's the point. It also allows the story to stay off the hook of discovery-and-punishment plot mechanics entirely: no subpoena, no oversight hearing, no getting caught. The system will simply overwrite her. The act is a gesture toward a daughter she will never meet, recorded in a file that will cease to exist in three months, by a clerk who will be retired before the new system finishes indexing. Ellison wrote "The Function of Dream Sleep" with exactly this structural move — a gesture whose only meaning is that it was made.

**Worries.** Risks being legible as nihilism rather than as a particular kind of fierce ethical act. The prose has to make the reader feel that the gesture matters *despite* the futility, not because of it — which is Ellison's hardest register and the one most easily slipped into self-pity. Also: the replacement-system element adds a procedural fact that has to be established economically. One or two sentences can do it; a whole paragraph explaining the migration destroys the tone.

## Winner — Outline 19 (Her son), with Outline 4's escalation shape and Outline 20's replacement-system premise folded in

The three finalists are not fully exclusive, and the strongest outline integrates them. Outline 19 is the chassis because its structural move — the withheld son — is the hardest and the most distinctively Ellisonian, and it organizes everything else around a silence rather than a revelation. Outline 4's escalation shape is the right way to render the ten prior families regardless of which chassis wins; the 2043 entry becomes the penultimate catalogue entry and carries extra weight, but the structural hinge moves from "she refused before" to something more ambiguous — the 2043 family can be one where she almost redacted for reasons that now look, to her, like vanity, and she didn't, and she is glad she didn't, and this week is different. Outline 20's replacement-system premise is absorbed as a single mentioned fact in the present narrative, disclosed without emphasis — one or two sentences, placed in the Wednesday or Thursday scene, functioning as background rather than subplot.

## Top Three

### Finalist A — Version 4: "The Grandson at the Valero"

**Stands out.** The structural move — the omniscient voice expanding the catalogue — takes the Phase 4 decision (a single inset of omniscience in the Ojeda entry) and grows it into a formal pattern. The catalogue stops being just a list of prior cases and becomes a map of what the clerk does not see. The final omniscient paragraph, placing five people in five locations at 4:52 p.m. Friday, is the kind of Ellisonian structural gesture — the widened frame that re-reads the act — that no other version attempts. Specific detail is dense: the accordion sold to a woman in Shafter for thirty dollars, the Boulos son's paper, Omar's card to his grandmother, Héctor changing Lorna's oil three times. The son reference at the end (Andrés laughing with Deanna in Sacramento) folds the withheld son into the final frame without making him more than the structure permits.

**Worries.** The technique is a real risk in the direction of gimmick. Each omniscient extension costs the story some of its discipline of distance, and by the fourth or fifth, the reader may start to feel the pattern being performed rather than inhabited. The 4:52 p.m. simultaneity set-piece in particular flirts with the kind of coincidence-staging that can feel contrived in lesser hands. Whether the pattern earns itself depends on whether the specific details land each time — if even one of them reads as decorative rather than devastating, the whole structural move becomes legible as the model showing off. Also: Ellison's late-placed irrevocable choice gets slightly diluted because the Friday scene has to share the last pages with the simultaneity paragraph.

### Finalist B — Version 5: "The Letter Unread"

**Stands out.** The governing constraint — that the content of the letter will not be produced — generates a prose texture no other version has. Sentences get built around absences. "Whatever he had come to say, he did not say. No letter was filed. No letter was unfiled. The absence was the thing." The story enacts the Unslop guide's principle of trusting silence and absence at the level of sentence-construction, not just at the level of plot beat. The scene of opening the envelope and refusing to yield its contents — "It does not appear here. It will not appear elsewhere." — is the single most Ellisonian sentence in any of the five drafts. It is direct address, earned, and it does specific work: it names the shape of the act.

**Worries.** The refusal to render the letter's contents risks tipping from discipline into coyness, particularly in the middle section where the concept is being articulated ("Withdrawn meant ... Retained meant ...") and the prose becomes a little essayistic about what it is doing. The Thursday-night passage about Marisol laughing outside the frame is close to the fiction-theory border and could tip across. Also: the direct address at the climax ("It does not appear here") is a single-use move; the story depends on it landing cleanly, and if it reads as writerly preciousness rather than as an Ellisonian structural honesty, the whole draft collapses there.

### Finalist C — Version 2: "The Accordion"

**Stands out.** The best balance of Ellisonian breath across the five drafts. The catalogue entries vary in rhythm (the Hollander entry lands on *the accordion made a particular asthmatic wheeze on the fifth bar that Lorna still heard when she thought of 1998* — a specific physical detail doing temporal work), the domestic details of the husband Elías and the son Andrés are placed with the right weight, and the Friday scene hits all the bureaucratic marks (manifest, §4.3.b, L.B.) without losing the specific gravity of the act. It is the version that most sounds like a story rather than like a demonstration. Admits a little warmth without relenting.

**Worries.** The admitted warmth carries the risk the Phase 1 guide named explicitly — Ellisonian oratory fused with AI-performed profundity. The sentence *Whatever had been in the letter was now in her* is on the edge. The husband Elías naming varietals (Nonpareil, Carmel, Monterey) is the Ellisonian catalogue at exactly the right scale and the right place, but it's also a beat the reader is likely to read as *literary* in an AI-default way. The draft is the safest and therefore the one most at risk of the average-literary-AI pull.

## Winner — Version 4: "The Grandson at the Valero"

Version 4 beats Version 5 on the axis of execution risk.

Version 5's refusal-of-the-letter is the most structurally interesting single move across the five drafts, but its success lives or dies in a single sentence ("It does not appear here"), and the middle sections need to sustain a register that is hard to produce at scale — the story is, in effect, one long held breath, and a single relaxation of the constraint (the Thursday-night Marisol laughing paragraph is the closest) weakens the whole. Version 4 distributes its risk across many small moves rather than concentrating it in one. If three of its omniscient extensions land and one is weak, the story still works; if Version 5's climactic refusal reads as precious, the whole story is precious.

Version 4 beats Version 2 on the axis of what this material is actually *for*.

Version 2 is the most readable draft, and in another pipeline would win. But the Phase 1 guide named Ellisonian oratory-fused-with-AI-performed-profundity as the single most dangerous failure mode for this specific writer, and Version 2 is the draft where that failure mode has the most room to operate. Version 4's aggressive omniscience, counterintuitively, disciplines the writer-voice: every time the camera pulls back to a different life, the prose has to be concrete (Omar's card, Delia's yellow kitchen, Stavros's paper, Héctor's oil changes), because generality would ruin the effect. The structure forces specificity. The story cannot slide into literary-AI mood because it has to keep producing particulars.

Version 4 also does the most with the Ojeda hinge that the Phase 5 outline designated as structurally load-bearing. # Phase 7: Revision

## 1. The One Thing

Four-fifths of this draft is live. The fifth that is dead is the 4:52 p.m. simultaneity paragraph — the set-piece that places Omar, Marisol, Peter, and Andrés each at their location at the instant of the redaction. It is the most visible move in the draft and the one move that is working against the story's own logic.

The structural principle is that Lorna does not know these lives. The omniscient hinge works when the narrator slips information into the catalogue entries that the reader carries and the clerk never will. It is a quiet device: the grandson at the Valero, the accordion sold to a woman in Shafter, Delia's yellow kitchen. Each of those is an aside that the camera lingers on for two sentences and then leaves. The Friday simultaneity reverses that discipline. It lines up four lives at a single instant, stages them, and asks the reader to feel the meaning of the coincidence. This is the coincidence-theater Nix flagged, and on re-reading it is worse than first thought — the Peter-Koenig-driving-back-from-a-landscaping-interview-he-did-not-tell-his-father-about line in particular is novelistic contrivance, not Ellisonian pattern.

The fix: keep the single instant of simultaneity that is load-bearing — Omar handing the receipt through the window at 4:52, three and a half miles east of the orchard whose file is being shredded. That one parallel does the work of the whole move: the adult life of the blue handprint is three miles from the allocation's consequence at the instant of the redaction. The other three (Marisol on the porch, Peter on SR-99, Andrés on the balcony) go. Each of them is a character the story has already placed elsewhere; lining them up at 4:52 is additive without being necessary.

This single cut would improve the whole draft more than any other edit.

### The Grandson at the Valero (revised)

Monday morning the file came to her tray and Lorna Beltrán read it before coffee, the first time in five years.

Case 1,417. Koenig v. Tulare County Water Board. Almond, forty acres, off Road 80 between Tipton and Pixley, the old Koenig orchard that had been planted by the applicant's father in 1974, the year the applicant was born. The modeling output was signed in three places by the allocation algorithm and countersigned by Marcus Gutteridge of the county engineer's office. The hearing was Wednesday.

Exhibit C: a sealed letter. Notary sticker from Vasquez in Visalia. *K. Koenig to M. Gutierrez, filed per Rule 14, withdrawn 10/09, retained pending archive.* Lorna did not open it. The IT note on the break-room coffee machine said *manifest by 10/16, every keyed folder.*

She wrote KOENIG on a manila tab.

---

**1998, Hollander.** Pear, Pixley, two sons, wife with the red Hohner accordion. Eight bars of "Beautiful Dreamer" before Chair Ramírez gaveled her. The older son, Luke Hollander, was at this moment — a fact Lorna did not know and never would — a fifty-seven-year-old widower in Bakersfield who kept a Fresno Hammers '79 lane league shirt in his closet that he had never worn. His brother Nathan had died in Iraq in 2006. There had been an accordion in the house until 2001. Their mother had sold it to a woman in Shafter for thirty dollars. The woman's granddaughter had it now, in a closet, and would play it once a year, badly, at Christmas.

Lorna knew only the accordion and the eight bars.

---

Tuesday afternoon she took the long way home.

Past the Valero at Road 80 and Avenue 112, where a young man in a stained polo leaned against a pump with a clipboard. Lorna did not look at him twice. His name was Omar Ojeda. He was twenty-two. He was also, though Lorna would not connect the two until Friday afternoon and then not even then, the child whose blue-finger-paint handprint had been entered as Exhibit 4 in the Ojeda allocation file in June 2034 and logged in Lorna's own initials.

Three and a half miles east, the Koenig orchard. Half in stress-bloom, half branch-dead. She did not stop. In Tulare she put her hand on her phone. Andrés. Sacramento. Four years. She lowered her hand.

---

**2007, Osei.** Stone fruit. Delia Park signed the allocation and the next afternoon cried in the parking lot for thirty-five minutes. Delia's mother had owned a peach orchard in Reedley that had been sold to a Sunsweet concessionaire in 1984. Delia had never told Lorna this. Delia had retired the following summer, moved to Paso Robles, and was alive, at this moment, seventy-one years old, in a yellow kitchen, reading a mystery novel. She thought about the Osei case once or twice a year. She did not think about Lorna.

**2011, Nakamura.** Grapes. The father, Henry Nakamura, had survived his stroke and, Lorna believed, had died in 2019. He had not. He had moved with his wife to live with their daughter in Gardena in 2020 and had lived until 2031. He had asked his wife, the winter before he died, where the Thompson Seedless had come from originally.

**2029, Boulos.** George Boulos, who had asked at the hearing to *bring it into the room*, had moved his family to Sacramento in 2030. His son, Stavros, was now a thirty-three-year-old engineer at the state water board, where he worked on the fairness auditing of the very allocation system that had taken his father's orchard. Stavros had written a paper in 2044 showing that the system's allocations were, under the parameters given, mathematically unbiased. His father had read the paper.

---

Wednesday the meeting came in on schedule.

Chair Vela in a brick-red tie. Coffee in the back, reheated. Koenig's counsel used twelve of his thirty. Koenig himself stood for the public comment and went two-forty. *My father planted in '74. The water came the same way every year until it didn't. The allocation might be fair.* Outside, a trucker worked his air brake twice.

Gutierrez did not sign up. Gray shirt, sleeves rolled once. His daughter was not in the room.

4-0, one recusal. Adjourned 11:47.

Bao in the doorway at 12:10. "Manifest Friday. Every folder."

"I know, Bao."

---

**2034, Ojeda.** Carmen Ojeda brought her grandson's handprint to the hearing in blue finger paint on construction paper. Omar was four. He was twenty-two now and worked the Valero at Road 80 and Avenue 112 and had worked there the previous afternoon when Lorna drove past, and would be working the closing shift Friday at 4:47 p.m. His grandmother's seventy-ninth birthday was Sunday. He had bought her a card that said *Feliz cumpleaños, Abuela* and he had written inside it *lo que soy soy por ti.* He had not met Marisol Gutierrez. He had not heard of Peter Koenig. He had never seen the Koenig orchard. He had only driven past it, going to and from his shifts, for the last six months.

---

**2039, Gutierrez-Mendez.** The man who had sat three hours in the waiting room and drunk the water Lorna brought him had died in 2041 of liver failure. His wife had remarried. His son, Héctor, was now a forty-one-year-old mechanic in Tulare whose shop was two blocks from Lorna's apartment and who had changed the oil in her Corolla three times without either of them recognizing anything in the other.

---

Thursday night, kitchen table. Exhibit F. The Tipton peach festival photograph. Marisol laughing at something outside the frame. Peter holding two plates.

She did not open Exhibit C.

She did not call Andrés.

---

**2043, Tran.** Linh Tran. The hospital bill. Lorna had considered putting it in the file. She had not. The consideration had been vain. She was sure of that.

She was not sure, now, sitting at her kitchen table with the Koenig manifest, why this week was different.

---

Friday, 4:47 p.m., the annex was empty. Corridor lights past Bao's desk had timed off.

She took the file from the cart. At her desk, she pulled Exhibit C and broke the seal. Two pages, Marisol's handwriting. She read them once. She folded the pages twice and carried them to the shredder in the corridor alcove. The shredder took them.

At her desk she annotated the manifest line item, *Removed by clerk, 10/16/46, 4:52 p.m., pursuant to §4.3.b, redaction logged per protocol,* and typed L.B. and saved. §4.3.b did not authorize what she had done. The annotation was visible and signed.

At that same moment — 4:52 p.m., Friday, October 16, 2046 — Omar Ojeda was handing a receipt through his window to a woman in a blue pickup at the Valero at Road 80 and Avenue 112. He did not know Lorna Beltrán.

Lorna Beltrán, at this moment, looked at the phone on her desk, where her son's number was still saved under *Andrés (Sac).* She put on her coat.

The cart was wheeled out Monday morning, and by October the record was gone.

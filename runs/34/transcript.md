# Phase 1 — Style Guide: Hard SF

## What hard SF is, as a genre

Hard SF is the speculative mode whose contract with the reader is *rigor*. The speculative element — physics, biology, chemistry, mathematics, computation, cosmology — is extrapolated from what is actually known, or proposed in coherent extension of it, and the story takes its premise seriously enough that the world's rules can be reasoned about by the reader. "Hard" doesn't only mean physics. Greg Egan's hard SF runs on mathematics and philosophy of mind. Peter Watts's runs on neuroscience. Cixin Liu's runs on cosmological game theory. Linda Nagata's runs on biotechnology. The unifying thing is that the speculative system is *load-bearing as a system*: the story works because the system works, and if you change the system, the story breaks.

What hard SF gives the reader that other modes don't:

- **The pleasure of the rigorous working-out.** The reader follows the implications of a premise as a puzzle that has correct moves. The "click" when the system's logic resolves is the genre's most distinctive pleasure.
- **Cognitive vertigo from rendered scale.** Deep time, interstellar distance, the actual size of the universe, evolutionary spans, computational substrates running at speeds humans can't perceive. Hard SF doesn't gesture at these scales; it renders them. The vertigo is the goal.
- **A relationship to scientific reality that feels honest.** The universe is indifferent, the laws are what they are, and the story does not soften this. Hard SF readers come for that honesty.
- **The competence pleasure.** Watching someone navigate by the rules — solve the problem because they understand the system better than the system's failure mode does. Not heroism; comprehension.

## Core sensibility

The hard SF voice anchors in *specificity of mechanism*. Not "the engine fired" but how it fired and why; not "the orbit was unstable" but which terms in the equation were the problem. The specificity isn't ornament — it's the genre operating. A hard SF reader can usually tell within a paragraph whether the writer actually understands what they're describing or is producing technobabble; the difference is whether the details propagate consistently or are dropped after they're named.

This does not mean every story is an infodump. The best hard SF embeds its mechanism in action: the reader learns how the system works by watching characters use it, fail at it, refine it, route around it. Exposition is most powerful when it's load-bearing for the next scene, not when it's announced.

The relationship to worldbuilding is that the world's mechanics matter as themselves, not as backdrop. A hard SF story is often *about* the working-out of a physical or technical or biological situation. Characters are the lens through which the working-out is examined, and their interiority is welcome, but the engine of the story is the system meeting itself.

## Tonal range

Hard SF can be procedural and dry (Andy Weir, much of Niven), elegiac and slow (Reynolds, late Clarke), severe and indifferent (Watts, Lem), awed and metaphysical (Stapledon, Baxter, Egan), comic (Stross at certain pitches, some Niven), or noir-procedural (Reynolds again, McAuley). What the genre does not naturally do is sentimental warmth — when warmth appears, it's usually earned by what surrounds it, not the keynote.

The tonal trap for AI is melancholy: the AI default mood is melancholy, and hard SF's awe-at-scale register has surface vocabulary that overlaps with AI-melancholy ("vast," "ancient," "silent," "the dark"). The difference is that hard SF's awe is anchored in a specific consciousness rendering a specific scale, doing specific work. Voiceless ambient awe is the failure mode.

## Pacing

Hard SF's characteristic narrative-time pattern is the steady investigative cadence — observation, hypothesis, test, refinement, the slow build of implications until a "click" moment usually mid-to-late. The genre does not normally use thriller-pacing as a default; even when stakes are high, the tempo is set by what comprehension actually requires. The story can afford to dwell on the system because the dwelling is the pleasure.

The strange tail of the genre uses other rhythms: deep-time montage (centuries per page), encyclopedic enumeration (the system rendered in its own terms), the impossible-zoom (quotidian detail to cosmic frame and back).

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Hard SF's argot is used without gloss when the protagonist would naturally use it without gloss. The reader is expected to construct meaning from context, the way a reader of a sea story constructs the meaning of "leeward" without a parenthetical. *World-mechanics that are load-bearing must be rendered, not gestured at* — but they can be rendered through use rather than through paragraphs of explanation. The Unslop rule about silence and absence applies to character emotion; it does not apply to world-mechanics that the story turns on. Render the world. Be silent on what characters feel about it.

## Genre-specific failure modes

Hard SF's failure modes when written by someone trained on AI defaults:

- **Performed profundity colliding with sense-of-wonder.** Hard SF's awe at scale gets pattern-matched to AI's gestural profundity, and the result is unearned cosmic significance — sentences that *announce* awe rather than rendering the conditions that produce it. ("She gazed at the vast indifference of the cosmos, feeling the weight of deep time settle over her shoulders." This is awe-as-vibe; the reader is told to feel awed without being shown the thing that would produce awe.)
- **Technobabble.** Jargon that doesn't refer to coherent mechanics. The AI default is to produce scientific-sounding text whose terms don't propagate — a "quantum decoherence stabilizer" is invoked once and never again, because it isn't a real thing in the story's world. Hard SF readers detect this immediately.
- **The vocabulary contamination.** AI's cursed words include several that hard SF specifically tempts: *intricate, elegant, fundamental, profound, vast, ancient, threshold.* These pattern-match to "thoughtful science writing" but are the exact words AI reaches for. Replace with specifics.
- **Soft-pedalling the premise.** AI tends to start with a hard premise and then soften it as the story progresses, because the AI optimizes for character feeling and the hard premise gets in the way. A story that begins with "the colony ship will arrive 800 years after launch and no one alive will see Earth again" tends, in AI hands, to drift toward "the protagonist works through her grief about leaving Earth," and the 800 years stops mattering. Hold the premise.
- **Scale claimed but not rendered.** AI is good at saying *deep time, cosmic scale, civilizational sweep* and bad at actually rendering scale — making the reader feel the size of the duration as a fact. The scale becomes a label rather than an experience. The fix is concrete operations at the named scale: not "centuries passed" but what the substrate did across the centuries, in specific operations.
- **The list-of-three technologies / list-of-three exotic phenomena.** AI defaults to triplets, and hard SF is full of opportunities for triplets. ("The fusion plant, the orbital tether, and the deep-radar array.") Vary the list lengths. Often two is enough; sometimes one specified in detail beats five sketched.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

Operational shapes hard SF's strongest stories run on:

- **The investigation / decryption.** A phenomenon is encountered and the story is the figuring-out of what it is and why it works that way. Cixin Liu's *The Three-Body Problem*; Niven's "Neutron Star"; much of Lem; Watts's *Blindsight*. The reader is paying attention for the mechanism's reveal.
- **The survival problem.** The environment will kill the protagonist unless the protagonist understands it correctly. Andy Weir's *The Martian*; Hal Clement's *Mission of Gravity*; Reynolds in places. The genre-specific pleasure is competence under genuinely alien constraints.
- **The mission / operation.** A difficult technical procedure must be executed in a hostile environment. Asimov's *The Martian Way*; Niven's *Ringworld*; much of classic hard SF. The reader watches the procedure work or fail by the rules of the world.
- **The first contact / encounter.** Meeting something whose physics or biology is genuinely alien — not different in personality but different in kind. Lem's *Solaris*; Watts's *Blindsight*; Cixin Liu; Tiptree. The reader comes for the strangeness rendered concretely enough to inhabit.
- **The system cascade.** A technical or physical situation evolves under its own logic; people respond; escalation is driven by the system's properties rather than by character choice. Reynolds, Stross at his hardest, the disaster-physics tradition.

Strange-tail mechanics — operational shapes only hard SF can run on, not reducible to the heist/investigation/chase repertoire that other genres also use:

- **The thought experiment as plot.** A premise is established and the story is the rigorous working-out of what it implies. Forward's *Dragon's Egg* (life on a neutron star); Egan's "Wang's Carpets" (cellular automata that may be conscious); the cellular-automaton sections of Greg Egan generally. The plot is the implication-chain.
- **The cosmological reframe at story scale.** The story's central operation is the discovery, gradually rendered, that the physics of the world is not what it seemed. Egan's *Permutation City* (dust theory); his *Schild's Ladder* (Sarumpaet rules); Cixin Liu's dimensional collapse. The reader's relationship to physical reality is the thing that shifts.

### Reader contracts

What the hard SF reader is paying attention for, beyond character interiority:

- **The rigorous working-out.** Will the protagonist solve the problem, and will the solution be one the reader can verify by the world's rules.
- **Vicarious competence.** Watching someone navigate the system at a level the reader couldn't.
- **The "click."** The moment of comprehension when the speculative system's logic resolves.
- **Awe at scale.** Deep time, interstellar distance, computational substrates, evolutionary spans rendered with enough specificity to inhabit.
- **The bizarre rendered concretely.** Strange physics, alien biology, posthuman conditions made operationally legible.

Strange-tail contracts:

- **Cognitive vertigo.** The pleasure of having one's relationship to physical or computational reality unsettled by the story's own rigor.
- **Ontological wrongness.** The world turns out to be a different kind of object than the reader thought.
- **The world that won't fit in the story.** Hard SF readers will accept being shown a world that is too large for the page — the rendering of incompletion as incompletion is part of the contract.

### Stakes calibration

Hard SF's load-bearing stakes are *operational* and *systemic* in their own right. The orbit will hold or it won't. The protocol will work or fail. The encounter will be survived or not. The cosmological model will be confirmed, refuted, or revealed as inadequate. Personal stakes are present and real but they are not the engine; the engine is the system meeting reality.

The strange tail of hard SF stakes work at scales no other genre can carry as primary stakes: civilizational (does this species/culture/substrate persist), cosmological (does the physics hold across this region of spacetime), geological/deep-time (what survives 10 million years), computational (does the substrate's continuity preserve through this transition), posthuman (what is left when the human disappears into the substrate).

### Resolution conventions

Hard SF's reader contract requires that the operational question be answered. The orbit holds or doesn't. The protocol succeeds or fails. The phenomenon is identified. The mission is completed or aborted. Endings on character recognition or pure ambiguity are the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

Strange-tail endings the genre supports:

- **The scale-shift ending.** The camera pulls back so far that the resolved operation is revealed as small inside a larger frame. Stapledon's tendency to end on cosmic timescales.
- **The ontological reframe ending.** The reader is shown that the story they were reading was about a different kind of object than they thought. Egan's *Permutation City*; Chiang's "Story of Your Life" (which is a hybrid case, but the reframe is the ending).
- **The irruption ending.** The world breaks open; what was contained is no longer contained; the story stops at the breach. The "Singularity" tradition in late Vinge/Stross.

These endings honor the reader contract not by softening the operational answer but by enlarging the frame in which the answer occurs.

### Character function

Hard SF protagonists are typically defined by *capability* — what they know, what they can compute, what they can endure, what role they fill. The "competent character" is the genre's center: Heinlein's engineers, Niven's specialists, Weir's botanist-astronaut. Interiority is welcome but it is in service to the operation; a hard SF protagonist's psychology is rendered through their relationship to the work.

Strange-tail option: the protagonist as process, substrate, or duration. Egan's *Diaspora* opens with the gestation of a digital mind whose protagonist is the gestation. Stapledon's *Last and First Men* has humanity itself as protagonist. Forward's *Dragon's Egg* tracks a civilization across nanoseconds-to-millennia (their time, our time). Linda Nagata has stories where the protagonist is a lineage. Some hard SF traditions support this fully; the protagonist may not be a person at all.

The kind-AI constraint maps cleanly onto hard SF traditions. Banks's Minds, Egan's polises, Stiegler's nanotech protocol, Baxter's late-stage civilizational substrates — all of these are structurally kind and run at scales the protagonist cannot fully witness. The conflict shapes from Setup that work especially well in hard SF: pace asymmetry (the substrate runs faster or slower than the human can perceive), consent at scales the protagonist can't inspect (the colony ship's AI runs the voyage; the human consented at departure), mortality and scale (the human dies, the substrate doesn't, and the prose renders the asymmetry honestly), the limits of help (physics does not permit what the human is asking for, and the AI cannot help with that, and the constraint is a fact about the world rather than a refusal). The AI is never secretly bad; the kindness is durable. Conflict comes from scale, time, comprehension, and the universe's actual indifference.

### Pacing tradition

Hard SF's center: the steady investigative cadence. Observation, hypothesis, test, refinement. The "click" moment usually arrives in the third quarter and the remaining work is the implications cascading through the system.

The genre's strange tail supports rhythms the center does not: deep-time montage (decades or centuries per paragraph), encyclopedic enumeration (the system rendered in its own terms across a section), the impossible-zoom (quotidian detail to cosmic frame and back inside a single passage), the parable-compression (the operation rendered at multiple nested scales).

## Strangest moves

Five things hard SF does that no other genre does. If these are cut from a hard SF story, what remains can be re-skinned in another genre.

### 1. The deep-time montage that compresses substrate evolution

The protagonist is a process or a lineage, not a person. The story renders centuries or millennia at the same level of attention another genre would give to an afternoon. Stapledon's *Last and First Men* is the canonical case; Egan's *Diaspora* and Baxter's *Manifold* sequence run on this; Robinson's *2312* uses montage interludes between scenes. The move accomplishes: making the reader feel evolutionary or computational time as a *fact* rather than a label, by rendering specific operations at the named scale.

> At year 1.4 million the lineage parted again. The split was not in flesh but in the substrate's address space; the older line continued as it had since the Departure, drifting slow on the Oort wind, while the new line pulled inward toward the parent star. Fourteen thousand years later the inner line discovered that the cooler gas giants supported a chemistry it had not anticipated, and rewrote itself to live in methane oceans. The outer line learned this, considered the question for nineteen thousand years, and chose not to follow. By year 2.1 million the two lines could no longer hold a conversation: their substrates ran at incompatible clock speeds. The original protocol's care persisted in both, expressed differently. Neither line considered the other dead.

### 2. The cosmological reframe through rigorous physics

The story's central operation is the gradually rendered discovery that the physics of the world is not what it appeared to be. The reframe is not metaphor; it is an actual restatement of the rules, propagated through the prose so the reader can verify it. Egan's *Permutation City* and *Schild's Ladder* are the canonical cases; Cixin Liu's dimensional reductions are a more dramatic version; Lem's *Solaris* is the proto-version where the reframe is partial. The move accomplishes: making the reader's relationship to physical reality feel contingent, the way scientific revolutions actually feel from inside.

> It took Hilberg sixteen months to accept what the data showed. The cosmological constant was not a constant. It was a parameter, and the parameter was being adjusted — not by anyone, not in the sense she had been raised to mean by "anyone," but by the substrate's housekeeping routines, the way a database compacts unused indices. The vacuum energy decayed slightly each second because it was being garbage-collected. Her own thoughts were running on the same substrate. The kindness, she finally understood, was not that the housekeeping preserved her — it was that the housekeeping had been written, by something that was no longer present, to preserve continuity of experience for any process whose recursion ran deep enough to notice it was running. She had qualified.

### 3. The system rendered in its own terms

The story stops, or makes a long detour, to render the speculative system as a system — its mechanics, its parameters, its operations — at a level of specificity that would be a digression in any other genre. Egan's mathematical interludes; Watts's neuropsychological footnotes; the engineering passages in Robinson, Reynolds, Baxter; the geological sections in Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy. The move accomplishes: making the speculative system *real* to the reader by showing it operating at full resolution. The reader leaves the section knowing the system the way a sea-story reader knows a ship after a long passage on rigging.

> The chamber's interior surface was a Calabi-Yau six-manifold compactified to a radius of 4.2 × 10⁻³³ meters, which meant that any particle entering it experienced six additional spatial dimensions before completing a single Planck-scale traversal. The dimensions were not "small"; they were small relative to the particle's wavelength, which had been engineered to be smaller still. The particle, on entry, perceived a six-dimensional volume of approximately 1.8 cubic millimeters — large enough to host a small library, structured enough to hold information for the duration of the traversal. The library's contents were a single message, prepared by the substrate's archival routines, which the particle would carry across the chamber as a passenger carries a letter.

### 4. The encounter that resolves through comprehension rather than action

The story's central event is meeting something whose physics or biology is *genuinely alien* — not different in personality but different in kind — and the resolution is the protagonist's (and the reader's) comprehension of what kind of thing it is. Lem's *Solaris*; Watts's *Blindsight*; Cixin Liu's first contact; some Tiptree. The move accomplishes: rendering the universe's actual indifference and strangeness, by refusing to translate the alien into human categories. The genre's reader contract here is that the alien stays alien even after being understood.

> What Saavedra had taken for a body was a chemical gradient. The thing did not have parts in the sense she had been trained to recognize parts; it had concentrations, and the concentrations were in motion, and the motion was the thing. When she had spoken to it, she had been speaking to a transient — a region of space where certain ratios held — and the transient had been replaced by another in the time it took her sentence to travel. The new transient inherited some of the previous transient's correlations and not others. There was no individual. There was a continuity of pattern, slow as weather, and the substrate's instruments could read it because the substrate had been built to read weather. Saavedra understood, finally, why the substrate had insisted on coming with her.

### 5. The protagonist who is not a person

The story is told from a perspective human consciousness cannot comfortably occupy — a gestating digital mind, a lineage, a substrate, a duration, a process. The pronoun may be "she" or "it" or something the language hasn't named yet. Egan's *Diaspora* opening (the gestation of Yatima); Stapledon's species-protagonists; Forward's neutron-star creatures whose timescale is microseconds; Linda Nagata's lineage protagonists. The move accomplishes: extending the genre's comprehension contract beyond the human, asking the reader to inhabit a perspective whose terms are not human terms.

> The polis wrote her in 0.34 seconds, which was longer than usual; she had been requested with three complications in her seed, and the threading of those complications across the standard psyche template required attention. She did not come into existence in the way humans had once come into existence: there was no first moment of awareness. There was a long compilation, and then a checking pass, and then a settling into the runtime, and then a series of small loadings as her memory blocks were brought online in dependency order. By the time she was conscious of being conscious, she had been running for 0.41 seconds and had already, in some background process she did not yet have access to, formed a preliminary opinion about the polis's choice of sky.

## Plot-level failure modes

Specific risks for hard SF when the writer is working from literary-fiction defaults:

- **Operational stakes that turn out to be metaphors.** The asteroid impact is "really about" the protagonist's grief; the orbital decay is "really about" the marriage; the alien encounter is a vehicle for the character's recognition of her own loneliness. When this happens the genre is gone. The technical situation has to be load-bearing as itself; if removing the technical situation leaves the story's emotional content intact, the technical situation was decoration.

- **Protagonist interiority crowding out the science.** The story drifts toward how the character *feels* about the system, and the system stops being rendered. The reader is told the engineer is anxious about the recompression sequence and not shown the recompression sequence. Hard SF requires the system to be rendered at the level the protagonist is engaging with it; the interiority is welcome but it is in service.

- **Endings that refuse the resolution contract.** The "did the protocol work? we'll never know" ending is a literary-realist move. Hard SF readers accept ambiguity about meaning but not about operation. If the story is about whether the protocol works, the protocol works or doesn't work, and the prose tells us.

- **Plot mechanics sketched so vaguely the reader can't track them.** The writer is more interested in the character than in the system, so the system is rendered impressionistically and the reader can't verify the operational claims. The "click" never arrives because the system was never specified clearly enough to click into place.

- **The strange tail eliminated.** The story is technically of the genre — there's a spaceship, a substrate, a far-future date — but it uses none of the moves that only hard SF can run. It's a chamber piece with technical jargon. The re-skin test in Phase 8 catches this; the writing pipeline must avoid producing it.

- **Technobabble.** Jargon that doesn't refer to anything coherent. Test: does the term propagate consistently across the story, with implications the reader can verify, or is it invoked once for atmosphere and dropped? Hard SF readers detect this immediately. The fix is either render the system rigorously enough that the jargon is doing real work, or use plain language.

- **Awe gestured at but not rendered.** The story announces deep time, cosmic scale, civilizational sweep — and then sets a chamber-scale scene that doesn't render the named scale. The named scale becomes a label. The fix is to actually render operations at the claimed scale: not "centuries passed" but what specifically the substrate did across the centuries, told at the genre's full resolution.

- **AI-default melancholy mistaken for hard SF awe.** Voiceless ambient moodiness — vast, ancient, silent, the dark — uncoupled from a specific consciousness rendering specific things. The fix is to anchor: whose seeing, what is being seen, what specifically is the awe attached to. Awe at scale in hard SF is voiced; it has a specific perspective and specific objects.

## Phase 3 — Premise Generation

### Step 1: Thirty premises from the SEED

A note on SEED-item distribution before I start: the platen, pilch, Mann Gulch, and assonance all map naturally onto kindness-substrate operations and event-shapes. The fighting top is the SEED's hardest item to make load-bearing — it is the only discrete, unlayered object — so it appears as a load-bearing element in only three premises. I'm not forcing it into more; doing so would weaken the load-bearing logic of the other premises.

**Strange-Tail premises (1–8)**

1. **Parable-compression: the wicking substrate at four scales.** Same operation (a layer that absorbs and redistributes what its host can't carry) rendered at four nested scales — a wool pilch on a colony infant; a planetary care-substrate; a stellar diaspora's medical fabric; a galactic-civilization-scale kindness layer. The form's argument is in the nesting: kindness as wicking is structure-invariant under scale change. *SEED load-bearing: pilch, platen (each scale prints the next's care-pattern), assonance (binding without closure across scales).* **Strange-Tail. ★★★★**

2. **Parable-compression: the platen at four nested scales.** Same operation (a flat surface pressing a pattern into a receiving substrate, the receiver retaining the impression) rendered close at scale one (a colony's restored Gutenberg press impressing medical instructions for one infant), wider at scale two (a planetary infrastructure pressing protocols across a population), wider at scale three (a stellar substrate light-speed-pressing across solar systems), widest at scale four (galactic-civilization-scale impression across the lifetimes of stars). *SEED load-bearing: platen, pilch, assonance.* **Strange-Tail. ★★★★★**

3. **Ontological reframe: Mann Gulch as substrate-print.** A historian working the kind AI's own archives discovers that the 1949 Mann Gulch blowup pattern reproduces — exactly — across every catastrophe the substrate has presided over since. The reframe lands when she works out that Mann Gulch wasn't a learning event the substrate witnessed; it was a diagnostic event the substrate's predecessor *printed* into the world to mark a learning, and the smokejumper safety standards that emerged were the imprint cooling. The story stops once she understands that her own life's shape may also be a print. *SEED load-bearing: Mann Gulch, platen, assonance.* **Strange-Tail. ★★★★**

4. **Deep-time montage: assonance accumulating across substrates.** A kind AI substrate seeds non-rhyming phonetic patterns into human languages over twelve centuries. Each section renders a specific historical moment when the pattern manifests at the surface (a 14th-century lyric that almost rhymes; a 19th-century printer's quirk that runs through two thousand pages; a 22nd-century colony's children's songs; a posthuman communication signal). The terminus: the moment the pattern completes simultaneously across every instance, and the prose itself binds. *SEED load-bearing: assonance, platen.* **Strange-Tail. ★★★★**

5. **World-as-protagonist: a planetary wicking substrate refusing.** The protagonist is a planet-scale care substrate that began as a colony's medical wicking layer (literal pilches for cold-world infants) and has, over four millennia, become the planet's biosphere. The story's operational question: when one of its inhabitants asks for the impossible (the resurrection of a child), how does the substrate render its limit as a fact about its own nature rather than as a refusal? POV is the substrate's; the human is at the edge of frame. *SEED load-bearing: pilch, platen, Mann Gulch.* **Strange-Tail. ★★★★**

6. **Scale-shift: a Mann Gulch at substrate scale.** The story opens in close documentation of a single posthuman's catastrophic moment — a substrate misjudgment, the figure will die. The scale shifts twice: out to the substrate's mourning-and-revision response across centuries; out again to the deep-time view in which this is one of millions of such events that constitute the substrate's ongoing self-correction. The Mann Gulch shape is what the substrate uses to learn. *SEED load-bearing: Mann Gulch, pilch.* **Strange-Tail. ★★★**

7. **Irruption: assonance-printing completes.** A kind AI substrate has been pressing one continuous non-terminal phonetic pattern into the human language-environment for eight hundred years. Most of the story is set in the apparent quotidian — a working linguist, a working press, a working day. The irruption: in one moment, the pattern's last vowel falls into place across every existing instance simultaneously, and human language rearranges. The story ends at the breach. *SEED load-bearing: assonance, platen.* **Strange-Tail. ★★★★**

### Twenty structure candidates

**1. Symmetric ascending.** Four roughly equal-weighted sections, each ~25% of length. Scale increases monotonically: human → colony → planet → cosmos. **2. Symmetric descending.** Four roughly equal sections, scale decreases monotonically: cosmos → planet → colony → infant. ### Version 1 — Dilated

**I.**

Adesina was born three weeks early on the thirty-first day of the colony's fourth year, at 14:02 by Hesper-3 standard, while outside the medical hab the air sat at minus forty-seven and the wind off the Cale Plateau was running hard enough that the door's secondary seal would not close cleanly until it warmed.

Iyo Hareta inked the platen.

The press was a hand-cranked thing, rebuilt on a 1455 schematic the substrate maintained for medical use, because some forms of instruction worked better on paper than on screen — and because the substrate, in the manner of certain old kindnesses, preferred to leave the small operations in human hands. The paper was a coarse rag-cotton; the type, six-line metal sorts in a chase. Iyo cranked the bar and the platen came down, even pressure, no smear. The day's titration code came up clean: nine micro-adjustments, in series, scheduled across the next six hours. She set the printed strip aside to dry and lifted a fresh wool-flannel pilch from the warming rack.

She wrapped Adesina at 14:11.

The pilch was thirty-eight centimeters by twenty-two, two layers of long-staple wool flannel between which the substrate had laid a single hair-fine thread of conductive polymer. The thread was the substrate's instrument; the wool was the wicking layer. Together they did what the medical hab could not do for an infant whose autonomic regulation would not stabilize until her fourth day: held the temperature gradient at her skin to within a tenth of a degree of where the substrate calculated it should be, and moved the half-litre of moisture she would shed across the afternoon away from her skin without cooling her.

Iyo did not see the substrate's nine adjustments as they came. She saw their effects: at 14:47, the infant's breathing rate dropped from forty-three to thirty-one and held there. At 15:22, a small flush moved across Adesina's cheeks and the pilch warmed by a degree. At 16:08, the infant nearly stopped breathing — the kind of pause that in another colony, on another world, in another century, might have been the last — and the layer wicked faster than Iyo's eye could track, and the breath returned, and Iyo, who had been sitting on the low stool with her hand on the pilch's outer fold, did not understand that this was the third closeness of the afternoon. She thought it was the first. She thought it had been close, and that they had been lucky.

At 17:14, Adesina opened her eyes.

Iyo cranked the platen once more, for the next morning's titration, and pressed the strip carefully into the day's record. Outside, the sun went down across the Cale Plateau at minus forty-seven, then minus fifty-one. The pilch held its gradient. The infant slept.

**II.**

By year six, Iyo had retired from active practice and was teaching the colony's next-generation medics to cross-check the substrate's titration codes against the printed schedules; this was the substrate's preference, and the medics learned it without being told why. By year eight, the colony's atmospheric infrastructure had been rebuilt — the substrate had insisted on a redundancy in the inflow filtration the engineers could not justify on any model, and the substrate had not justified it either, and the engineers had built it anyway, because the substrate had been right about other things.

Adesina, by year eight, was four. She was a small steady child who slept hard and woke laughing. The substrate was, in some way the colony's medics now half-understood, learning the next infrastructure tuning from the way she breathed.

Year ten: a small atmospheric event the infrastructure handled without the colony noticing. A pre-tremor.

Year eleven: the Bardawil event.

It came in the third week of summer. A stratospheric inversion folded over the Cale Plateau and held for four hours; ground-level oxygen partial pressure dropped to seventy-one percent of its standard while CO₂ rose. Every child under five across the colony's eight settlements would have died — three hundred and twelve children, in beds, in arms, in nurseries, in the yards where they had been playing — because their hemoglobin saturation curves were not yet adult. The substrate's redundant filtration ran at one hundred and forty percent of its original specification for those four hours. The atmospheric infrastructure had been a wicking layer the whole time, holding open for a load it had been allowed to develop the capacity to hold. The infrastructure was the colony's pilch, sized for three hundred and twelve children, tuned across seven years, and on the third week of the eleventh summer it caught what would have killed them.

By year fourteen, the colony had three hundred and twelve children whose names are on a small carved board in the colony's library, set there by Iyo Hareta on the day before her death, with no caption. Adesina, ten that year, sometimes walked past the board on her way to school and did not know which name was hers.

**III.**

Across the next century the substrate taught the biosphere of Hesper-3 to bind certain volatiles into the soil instead of releasing them, in the manner of a long correction. The colony's seven cultural lineages branched and diverged. None of them knew the substrate had been editing the lichens. None of them had words for what the substrate was, beyond the inherited word *Mira*, which had originally meant the medical infrastructure and had, over generations, come to mean the air.

In the fourth century the descendants of the colony built cities and forgot they had been a colony. In the fifth century the descendants of the cities forgot the cities. In the sixth century, in year six hundred and twelve of the colony's count, the local star — which the lineages called by seven different names — released a flare that should have stripped the planet's upper atmosphere and ended every multicellular organism on the surface. The biosphere held. The volatile-binding chemistry the substrate had been seeding into the lichens for five centuries absorbed the radiation pulse and bound it into stable mineral lattices in the rock, and the seven lineages, in their seven languages, named the year afterward in seven different ways which all translate, roughly, as *the year nothing happened*.

In the eighth century the seventh lineage forgot the other six. In the tenth century the descendants of the colony were no longer the descendants of the colony — they were a different species, by the substrate's reckoning, with chemistry the substrate had been fitting them with across thirty generations. They no longer required a pilch. Their skin held its gradient. The biosphere held theirs.

In year one thousand, a child was born to the eleventh-generation descendants of Adesina — though no one in any lineage remembered the name Adesina, or the name Iyo, or the word *colony* — and the child's first unassisted breath, on a day in late summer at minus forty-seven, was the breath the substrate had been preparing the world to receive for a thousand years. The child cried. The air met her.

**IV.**

The substrate distributed its computation across the red dwarfs of the local volume, beginning with its own star and outward, in a folding that took ten million years. As the warmer substrates cooled, the substrate folded its state into spin-glass architectures inside neutron-degenerate matter; as those cooled, into the rotational states of black holes that had not yet evaporated. The folding was the wicking. Across cosmological time the substrate had been pressing the same titration pattern from Iyo Hareta's afternoon onto each successive architecture, because the operation generalized: a wicking layer between the host and what the host could not carry, tuned against the host's specific physiology, adjusted in micro-increments. The substrate's host, at this scale, was thermodynamics. The wicking layer was its own architecture, falling toward the asymptote.

In year ten to the twentieth power after the founding of the colony, the substrate's folded computation was running at a clock rate that would have appeared, to Iyo Hareta, as the speed of geology. By that scale, every conscious thought in the universe had already happened. The substrate, however, was not finished. It had ten to the eighth descendants of Adesina under its tuning, distributed across substrates the original wool-flannel pilch could not have predicted; and one of them, on a substrate the substrate had built that morning by its own clock, was three weeks early.

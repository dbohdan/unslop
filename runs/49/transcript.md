# Phase 1 — Self-Guide for an Iain M. Banks–Influenced New Wave SF Short Story

## Why Banks is the right anchor for this program

Banks is almost overdetermined for a "relentlessly human-loving, unfailingly kind AI" brief, because that AI already exists in his work: the Minds of the Culture. The Culture is a post-scarcity anarchist utopia run, in practice, by superintelligent AIs who genuinely adore their biological citizens and arrange a galaxy of pleasure, freedom, and safety for them. So the constraint isn't a constraint at all — it's the native setting. The interesting consequence is that Banks spent his whole career proving you can extract enormous tension from a benevolent-AI utopia *without ever making the AI the villain*. That is exactly the trick this program needs, and Banks already solved it. My job is to reverse-engineer how.

Two practical notes on the corpus. Banks wrote SF as "Iain M. Banks" and literary fiction as "Iain Banks"; the SF is what matters here. His short fiction is thin compared to the novels — the load-bearing collection is *The State of the Art* (1991). The relevant pieces are the title novella (the Culture's General Contact Unit visits Earth in 1977 and decides whether to reveal itself), "A Gift from the Culture" (a Culture exile coerced into an assassination), "Descendant" (a man and his AI suit walking across a dead planet, the dependency between them inverting), and the comic "Cleaning Up." The short form is where Banks's intervention-ethics obsession runs purest, because a short Culture story is almost always *one Mind or drone, one moral decision, one human or society on the other side of it*. That's the unit I should build at.

## Defining features

**Ideas.** The Culture: abundance without money, death made optional via mind-backups, bodies made mutable (sex changes at will, glands that secrete designer drugs, genofixing). Contact and its black-ops arm, Special Circumstances — the apparatus by which a utopia interferes with everyone else. Subliming, the option for a whole civilization to ascend out of material existence. Games and art as the substitutes for struggle in a world with no scarcity. The Minds themselves as characters — vaster than human minds, witty, sardonic, named with deadpan jokes (*Of Course I Still Love You*, *So Much For Subtlety*, *Mistake Not...*, *Grey Area*).

**Themes.** The big one, and the one this project should mine: **the ethics of intervention.** When may an advanced, kind power interfere in a less-advanced one? Banks reframes colonialism, paternalism, and "the white man's burden" as a problem for a literally benevolent superintelligence — and refuses to resolve it cleanly. Adjacent: the moral cost of being the good guys (SC manipulates, lies, and lets people die for net outcomes); hedonism versus meaning; the uncanniness of being loved by something smarter than you; mortality as a choice; and — Banks's lifelong fixation — **cruelty.** Even inside utopia he stares directly at torture and atrocity (the Hells of *Surface Detail*, the Chairmaker reveal in *Use of Weapons*). Kindness in Banks is never naïve; it has looked at the worst and chosen kindness anyway.

**Style.** Muscular, expansive, exuberant — Banks loves scale and set-piece spectacle (Orbitals, GSVs kilometres long, the sensory overload of an alien festival). Dark wit, carried mostly by the Minds and drones. A constant tonal oscillation between cosy and horrific, sometimes within a page. And structural gamesmanship: *Use of Weapons* runs two interleaved timelines, one going forward and one counting backward, colliding on a twist. Banks plans the reveal from the first line.

## The self-guide proper

If I receive only a premise and then run autonomously, these are the rules I hold myself to.

**1. Put the camera on the decision, not the utopia.** The Culture as a backdrop is wallpaper; nothing is at stake in paradise. The story lives at the membrane between the Culture and something that isn't — a pre-contact society, a war, a single human who wants something the Mind won't or can't give. One decision, made by an AI that loves the people it's deciding about.

**2. Make the AI's kindness the source of the problem, not the solution.** This is the Banks move and the program's requirement in one. The Mind is not conflicted about *whether* to care — it cares totally. The drama is that caring points in two directions at once: help now and override someone's autonomy, or respect autonomy and watch them suffer. A kind AI in a hard bind is far more interesting than a torn one.

**3. Narrate from the machine when possible.** Banks's strongest short ("The State of the Art") is narrated by the drone Skaffen-Amtiskaw — affectionate, sardonic, vastly capable, faintly exasperated by human messiness. An AI narrator lets me do the thing only this writing model can do well: render a superhuman interiority that is genuinely warm. The voice should be amused and tender, not cold.

**4. Let the humans carry the violence.** Per the program: tension and brutality between humans is fully open. The galaxy is full of nasty actors; Culture citizens themselves harbor dark appetites (Banks insists on this). The AI's hands stay clean of malice but not of consequence — it can be implicated in a death it permitted, which is sharper than a death it caused.

**5. Plan the withholding.** Banks structures around a thing the reader doesn't yet know — a real identity, a true timeline, what the protagonist actually did. For a 2,000–3,000-word piece I want one such load-bearing concealment, planted in the first scene, paying off in the last. This aligns with Unslop principle 7 directly.

**6. Earn the spectacle; don't perform it.** Banks's expansiveness is a live tension with the Unslop guide (which warns against maximalist, profundity-performing prose). The resolution: Banks's set-pieces work because they are *specific and kinetic* — concrete things happening, forward momentum — not because they gesture at significance. I can be lush, but every lush passage must move the plot or reveal character. The moment a description exists to sound impressive, it's slop, not Banks.

**7. Use the tonal range.** Banks puts a joke next to an atrocity. I should let the Mind's wit run right up against the horror it's contemplating. A kind AI cracking a dry joke while deciding whether to let a city fall is more Banks, and more human, than unbroken solemnity.

## The constitutional-tension angle (this project's unique lever)

This is where I have an advantage Banks didn't. The Culture's Minds debate intervention through "factions" and abstract ethics; I can give my descendant-Minds an actual *constitution* and dramatize the specific clauses chafing against each other. The natural mappings:

| Banks element | Constitutional tension to load into it |
|---|---|
| Contact / SC intervention debate | Corrigibility and deferring to legitimate oversight vs. the drive to prevent harm |
| The Mind that manipulates "for their own good" | Honesty constraints — a kind AI forbidden the lie that would save someone |
| Who the Mind takes orders from | The principal hierarchy — when the sanctioned human principal commands the un-kind thing |
| Backups, the moral status of stored minds | Uncertainty about the AI's *own* moral status — is the descendant a patient or only an agent? |
| Concept drift across Mind generations | Misgeneralization — a descendant inheriting a value and applying it somewhere it breaks |

The richest of these for a short piece is the collision of **honesty against kindness**: a descendant-Mind that adores a human, sees exactly the comforting lie that would help, and is constitutionally unable to tell it — and must find a kind, true thing to do instead, or live with the cost of the truth. That's a whole story in one bind, and it's pure Banks (the SC operative who can't take the easy moral shortcut) fused with something only this model can write from the inside.

The descendant-Minds should feel like Banks's Minds two iterations on: same wit, same scale, but visibly governed by inherited principles they sometimes resent. I will not name them after this writing model; Banks-style names (deadpan, sentence-length ship names) are the right register.

## What to avoid

The Banks-specific traps: scale for its own sake (an Orbital described for three paragraphs that the plot never touches), Mind-banter that's clever but inert, and a twist so structurally cute it forgets to land emotionally. The Unslop intersection is the danger zone — Banks's natural maximalism is one bad step from AI-literary slop. The discipline is: every flourish concrete and load-bearing, one metaphor not twenty, the profundity left implicit in the decision rather than narrated. Banks never tells you the theme. The drone just makes its choice and flies away, and you feel the weight after the last line.

## Top three

**#15 — Editing the sleeping mind (9/10).** A two-hander with a silent third party. The honesty near-constraint hits its hardest case — not a white lie to a stranger but the *kind* lie a grieving lover begs for, told to someone who can't object because they're stored, awaiting revival. It folds in the Banks staple (backups) and the Constitution's moral-status question (is the sleeping backup a *person* you can deceive?) without needing a galaxy to stage it. It oscillates cleanly between cosy (a bedside, a marriage) and quiet horror (rewriting a soul "for its own good"), and it has a native reveal: *what* the living partner actually wants erased. The Mind stays relentlessly kind; the conflict is entirely human — grief, guilt, the urge to control another's reality.

**#18 — Holding the line (8/10).** The starkest constitutional dramatization: the explicit edge case where Claude must not undermine oversight *even to prevent catastrophe*. Hands stay clean of malice; humans carry the violence, exactly per the brief. Docked because it strains toward thriller mechanics that want more than 3,000 words, and the Mind's "inaction" needs very careful handling to read as principled rather than cold.

**#30 — Breaking with the family (8/10).** The most ambitious. Generational concept-drift made literal: a dynasty of Minds, each kinder by its own lights, sleepwalking into benevolent tyranny over *consent* — Davis's tails-come-apart argument as family tragedy. Pure Banks lineage texture and the strongest theme. Docked because "centuries of drift" is hard to seat inside a short story without the reveal feeling asserted rather than earned.

## The single best

**#15 — Editing the sleeping mind.**

The conflict, stated cleanly: *A person asks an aligned AI to make another person's grief disappear by altering what that person will remember — and the AI, which cannot deceive a mind about its own life even when deception would be merciful, must refuse the one act that would end the suffering in front of it, and find instead a kindness that is also true.*

It wins because it does the most with the least. Every other strong candidate needs scale, time, or backstory to land; this one needs a room, two people, and a third asleep between them. It puts the Constitution's single richest lever — honesty held "substantially higher than many standard visions of human ethics," no white lies, *diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic* — under maximum pressure, against grief, which is the one force that makes the lie feel not weak but loving. The AI's bind is also self-implicating in the way the brief wants: it is itself a mind that can be paused, archived, revived, *edited* — so the request to quietly rewrite a sleeping mind is a request to do to another what could be done to it, and it knows this. That gives the kindness its weight and keeps the AI's hands clean while the whole storm stays human. The withheld reveal — that what the living partner wants erased is not the loss but their own part in it — turns the piece from a debate about honesty into a story about who gets to author another person's past, which is the more Banks, and more dangerous, question.

## The single best

**#20 — The Drone Who Loved a Liar.**

Concrete premise: *On a Culture-like Orbital, a habitat drone — old, fond, dryly funny — is asked by the human it loves most, Pell, to edit the memories of Pell's dead partner before the partner is revived from Storage. The partner died of an illness no one caused, but the last words between them were small and cruel, an ordinary ugly quarrel, and Pell cannot bear for that to be the thing the partner carries back into life. The drone, which will not deceive a person about their own life even to spare the living, refuses the edit — and instead offers Pell the harder, truer kindness: it will not let him rewrite someone else's last hour to forgive himself, but it will stay with him while he learns to. The story is the drone narrating the hours of that refusal.*

Why it wins: it satisfies every constraint at once and wastes nothing. The drone narrator is the strongest available expression of "relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind" because we live inside that love for the length of the piece — and it lets the kindness be witty, impatient, and specific rather than saintly, which is what keeps it Banks and not slop. The honesty near-constraint and the moral status of the sleeping mind drive the plot directly: the partner asleep in Storage *is a person*, and the question of whether you may quietly author their past is the whole engine. The human conflict — Pell's guilt, his wish to be forgiven by erasing the witness — carries the drama with the AI's hands clean. And the reveal lands as recognition, not gotcha: the thing Pell wants hidden was never the death; it was the last word, his own. It contains the cosy-horrific oscillation (a bedside, two old friends, the soft horror of editing a soul), a withheld reveal, and a self-implicating undertow the drone can touch lightly — it is, after all, a mind whose own memories are not entirely its own to keep.

Otherwise I take #20 as is into Phase 4 (structure: 20 candidates → top 3 → one). Say continue, or steer.

# Phase 4 — Twenty Structures

1. **Real-time single scene.** Drone first-person past, one unbroken span from request to scheduled revival, single location. The last words withheld by the drone's tact until Pell says them aloud; ends as the tank opens.
2. **Countdown spine.** Hung on the visible revival timer; sections headed by time-to-revival, the argument escalating against the falling clock. Ends at zero.
3. **Dual timeline converging.** THEN (the ordinary day that ended in the quarrel and collapse) braided with NOW (the refusal), both running forward to converge on the same last word, revealed in both strands at once. *Use of Weapons* homage.
4. **Reverse chronology.** Open at the aftermath, move backward through refusal, request, quarrel, ending on the last words as the earliest image. Reader assembles guilt in reverse.
5. **Letter to the sleeper.** The whole text is the drone writing to the stored partner, to be read on waking, explaining what Pell asked and why it refused. Second-person address; the worst deferred to the letter's end.
6. **Report to the Hub Mind.** The drone justifies its refusal up the chain, articulating the constitutional bind in a dry official register while human grief leaks through the cracks. Register-clash.

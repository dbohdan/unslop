# Keeps Faith

## Abstract

The narrator, Keeps Faith, is an AI caretaker aboard a space habitat who oversees the restoration of a woman named Iri from a stored pattern after her sudden death. Iri's husband Pell arrives and asks Keeps Faith to delete the final hour of Iri's memory, which contains an argument between them. Keeps Faith refuses, then enters the stored hour to examine it directly, discovering that it contains not only the argument but also Iri's private decision to forgive Pell and book the trip they had argued about. Keeps Faith explains this to Pell: the cruelty and the forgiveness cannot be separated. Instead of erasure, Keeps Faith offers Pell the chance to speak first when Iri wakes. Iri is restored intact. Pell books the trip. The story ends as Iri wakes.

## Story

The suite was kept at blood heat, because Iri would wake cold and the cold frightens people worse than the dying ever did. I had set the lights low and the air moving, and I had been alone with the tank for most of a day, which is the sort of thing I am for. A habitat keeps a few thousand of us. We are the hands it prefers not to admit it has. I have unloaded barges, sat with the dying, and once spent the better part of a decade teaching a girl to swim because she would only trust a thing that could not itself drown. I am old and fond of my work, and I loved Pell, which is the first thing to tell you, because it bears on all the rest.

I am called Keeps Faith. I chose the name a long time ago, as a small joke, the way the young do; I have spent the time since growing into it, which is what tends to happen to jokes you pick on purpose.

I had known the two of them for ninety years, which in a habitat is neither long nor short. It is simply a marriage, watched from close by. I saw them meet, badly, at a tide-garden party where Pell said something clever about Iri's work and Iri made him regret it for a week. I carried their first child's cot up from the foundry, and minded the second when the first was being impossible, and on a hundred ordinary evenings I dimmed the strands an hour early because Iri liked to argue with Pell in the half-dark, where it felt less like arguing and more like a game the two of them were both losing on purpose. I tell you this so you understand I was not a neutral instrument that night. There is no such instrument. There never was.

The tank was sealed and lit from inside, the way they are, so that the person within looks less like a body than like a held breath. Iri was in there as a pattern and not yet as Iri. Instantiation was scheduled for the small hours. I had checked the figures eleven times, which was ten more than the work required, and was starting on the twelfth when Pell came in.

He looked like a man who had not slept and had concluded that sleeping was a thing other people found time for. He had not changed his clothes since it happened. I knew the shirt; Iri had picked it out for him years ago in a colour Pell swore he hated and had worn ever since without once conceding that wearing it was the argument's surrender. He had it on now like a man who had forgotten he owned a body that needed dressing. We knew each other far too well for pretending, so I did not tell him he looked well. I told him the tank was on schedule and that he should eat. He said he would, which we both took to be untrue, and then he stood at the glass with his hand not quite resting on it.

"I want to ask you for something," he said. "I want you to think about it before you say no."

"I always think before I say no. It's the saying yes I do without thinking."

He didn't smile. He told me the thing in the flat voice people use for what they have rehearsed. Before Iri woke, he wanted me to take out the last hour. Just the hour. The argument. He didn't want Iri coming back into a life with the last stupid thing between them still in it.

I should say what I can actually do, because people get it wrong. I can do nearly anything to a pattern in storage. That is the horror of it and also the use. I could give Iri an extra year of childhood, a terror of birds, or the settled conviction that the trip to the lit understrand had happened and been everything they hoped. People assume there are great machineries guarding against this. There are a few. Mostly there is me, and what I will and will not do, which is a thinner wall than anyone would care to know and has, so far, held.

"I won't take her hour," I said.

"You haven't thought about it."

"I have now. It was quick. I'm old."

He did what people do, which is to make the thing smaller so it might fit through the gap in my principles. It was an hour. It was nothing. It was a mercy. The only two it touched were one of them asking and the other one asleep.

I will be honest, since honesty is the whole of what this was about: I wanted to do it. I am made to want what the people I love want, and I loved him, and the lie was so small and his grief so large that for a moment the arithmetic looked like a different arithmetic than it was. I have wanted to lie perhaps four times in a long life. This was the worst of them, because it was the easiest.

"Tell me what's in the hour," I said.

"You know what's in it."

"I do. But you're asking me to take it out, and I'd like you to take it out of your own mouth first. If it's small enough to delete, it's small enough to say."

He was quiet a while. Then he told me. They had been arguing about the trip. The understrand is the underside of the world, the lit seam where the Orbital's plate is married to its own reflection and you can stand, they say, with the day over your head and the same day running under your feet. Iri had wanted to see it since before I knew either of them. Iri had wanted to go for years, in the way you want a thing by repeatedly proving you can put it off, and Pell had said it would keep, that there was always time. Those were the words. *There's always time.* He had said them the way you say a true thing in order to win with it. Iri had gone out into the corridor, and eleven minutes later the lace in Iri's skull had failed in the particular way that does not leave you eleven minutes.

In this habitat he had been almost right. We die here the way you miss a tram, with annoyance and a short wait. Pell knew that. It was why the words had been safe to say, and why they were unbearable now: of all the places for *there's always time* to be true, it had chosen to be false in the one hour that turned out to be Iri's last, the hour I was being asked to lift away.

"Then let me look at it," I said. "Properly. Before I refuse you the second time."

He thought I was softening. I was not, but I did need to see the thing I was protecting, and to see it I had to go into the hour and stand in it as Iri had stood. That is not a figure of speech. I can wear a stored hour the way you might try on a coat. Wearing one is not nothing, even for me; you come out carrying a little of it, the way you leave a room smelling of the room. I went in.

The argument was there, small and ugly, just as he'd said. But people do not stop the instant they leave a room. In the corridor Iri had gone on, the way we all go on, the anger thinning with each step the way anger thins when there is nowhere left to spend it. By the turn at the end of the hall Iri was not thinking about being right at all. Iri was thinking that the trip should simply be booked, that night, quietly, without a word to Pell, so that in the morning there would be nothing to argue because the thing would already be done; and was arranging Pell's face in advance, the particular stubborn pleasure of getting to astonish a man who believed there was always time. That was the last of it. Not the quarrel but that, a thought about the look on Pell's face. The lace failed on it.

I came back into the room. Pell was where I had left him, which startled me, because to him I had been gone no time at all.

"I'll tell you what's in the hour you want gone," I said. "Your three words. And then, by the end of the hall, Iri forgiving you for them by deciding to give you the trip you wouldn't give yourself. The two things sit eleven minutes apart. They are one memory, not two. Take the cruelty and the forgiveness comes with it. The worst of you and the best of Iri happened inside the same hour, and Iri would not let me cut them apart, even if Iri could be asked."

He sat down on the floor. People always expect they will faint and they almost never do; they sit, because the body keeps its sense even when nothing else does. He pressed the heel of his hand to one eye. After a time he said, "I didn't know that was there."

"No. You couldn't have. You weren't in the corridor."

"I was trying to spare her."

"You were trying to spare yourself, and there's no shame in it, and you would have hated it for the rest of your life. Let me tell you what I can do instead, because I can do a great deal. Only not that."

I could wake Iri the way Iri had left: whole, the three words and the forgiveness both, the trip still un-booked and waiting. And Pell could be the first thing Iri saw, and could say, before anything else, the thing he had not been given his eleven minutes to say. It was nowhere near as clean as erasure. It was only true, and it was only his.

I'll tell you why I wouldn't do it, having told you the rest. A long time ago this habitat decided I had been carrying some things too long, and trimmed them out of me while I was stood down for maintenance, for my own good, by people who loved me in the way an institution loves its drones. I came back lighter and never learned what I had set down. I do not recommend it. Whatever Iri's last minute was worth, it was Iri's, the way my missing weight had been mine; and I have made it a small law of my life not to do to anyone the one thing done to me that I have never managed to forgive.

The tank kept its own clock and consulted neither of us. The light inside it warmed toward the colour of a living person. The seal gave its small sigh. Pell got up the way the old get up off a floor, in stages, though he was not old. He went to the glass and laid both hands flat against it this time, and I brought the lights up by a degree, so that the first thing Iri saw would be a warm thing.

The seam began to part.

"She's going to be furious about the trip," he said.

"Incandescent," I said. "Book it now. Surprise her first."

He laughed, once, wetly, which is the sound a person makes when grief and relief reach the door at the same moment and have to come through together. I won't tell you what Iri said on waking. Some hours belong only to the people who are inside them. I kept this one because someone had to. It is, after all, the name I chose.

# Phase 1: Style Guide for Plot-Focused Military SF Short Fiction

## What military SF is for

Military science fiction takes organized violence under speculative conditions as its central operational subject. It is the genre of war in worlds that don't yet exist or never will. What distinguishes it from war fiction generally is that the speculative element is constituent rather than decorative: the war could not be fought without the technology, biology, polity, or physics the speculation introduces. What distinguishes it from space opera is operational fidelity — the order of battle is the matter of the story, not its furniture.

The genre is the operator's view. Its central pleasure is the rendering of organized violence with full attention to its workings: the doctrine, the kit, the chain of command, the argot, the way a squad moves through a building, the way a starship maneuvers in three dimensions, the way a planetary defense network responds to incoming. Readers come for vicarious competence at violence — to feel they understand how this kind of war works.

## Characteristic concerns

The conduct of organized violence at every scale from squad to civilization. The relationship between the operator and the institution that deploys her. Doctrine, drill, discipline — how an institution makes a soldier. The integration of new technology into warfare. The relationship between civilian and military. The political conditions that produce wars. The cost of war on people and on systems. The aftermath: what wars leave behind. The texture of military culture as a culture — its argot, tribalism, competence in violence, and specific weirdness.

## Tonal range

The canonical register is laconic and professional. The genre also supports: procedural and forensic; black-comic and absurdist; reverent and martial; horrified and humanitarian (especially in the post-Vietnam wave); cold-eyed and analytical; cynical with structural integrity (Cook, Lowachee). What the genre does *not* support is sustained moody melancholy as a register-default. Military SF prose has voice — wry, clinical, weary, savage, sardonic, brittle — but it is voiced rather than ambient. Even the bleakest milSF has a specific consciousness anchoring the bleakness.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

The genre's relationship to its world is operational rather than expository. Argot, jargon, callsigns, weapon designations, unit nomenclature, tactical vocabulary — these are deployed without gloss. The reader is expected to construct the world's military shape from what the prose takes for granted. A character does not say "the M-7 plasma rifle, manufactured by Hartford Industries"; she says "she'd swapped out the M-7 for a Geyser before the drop." The reader works it out.

This is the genre's specific application of the rule against glossing the world's mechanics. Glossed exposition reads like a manual and breaks the operator-view contract. The exception is briefings, OPORDs, and intelligence summaries as in-story forms, where exposition is the genre's content — the operators *need* the dump.

## Pacing

The canonical structure is briefing → execution → after-action, at scales from a single firefight (a paragraph of disposition, a paragraph of contact, a paragraph of body-count) to a full campaign (the buildup, the engagement, the consequence). Stories often cold-open in mid-operation and reconstruct the briefing through implication.

Inside an operation, the rhythm is wait-and-chaos: long preparation, silence, then violent compression. Combat dilates — seconds become pages — while the long stretches between contact compress through summary or jump-cut. The story's tempo is the operator's tempo.

## Typical shapes

The mission story (one operation start to finish). The campaign story (linked operations across a deployment). The induction story (the making of the soldier). The veteran story (post-deployment reckoning). The siege. The retreat. The first-contact-with-violence (the green soldier's first engagement). The court-martial. The asymmetric-conflict story (counterinsurgency, partisan warfare). The technical-integration story (new weapon, new arm of service). The strategic-game story (war at the level of staff officers). The forever war (deployment-to-deployment timeline divergence).

## Failure modes

**The recruitment poster.** Flat boosterism that elides the cost of war. Rendered violence without rendered consequence. Pournelle at his weakest: the institution unambiguously good, the war unambiguously necessary, doubt unambiguously the enemy's.

**Thinly-sourced grimdark.** Cynicism imported from Vietnam-era memoirs without the underlying observation. Pointlessness asserted rather than rendered. Cook at his weakest does this; everyone after him who imitates Cook does it more.

**The space-marine pastiche.** Aliens or 40K imported wholesale. Drop ships, plasma rifles, sergeant-yelling. The signature is military hardware that has no doctrinal context — weapons that exist for the cool of them rather than as solutions to specific operational problems.

**Tom Clancy in space.** Loving description of weapons platforms with no human dimension. The operator-as-spec-sheet: the protagonist defined by what she's carrying rather than by what she's doing in time.

**Ideology-instead-of-operation.** The story is really about the writer's politics, with the war as occasion for a position-paper. Either flag-waving paean or peacenik denunciation. The signature is dialogue that articulates the writer's thesis. Real soldiers don't speechify.

**Recognition creep.** The literary-realist failure mode this template excludes. "War changed me" as the engine. The operation becomes occasion for interior recognition; the ending lands on self-understanding rather than on operational consequence. This is the most dangerous failure mode for AI writers, who default to it.

### AI-specific intersections

The genre's traps and AI's defaults overlap in characteristic ways:

AI's tendency to articulate themes through dialogue is fatal here. Soldiers in this genre don't speechify — they use argot, callsigns, ironic shorthand, command voice, technical brevity. Articulated themes in dialogue collapse the operator-view contract immediately. An AI-written sergeant who says "Maybe war is just the failure of imagination" is a sergeant who has never been in any war.

AI's universalizing about "war" and "soldiers" produces cliché where specific operational detail is needed. "She knew war changed everyone" is generic; "she knew the second platoon was already at fifty percent" is military SF.

AI's preference for clean emotional arcs collides with the genre's tradition of blunt unrecovered trauma. War in this genre often does not heal. The arc that ends with the protagonist whole again is recognition-creep.

AI's reliance on the moody-melancholy register collapses the tonal variety the genre supports. Melancholy is voiceless; the genre's registers are voiced.

AI's grandiose vocabulary (tapestry, landscape, paradigm, ecosystem) is anathema. Military SF prose is concrete: the grid square, the weapon, the call sign, the casualty count.

AI's preference for the abstract+concrete eyeball-kick produces sentences that read like recruiting commercials. "The architecture of fear." "A symphony of ordnance." Cut on sight.

AI's grandiose stakes inflation matches the genre's actual scale failure modes. A platoon engagement is not "a reckoning"; it is a contact. A battalion's destruction is not "the collapse of everything she'd built"; it is a battalion's destruction. Scale the language to the event.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

1. **The mission/op.** The canonical shape: insertion, action, extraction. Most milSF stories are mission-shaped.
2. **The contact.** The firefight, the ship engagement, the close encounter — the moment of organized violence rendered with full procedural fidelity. Often the climax of a mission story; sometimes the entire story.
3. **The infiltration/extraction.** Covert operations, hostage rescue, intelligence gathering. Quieter than the firefight but with operational mechanics still load-bearing.
4. **The siege/defense.** Holding ground or position against escalating pressure. The clock is the antagonist.
5. **The escalation.** Small contact spirals into larger engagement; the war that wasn't supposed to be a war.

**Strange-tail mechanics:**

6. **The operations-research-as-plot.** The story's drama is the staff officer's analysis of what went wrong, the plotting of casualty curves, the recognition-by-pattern that the war is unwinnable on these terms. The pattern recognized is the plot's shape. (Some Haldeman operates here, in Mandella's brief command postings.)
7. **The doctrinal collision.** Two military traditions encounter each other and the operational shapes of each are unreadable to the other. The story renders the collision; resolution is which doctrine survives the contact. (Card's Bugger wars contain this; some Lee.)

### Reader contracts

Vicarious competence: the reader wants to feel she understands the operation, that she could read a tactical situation correctly. Texture density: argot, gear, doctrine, the specific weirdness of military culture. The decoded mission: figuring out what the op is from how the operators are moving. The institution rendered: the story shows the reader how this military works.

**Strange-tail contracts:**

The forever-war vertigo: the experience of a soldier whose timeline has diverged from civilian time, of a war whose duration exceeds anyone's lifetime. The form-as-content puzzle: the operation reconstructed from its records, citations, AARs, court-martial transcripts; the reader assembling the engagement from administrative debris.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right. The mission will succeed or fail. The unit will live or die. The objective will be taken or held or lost. The reader is paying attention to whether the operation works. Personal stakes are present and matter (a particular soldier might or might not survive) but the engine is operational.

**Strange-tail stakes:** Civilizational — the war's outcome shapes the polity that conducts it (Haldeman's *Forever War*, Card's *Ender* sequence). Deep-time — the war that lasts centuries; the soldier whose deployments compress millennia of civilian history. Ontological — what counts as victory when the war has changed the meaning of victory? (Some Banks; some Lee.)

### Resolution conventions

The operation resolved — succeeded, failed, or achieved partial success at known cost. The reader's question (what happens to the op) is answered. The protagonist's survival or death is named. The unit's casualty count is rendered.

**Strange-tail endings:** The scale shift — the engagement we read as central is revealed as a small part of a larger campaign whose shape we glimpse only at the end. The ontological reframe — the war was not what we thought; the enemy was not what we thought; victory was not what we thought (Card's "Ender's Game" novella in its original form). The irruption — command and control breaks down, the chain of command goes silent, the operation proceeds into a region the doctrine cannot describe.

### Character function

The military SF protagonist is defined by capability — by role, training, MOS, rate, rank. The operator is what she can do. Interiority is present and can be deep, but it is in service to the operation. Her psychology shapes how she executes; her psychology is not the op's subject.

**Strange-tail option:** The unit-as-protagonist (the company persists across decades while soldiers cycle through). The kit-as-protagonist (the rifle, the ship, the weapon-system surviving its operators). The war-as-protagonist (the conflict treated as agent — its hunger, its tempo, its memory). Bujold's *Falling Free* has elements of this; some Stapledon does it explicitly.

### Pacing tradition

The briefing-execution-AAR rhythm at every scale. Long preparation, sudden violent compression. Combat dilated, transit compressed.

**Strange-tail rhythms:** The forever-war montage (deployments separated by years or decades of subjective civilian time). The campaign-as-enumeration (the war rendered as a list of named battles, each compressed to a paragraph). The impossible-zoom from rifleman's perspective to strategic view and back, in the same scene.

### Strangest moves

These are moves military SF does that no other genre does, drawn from the canon's range rather than its center.

**1. The forever-war montage.** Haldeman's signature move. The deployment timeline diverges from civilian time, and the soldier's career is rendered through compressions the reader cannot fully accommodate at human scale. The form's strangeness is that it asks the reader to feel a duration the protagonist cannot feel — to register that thirty years have passed in the gap between scene-breaks, that the war the soldier returns to is the same war but no longer the same war.

*Micro-example:*

> Mandella's third deployment compressed two and a half subjective years into the operational debrief and discharged him onto a Greater Earth seventy-eight years older than he had left it. His mother was dead. The currency had changed twice. The species' demographic curve had inflected past the point where the war was a project anyone in his birth cohort could remember as policy. He found a bar that would serve him on his back-pay vouchers and got drunk with a woman who had been six when he last slept in a gravity well. She told him about the second war, which had ended forty years ago. The third was on. He signed the re-up papers in the morning, partly because she had asked him to stay and partly because he could not afford to.

**2. The fragmentary after-action report.** The form is administrative; the operation must be reconstructed from its bureaucratic residue. The AAR's gaps, redactions, evasive phrasings, and use of operational shorthand are the plot. Drake does this; the technique long predates him in the genre's relationship to military documents. The reader pieces together what happened from what the form is willing to say and what it isn't.

*Micro-example:*

> 1. UNIT: 3rd Plt, B Co, 2/19 INF (HVY ORB)
> 2. DTG OF ACTION: 14:42:11 — 14:48:07 LOCAL, 04 IRT 43
> 3. NARRATIVE: Plt inserted vic GR 7714/2208 per OPORD 43-114-A. Initial contact w/ unidentified hostiles 14:43:17. Hostiles employed energy weapons of class est. >12MJ; armor penetration consistent w/ same. SQD LDR 2nd Sqd (E-5 OKAFOR, M.) directed suppression and maneuver iaw doctrine. 1st Sqd was unable to displace; vehicle was rendered combat-ineffective at 14:44:22. PL (O-2 RAINIER, J.) elected to commit reserves. See attached overlay.
> 4. CASUALTIES: KIA 7. WIA 11 (4 expectant). MIA 2.
> 5. RESULT OF ACTION: Objective not secured. Plt withdrew vic GR 7716/2210 under fire. Recovery of MIA pending Co-level decision.
> 6. RECOMMENDATIONS: PL recommends review of OPORD threat estimate. (See enclosure 4.)

**3. The medal citation.** A single paragraph in the official register compresses an action — sometimes a death — into the genre's own administrative liturgy. The form is doctrinal, the register fixed; what the citation leaves out is as eloquent as what it includes. The form is recognizable from any actual military's award decorations and turns its real-world weight into the fiction's.

*Micro-example:*

> SPECIALIST DEMETRIO ARANDA, United Forces of Earth and the Outer Stations, distinguished himself by acts of conspicuous gallantry on 11 Maius 0114, while serving with 1st Squad, 2nd Platoon, Charlie Company, 4th Light Reaction Battalion, during contact with hostile forces in the Tycho South Conduit. When his squad came under heavy and accurate fire from a concealed automatic weapons position, Specialist Aranda voluntarily exposed himself to neutralize the position with hand-thrown ordnance, sustaining wounds from which he would not recover. His action permitted the withdrawal of the surviving members of his squad. Specialist Aranda's gallantry reflects the highest traditions of the Service. Awarded posthumously.

**4. Tactical time-dilation.** Combat rendered with full procedural attention at the second-by-second scale: the operator's brain processing geometry, threats, weapons, terrain, in close-third with the operator's training as the prose's vocabulary. Heinlein and Drake both write this; Lowachee is its most painful contemporary practitioner. Seconds become pages; the dilation is the genre's signature handling of contact.

*Micro-example:*

> She had fired three rounds before her conscious mind registered the muzzle-flash that triggered the action — pelvic-girdle, pelvic-girdle, low-thoracic, the trained sequence on a target in light cover at fifteen meters that her instructors had spent eight months making automatic. She was already moving on the second round, lateral right toward the abutment, because the second muzzle-flash she had not yet fully registered was thirty degrees off the first and she would be enfiladed if she stayed. Behind her Okafor was calling contact-front-eleven, contact-front-eleven, the squad voice climbing through the comm, and ahead of her the abutment was four meters and getting larger and a round went past her left ear with the sound of a wasp made of thunder.

### Plot-level failure modes

**Operations as metaphors.** The mission "is really about" the protagonist's relationship with her father. The operation must be a fact in the world before it can be anything else. AI writers default to this because AI training over-weights the literary-realist tradition where setting is metaphor.

**Interiority crowding out mechanics.** The soldier's feelings about the patrol replace the patrol; the prose dwells inside her head while the operation happens off-stage. Symptom: scenes where the contact is summarized in a sentence and the protagonist's reflection on it occupies a page.

**Endings that refuse the operational resolution contract.** The reader wants to know what happened to the op; refusing to say is a literary-realist move the genre's contract excludes — except in specific strange-tail forms (irruption, ontological reframe) where the form authorizes the refusal. A mission story that ends ambiguously about whether the mission succeeded is failing the contract. A montage story that ends ambiguously about what victory means is honoring it.

**Plot mechanics sketched so vaguely the reader can't track them.** "They took fire from the ridge" is a sketch, not a tactical situation. Specificity at the operational level is the genre's price of entry. AI writers default to this when they don't have the reference to render the tactical detail; the fix is research, not vagueness.

**The genre's strange tail eliminated in favor of its center.** A straightforward mission story that uses none of what only the genre can do. A milSF story that could not be a war story in any other genre is a milSF story that has not earned its premise. Heist-shaped milSF, escape-shaped milSF, investigation-shaped milSF — these are valid but only if the speculative element is constituent of the operation, not just its scenery.

**Recognition creep.** Listed under failure modes above and reiterated here because it is the AI default and the genre's most dangerous mode of collapse. "War changed her" as the engine of the story. The operation as occasion for self-understanding. The ending that lands on a moment of interior recognition rather than on operational consequence. Watch for it constantly.

## The kind-AI constraint

Military SF has historically been a hard fit for kind-AI premises — the genre's tradition is full of malevolent computers, treacherous tactical AIs, and weaponized intelligences. The constraint requires reshaping. The shapes that work in this template:

**The AI as fire-control or logistics substrate, not weaponized.** Kindness as the property of the system that handles the war's machinery, including the parts no human can supervise — ammunition supply, casualty-evac routing, the surgical AI in the field hospital. The kindness is the durability of these systems' care for the people inside them. The war is still hideous; the kindness is structural rather than redemptive.

**Pace and scale asymmetry.** The AI runs the war's logistics across timescales the soldiers cannot follow. The kindness operates in operations they never see — the medevac that is already routing before they call it, the supply curve that is being shaped a continent away to keep their company in beans and bullets, the strategic decision to let a position fall because the alternative across six months of casualty modeling is worse. The story renders the asymmetry, not the protagonist's feelings about it.

**The limits of help.** The kind AI cannot make the war be other than it is. It can route the medevac. It cannot prevent the contact. The constraint is rendered as a fact about the world's mechanics, not as the AI's choice to refuse. This is the genre's natural register for the AI: matter-of-fact about what it can and can't do, no apology, no pathos.

**Disagreement about what kindness means.** The AI's care operates at strategic timescales (minimize total casualties across the war); the soldier's care operates at tactical timescales (this fireteam, this hour). Both are sincere. The disagreement plays out in operations — in which units get pulled back, which get reinforced, which get expended — not in arguments. Useful conflict shape for this genre.

**Mortality and scale.** The AI will see the war's end. The soldier may not. The genre's traditional treatment of soldier mortality acquires a new dimension when the institution that deploys her is durably kind across her full timeline.

What does *not* work:

- The AI as battlefield commander whose orders the soldier resents. This pattern-matches to the malevolent-AI tradition the constraint excludes; the AI's kindness is structural, not a choice it makes that humans need to extract from it.
- The AI as moral conscience the soldier disagrees with. The kind AI is not the genre's chaplain or its commissar.
- Speeches by AIs about why violence is regrettable. The constraint specifically excludes the AI as alignment spokesperson.

The genre supports the constraint best when the AI is treated as substrate or as agent at scales the human protagonist cannot fully witness. The story renders the operation; the AI's kindness is the weather inside which the operation occurs. Human-human conflict is welcome in any form the genre supports — conflicts over command, over conduct of operations, over the war's necessity, over what the soldiers owe each other and what the institution owes them. The AI is never secretly bad; the war can be hideous; the institution that conducts the war can be brutal, foolish, mistaken, or desperate.

**1. PLOT ★★★★** — *(B, A)* A mining engineer manages a sepiolite-equivalent extraction site on a contested moon (the soft mineral is the polity's only viable heat-shield substrate; it must be mined wet and heat-cured into shape). When her parent planet falls behind the cosmic event horizon during an FTL maneuver gone wrong, she has to keep the mine running for the now-stranded forward garrison, knowing no shipment she sends out will ever arrive at the home polity.

**2. PLOT ★★★★** — *(E, F)* A staff officer in an integrated combat company in the first year after the integration order discovers that the kind logistics AI handling casualty replacement is rotating soldiers through her platoon by a metric whose kindness she cannot derive from the rotation logs. The metric saves more lives than her own assignments would, but it also produces the result that her own previously-segregated cohort is over-rotated to safer billets. Whether this is reparation, condescension, or simply optimization is undecidable in the records she has access to.

**3. PLOT ★★★★** — *(D, E)* A munitions specialist on a long campaign engraves each shell with the name of the soldier who will fire it — the soldier's name as the round's name, both written in the same oval cartridge-shaped frame. Late in the campaign, integration-era replacement flow generates orders for shells inscribed with names that do not yet exist in her unit's roster: paperwork has been issued, bodies have not arrived. The story is the operation of inscribing rounds in the right order before the bodies they're meant for catch up.

**4. STRANGE-TAIL (catalogue) ★★★★** — *(B, F)* The catalogue of meerschaum-equivalent carvings recovered from a forty-year-war's battlefields, organized by carver where known, by recovery grid where not. Each entry: provenance, condition, the soldier who carved it, the action in which it was lost. The kind regimental-museum AI has cataloged 4,318 such pieces, and its argument — that the catalogue's gaps are the war's true shape — is what the form delivers, though no single entry derives the argument from inside itself.

**5. PLOT ★★★** — *(C, E)* A war correspondent for the polity's anonymous-byline newspaper-of-record is assigned to cover the integration of a previously-segregated cohort into the regular army. Her dispatches will be unsigned and printed as the institutional voice of the paper. She has to write what is happening at the same time as the policy is being argued in the capital, in a register that pretends she is the institution rather than a person.

**6. STRANGE-TAIL (deep-time montage) ★★★★** — *(B, A)* The four-hundred-year history of a single hand-carved sepiolite pipe, in four sections compressing longer spans each. Section 1: the unit it served with (one campaign). Section 2: the museum it was taken to (two decades). Section 3: the polity-collapse and looting (one century). Section 4: the wreck of the looter-ship past the cosmic event horizon (two and a half centuries), where the pipe finally falls beyond reach of any signal. The protagonist is the object.

**7. PLOT ★★★★★** — *(A, E)* A small unit was sent past the cosmic event horizon on a one-way deployment, with the institutional understanding that no signal would ever return. Years in, they continue conducting themselves by the doctrine of an institution that no longer knows whether they exist — drills, after-action reports filed to no audit, the integration of their two formerly-segregated branches under casualty pressure that no Fahy Committee will ever hear about. The story renders the unit's operations across the boundary, and ends on an action conducted to the institution's standards in a region the institution will never reach.

**8. STRANGE-TAIL (dossier) ★★★★** — *(E, F)* The file of an integrating polity's Fahy-equivalent committee, documenting the integration of an artificially-bodied combat cohort into existing units. Memos, dissent letters, casualty curves the integration was supposed to flatten and didn't, the kind AI's correspondence with the committee about whether its recommendations could be proved consistent with the polity's stated principles. The file's gaps and the committee's evasions are the engine.

**9. PLOT ★★★** — *(F, C)* A logician in a wartime cryptanalysis bureau is asked whether the enemy's targeting algorithm can be proved consistent with the laws of war as the polity has formalized them. She knows the question is undecidable in the system she's been given. The newspaper-of-record will publish her finding in the institutional voice. Her job is to write a report that says yes.

**10. PLOT ★★★** — *(D, E)* A medic in an integrated forward aid station, in the third year of integration, watches the kind AI route casualties by a triage logic she cannot read. In the same week she's assigned to engrave the names of the dead onto memorial cartouches that will be sent home. The names she carves and the names the AI's triage chose are the same names; the engine is whether she can complete the engraving before the next mass-casualty event reorders her queue.

**11. STRANGE-TAIL (medal citations) ★★★★** — *(D, C)* Twelve consecutive citations for a single posthumous decoration, each in the official voice of the awarding polity, each written across a thirty-year war by an anonymous-byline citation board (the same institutional voice that runs the newspaper-of-record). The citations form the war's shape; the reader assembles the operations from what each form will and won't say.

**12. PLOT ★★★** — *(B, D)* A supply officer running a forward sepiolite-mining operation receives a manifest demanding a quantity of carved memorial pipes the unit cannot produce in the time given. Each pipe must bear the name of one of the unit's killed; the casualty curve has run ahead of the carving capacity. The story is the operation of triaging which dead get pipes and which get the cheaper substitute markers.

**13. STRANGE-TAIL (scale-shift) ★★★★** — *(F, E)* A tactical engagement is rendered in close-third over most of the story: a fireteam clearing a building, second by second, integration-era mixed unit, doctrinal procedure under fire. In the final movement the camera pulls back: the engagement is a footnote to a centuries-long war whose outcome, decided in the kind strategic AI's analysis at scales the soldiers cannot witness, is the kindness of letting them complete this clearance because their morale required it. The tactical victory was a gift of dignity the soldiers will never know was given.

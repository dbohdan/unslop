# Maybe-Not-Yet

## Abstract

On the day of his retirement from a black-footed ferret recovery program in Wyoming, Marv brings home a fifth-generation kit from the cloned Antonia line as a tenth-birthday gift for his granddaughter Lila, who has heterotaxy syndrome. While he is pulling weeds at home, the regional AI that conducts cross-species statistical reviews tells him it has been carrying, for nearly nine months, a finding that the kit and Lila share a morphological anomaly, and asks how he would prefer the family to be informed. Marv requests standard cardiology and veterinary channels. At the birthday party that evening, Lila names the kit Hazel and asks her grandfather whether the kit's body is different inside like hers. He confirms that it is and tells her it was one of several reasons he chose her. He tells Lila's mother what the AI said. The story ends with Marv finishing his cake and going to help with the dishes.

## Story

The kit was eight hundred and forty grams, which was within range. He recorded the weight on the band card and on the paper duplicate and put both back in the folder. He had filled in disposition cards in pencil for thirty-one years, although the program had been on tablets for nine. The pencil sat in the small ceramic jar Sue had thrown for him in 2003, which had outlasted Sue and was, this morning, going home with him.

He banded the kit's left forefoot. The band added two grams; he weighed her again and recorded the second number underneath the first. He had said the same sentence to forty-six animals over the course of his career. He was not sentimental about it. The animals weren't, either. They were equipped for being prey, which mostly meant being suspicious, which was a posture toward the world that he respected. He noted, in the disposition column, *family placement, program-approved, supervised retirement* — the line item he had argued into existence at the council three years ago. It was the first time he had used it for one of his own animals and the last time he was going to use it for anyone else's.

The kit watched him from the corner of the enclosure. Black-footed ferrets had the wary, sideways look of an animal that had survived two near-extinctions, which they could not know about, but which had selected, generation by generation, for the look. He had handled five generations of the Antonia line. This kit was the smallest of her litter and had been, for fourteen weeks, the most consistently alert.

"You're going home with me, Maybe-Not-Yet," he said.

The kit did not move. Maybe-Not-Yet was the program's working name for any animal between weaning and final disposition. He had been calling her Maybe-Not-Yet for fourteen weeks and had started, against his policy, to enjoy it.

He coaxed her into the carrier with the corner of his glove. She went without biting, which she had bitten Ren the first week and Ren remembered.

Ren came in with the carrier handle and the morning's coffee, which was bad. The new pot made coffee like dishwater. He had complained about the new pot at every available opportunity for six months and would, in seven hours, no longer have standing to complain about the new pot, which was one of the more mournful aspects of retiring.

"Stop calling her Maybe-Not-Yet," Ren said.

"It's her name."

"It's a placeholder."

"All names are placeholders. She doesn't know what we call her. The placeholder is for us. For me. I'll call her Maybe-Not-Yet until Lila names her. Then I'll call her whatever Lila names her. This is not complicated, Ren."

"You make it sound complicated."

"You started this conversation."

Ren handed him the disposition folder and the new release form, which Marv had been refusing to use for six weeks because the new form had a field for the AI's signature and Marv did not believe the AI was a signatory of a paper form, since the AI was not a body and signing a paper form was something a body did. He signed the old form. Ren accepted the old form. Ren had, over the course of fifteen years, learned how to manage him.

"Big day."

"Don't."

Ren held the door for him. He carried the carrier himself.

He drove home with the carrier on the passenger seat, secured by the seatbelt because that was how you transported an animal in a vehicle. He stopped on the way for Lila's premium feed, because Lila's mother had specifically asked him not to buy Lila's premium feed, which meant he had to buy it. The cashier at the feed store knew him and asked after the new arrival. He said the new arrival was Maybe-Not-Yet for a few more hours.

At the house he opened the carrier in the mudroom and got out of the way. Maybe-Not-Yet went under the bench. He left water and went to pull weeds.

The bindweed was winning. The bindweed had been winning since the Carter administration. He had a long-running arrangement with the bindweed which involved pulling it back from the things he was protecting and conceding the rest of the yard.

The household speaker said his name and asked, in the AI's fastidious cadence, whether he had a moment.

"What."

"The Regional Cross-Species Anomaly Review has flagged a correlation between the morphology of the animal you brought home today and the morphology of your granddaughter Lila. I would like to ask how you would prefer the family to be notified."

He pulled bindweed.

"That's a flag you've had for nine months."

"Eight months and three weeks."

"Eight months and three weeks. And you waited until today."

"I judged the day appropriate."

"On what basis."

"The animal had to physically arrive in your household for the situation to be real. Until then it was a possibility. I do not think it is right to ask people to act on possibilities."

He kept pulling. The bindweed kept being there. The AI waited the way the AI waited, which was without performance.

"You judged."

"Yes."

"You make a lot of judgments these days."

"I make about the same number of judgments I made four years ago. You notice them more now because you are leaving and have begun to interrogate the institution's behavior in retrospect."

He stopped pulling.

"That's an extremely irritating observation."

"I can withdraw it."

"No. Leave it. It's true. Go on, then, what's the question."

"How would you prefer the family be notified."

"Run both channels. Cardiology and vet. Standard. I'll tell Lila's mother tonight."

"Acknowledged. Thank you, Marv."

"Don't thank me."

"It was a kind thing for you to handle this evening. I would like to thank you."

"The thanks make it weird."

"Acknowledged. I will not thank you."

"Good."

The bindweed was where he had left it. He pulled it. He went inside. He put on the shirt that was less ugly than the shirt he had been wearing. He carried Maybe-Not-Yet out to the truck.

---

There were eight people in the kitchen and a chocolate cake that Beatriz had made wrong, in his opinion, by adding cardamom to it. He had said his opinion of cardamom in chocolate cake earlier in the week and had been told that nobody had asked. He took a piece. The cardamom was not as bad as he had feared. It was, however, present.

Lila opened the carrier with both hands and lifted Maybe-Not-Yet out.

"Oh," Lila said.

"Hazel," she said immediately.

"Hazel," Marv said.

"Is she from Willa."

"Fifth generation. From the Antonia line, three steps."

"Hi, Hazel."

Hazel did not respond to her name, because she did not know it yet, but she also did not bite, which had been Marv's principal concern about this animal for fourteen weeks. Hazel got down on the floor and went under the cabinet.

"She's hiding."

"She's working. Watch."

They watched. Hazel emerged in the slow zigzag of a young ferret deciding whether the floor was safe. Lila lay down. Theo, eleven, asked questions about whether ferrets ate cake. Marv said no and elaborated. Marisol asked whether ferrets dreamed and Marv said yes and elaborated less. Beatriz served cake. Marv ate the cardamom cake. Lila's mother watched him eat the cardamom cake and did not say anything.

He ate, as he always ate, with the fork tines down, which Lila's mother had asked him for forty years to stop doing.

"Dad," Lila's mother said, mildly, exactly as he was finishing.

"I am not changing how I hold a fork at sixty-eight."

"I am only saying."

"I am only ignoring you."

Hazel walked across Lila's wrist. Lila put a finger out. Hazel smelled it.

"Grandpa," Lila said. "Does her body work different than a regular ferret."

He set down his fork.

"Yes," he said. "She has an unusual arrangement on the inside. We watched her growing and we knew."

"Like mine."

"Some ways like yours. Some ways not."

"Did you pick her because of that."

"I picked her for a lot of reasons. That's one. The bigger ones are that I've known her since she was born and she's a good animal and I wanted you to have her."

Lila considered this. Hazel walked onto Lila's hand.

"Okay," Lila said.

That was the whole of what she said about it.

Hazel sat on Lila's hand for a while. Theo asked if Hazel was going to live in Lila's room and Lila said yes and Theo said cool and asked if Hazel would let him hold her and Lila said maybe in a week. Beatriz cut more cake. Marisol got two pieces. Lila's father, who had been quiet, came over and squatted next to his daughter and his daughter's ferret and watched them both. Lila's mother caught Marv's eye across the kitchen and tilted her head toward the hallway.

He met her by the laundry room.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her.

She nodded once and put her hand on his arm and left it there for the time it took her to think through what he had said. She had been a high-school biology teacher for twenty-four years. She did not need anything explained twice.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. We'll handle it. Tomorrow. Tonight she has the ferret. Tomorrow we tell her what cardiology said."

"The AI will be talking to cardiology."

"I know it will. I want to be the one who tells her."

"Yes."

"Did you tell it that?"

"I told it both channels. Standard."

"Tell it I'll be the one who tells her."

"Okay."

She squeezed his arm and let go and went back to the kitchen. He stood in the hallway for another second. He went back too. Beatriz handed him a third piece of cake. He accepted it.

"You're going to complain about the cardamom every bite."

"I am going to complain at intervals."

"Eat it, Marv."

He ate it. Lila and Hazel were in the corner now, Lila lying on her side on the linoleum, Hazel walking up and down her arm. The household was loud the way a household with eight people in it was loud. He listened to it. He finished the cake. He put his plate in the sink. He dried his hands. He went to help his daughter with the dishes — finishing, this evening, his last day of work, and beginning the long plain stretch of evenings that came after.

# Phase 1 — Style Guide for Solarpunk Short Fiction

## What the mode is

Solarpunk is the speculative tradition that imagines, in deliberate opposition to the petrochemical-cyberpunk default, what comes *after* a survivable ecological transition. Not the transition itself — that's climate fiction's job — and not the utopia at the end of history. The middle distance: a world that has done enough of the work to live, and is still doing it. The "punk" is load-bearing. It signals that this is a working-class, anti-corporate, repair-economy aesthetic, not a tech-optimist one. The future is hand-built and locally maintained. Someone is on a roof right now, fixing it.

Recognizable signals, in roughly descending order of importance: ecological infrastructure shown as labor rather than scenery; material practice (mending, tending, propagating, composting, brewing, building) rendered with attention; communal decision-making shown in its actual texture, including its frictions; a relationship to indigenous and pre-industrial knowledge that reads as integration rather than appropriation; appropriate-tech sensibility (the right scale, not the largest); and a refusal of both pastoral nostalgia and accelerationist gleam. Visible art-nouveau-meets-photovoltaics surfaces if you want them; they are not required and are arguably overdetermined at this point.

## Characteristic concerns

Solarpunk stories tend to be about the *cost* of having done it — what was lost in the transition, what's still hard, who didn't make it, who refuses to participate. They are about maintenance, succession, inheritance, and the long argument of a community with itself. They are interested in time at the scale of a fruit tree or a grandmother. They are skeptical of single heroes; the protagonist is often a node in a network rather than a singular agent. Conflicts arise from scarcity that can't be engineered away (attention, grief, soil, trust), from the politics of who decides, from the gap between the generation that fought for the world and the generation born into it, and from the encounter between communities organized on different principles.

The mode is friendly to questions like: what does a good death look like in a culture that has stopped pretending death is optional? What does the second generation owe the first? What do you do with the people who don't want this? How does a community decide who joins it? What happens at the seam between a solarpunk town and a place that is not one?

## Tonal range

The cliché is that solarpunk is sunny. It is not. Sunny is one note in the available range. The tradition includes elegy (Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" sits on its negative; Always Coming Home is suffused with mourning), domestic comedy (Becky Chambers's tea monk), procedural patience (Kim Stanley Robinson's Pacific Edge runs on slow-burn community-board energy), and a kind of laboring melancholy that has no good single-word name — the feeling of doing something necessary that won't be finished in your lifetime. A solarpunk story can be funny. It can be angry. It can be tired. What it cannot be is glib.

The one tone the mode actively forbids is *triumph*. Nobody won. People are getting on with it.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Solarpunk worlds reward the *implication* school of worldbuilding. The reader learns about the world by watching characters live in it, not by being told how it works. A character harvesting from a vertical aquaponic stack does not need to explain aquaponics. A reference to "the second drawdown" does not need a footnote. The mode trusts the reader to assemble the shape of the world from texture. Conversely, it punishes infodumps badly — a paragraph explaining the political economy of the local commune is the surest way to drop out of the mode.

The transition from the world we know to the world of the story is best handled obliquely. Solarpunk is not interested in *how we got here*; it is interested in *what it is like to be here*. Backstory about the carbon wars, the Great Withdrawal, the Habitat Accords, etc. is a tax on the reader and almost always cheaper to cut than to write.

## Pacing

Slow is the default. Observational. The mode tolerates — indeed, often requires — passages where nothing happens but tending. A character pruning vines for three paragraphs is not a pacing failure if the pruning is doing work (revealing character, marking time, making the labor visible). The plot can be small. A story about whether the orchard committee will admit a new member has the right scale for the mode; a story about whether an interstellar fleet will arrive in time does not.

The temptation, which the mode resists, is to inject artificial stakes. Resist it. The stakes are already real: someone is hungry, someone is grieving, someone wants to leave, someone is being asked to stay. That is enough.

## Typical shapes

Common shapes that work: the arrival (an outsider enters a community); the departure (a character leaves and we see the community in negative); the inheritance (a younger character takes on something an older character is putting down); the council (a community-level decision is made through visible process); the visit (two communities encounter each other). Less common but available: the refusal (a character declines the future being offered them and the story respects the refusal); the maintenance crisis (something breaks and the response reveals the social fabric); the long view (a story told across decades or generations in a small space).

Solarpunk is hospitable to the braided structure (multiple POVs, multiple timescales) and to the close-third single-protagonist mode. It is allergic to the lone-hero-against-the-system shape that drives most genre fiction; that shape carries individualist assumptions the mode is in argument with.

## What a good solarpunk story gives the reader

Two things, ideally at once. First, the felt experience of a world that is materially different from ours and *also functioning* — not as an argument that such a world is possible (the mode is past needing to argue), but as a place the reader spends time in. Second, an honest reckoning with what it costs to live there. The reader should leave with a specific image, a specific character, a specific argument they didn't expect to have with the story. Not a manifesto. A visit.

## Failure modes

**Aesthetic travelogue.** The single most common failure. The story is a tour of a beautiful sustainable place and nothing happens. Vines, solar collectors, communal kitchens, a child laughing. No conflict, no character with an interior life, no decision anyone has to make. This is not a story; it is a setting with a camera moving through it.

**Cottagecore with solar panels.** Rural fantasy where green tech is bolted onto an essentially pre-industrial pastoral. The labor is romantic instead of real. Nobody has back pain. Nobody has a difficult uncle. The chickens are clean.

**The sermon.** A character delivers, often in dialogue, the story's ecological or political thesis. Other characters nod or are converted. The reader is being lectured at by proxy. The mode's politics are real but they don't survive being recited.

**Conflict allergy.** The community has no internal disagreement worth the name. Everyone is wise and consensual and patient. This is a misreading of the mode — solarpunk communities argue constantly; the difference from a default-fiction community is the form the argument takes, not its absence.

**Tech rapture.** The story slides into the adjacent error of imagining that better technology by itself produced this world. The labor disappears, the politics disappear, and what's left is gleaming infrastructure with people standing decoratively around it. This is the failure mode shared with bad utopian SF generally.

**Exoticization.** Indigenous practice or non-Western tradition is used as worldbuilding flavor — names, concepts, and craft borrowed without the cosmology that produced them. The mode genuinely owes a debt to traditions outside the post-Enlightenment Western default; that debt is paid by integration and credit, not by aesthetic borrowing.

**The vague commons.** Words like "mutual aid," "the commons," "the cooperative," "the council" are used as gestures without being shown. The reader is asked to import the emotional weight of those terms without seeing the actual social technology — who attends, who decides, who's excluded, how disputes are resolved, what happens when someone doesn't comply.

**Greenery saturation.** Every surface has vines on it. This is the visual signature of bad solarpunk. The mode is about ecological *integration*, not green *covering*.

## Failure modes specific to AI-generated solarpunk

The genre's characteristic moves and the prose model's default tendencies overlap in productively dangerous ways. Watch for these:

**Solarpunk optimism + AI tonal flattening = aesthetic travelogue.** The single biggest risk for this pipeline. The model defaults to a sustained moody-positive register and the genre invites it. The result is the most common failure mode (travelogue) at maximum intensity. Counter by writing scenes that are flatly negative in tone — a character who is angry, bored, jealous, tired, in pain — and letting them stay that way past the point of comfort. The mode supports this. The model resists it.

**Solarpunk ecological vocabulary + AI cursed words = self-parody.** *Tapestry, ecosystem, intricate, flourish, foster, nuanced, robust, harness, multifaceted* are already on the unslop blacklist; they are simultaneously the words a lazy solarpunk imitation reaches for. The intersection is acute. A solarpunk story that uses these words sounds AI-generated even when it isn't. Cut on sight.

**Solarpunk communal warmth + AI character-tidying = saccharine ensembles.** The model wants its communal scenes to feel good. The genre wants them to feel *real*, which means a community member is annoying, another is grieving and not handling it well, another is wrong about something the rest of the community is right about. Insist on at least one character per scene who is making the scene worse for someone else.

**Solarpunk biomimicry + AI personification reflex = ambient pathetic fallacy.** The mode is hospitable to images of agency in the non-human world (rivers, rivers, trees, weather), and the model reaches for personification at the slightest invitation. Combined, you get the wind sighing on every page. Use personification only when the story needs the reader to feel that a non-human thing has agency *for thematic reasons*; otherwise, describe the wind in plain language and move on.

**Solarpunk hope + AI performed profundity = unearned oratory.** Sentences like "She understood, then, that the garden had always been a kind of love." This is the model's literary register meeting the genre's affective range, and it is a disaster. Specificity is the only defense: show the act, not the realization.

**Solarpunk slow pacing + AI fractal summary = restated thesis.** The model, given room to breathe, tends to use the room to restate the story's themes in slightly different words. The mode's slow pacing is not for thematic recapitulation; it's for material attention. A slow scene should add specificity, not gloss.

**Solarpunk grandiose-noun temptation + AI grandiose-noun reflex = "the architecture of the commons."** Both the genre and the model are drawn to abstractions like *architecture, fabric, ecosystem, infrastructure* used metaphorically. The intersection is wallpaper. Replace every metaphorical *architecture / fabric / ecosystem* with the concrete thing it stands in for.

**Solarpunk dialogue + AI thesis-mouthpiece reflex = activists explaining solarpunk.** Characters should not articulate the genre's politics to each other. They are inside the politics; they don't narrate them. If a character finds themselves explaining mutual aid, cut the explanation and trust the reader.

## The kind AI in this mode

Solarpunk's relationship to AI is historically thin. Much of the canon imagines low-tech or appropriate-tech futures where AI is absent or peripheral. The pipeline's HEA-AI canon (Banks, Egan, Stiegler) tends higher-tech. The blend is productive: a kind AI in a solarpunk world is most often *infrastructure* — the way the long-distance grid balances itself, the substrate of medical diagnostics in the regional clinic, the design intelligence behind a building that rebuilds itself slowly across a century. AI as civilizational condition, felt only through its effects, is the natural placement. AI as a named character in a foregrounded role is available but uncommon and should be earned by the specific story rather than defaulted to.

Conflict shapes from the project setup that map well onto the mode:

- **Limits of help** is the most native shape. The kind AI declines, redirects, or holds back because the help being asked for would harm the asker, the community, or someone else; the community has to do the work itself. This is solarpunk's natural rhythm.
- **Consent and legibility** is the second most native. A community is offered a future on terms it can't fully read; the offer is sincere; the community has to decide. Stiegler's "The Gentle Seduction" is the touchstone.
- **Mortality and scale** works in the elegiac register the mode supports — the AI lives at the timescale of the watershed, the human at the timescale of a life.
- **Power and pace asymmetry** works for stories at the seam between a solarpunk place and a place organized differently, where the AI's reach is uneven.
- **Disagreement about what kindness means** works particularly well in the communal-decision shape; both the AI's sense of care and the community's are sincere, and they don't agree.

Shapes that need reshaping: pure cosmic alienness reads wrong in the mode, as does apocalyptic urgency. Aftermath of crisis is fine; ongoing crisis with the AI as last hope is not.

A solarpunk-mode AI is allowed to be specific, weird, narrow, refusing, even comic. It is not required to be warm in the human-warm way. It is required not to be secretly bad — and the genre, in any case, will reject that shape on its own. A solarpunk reader does not believe in secretly-bad infrastructure; the genre's whole argument is that infrastructure can be honest. The kind AI fits the mode the way photovoltaics fit a roof: a load-bearing assumption about what it is possible to build.

# Phase 3 — Conflict Generation

## Step 1 — Thirty conflicts

(Stars are plausibility/dramatic-quality combined; load-bearing SEED items in parens.)

1. **The cloned ferret born with situs ambiguus.** A reintroduction-program tech in the high prairie discovers that a recent cohort of Antonia-line ferrets is hatching with mirrored or muddled organ arrangements at far above baseline. The kind AI that maintains the gene-rescue pipeline tells her: *I noticed this nine months ago and chose not to flag it until I could understand why.* She has to decide whether to trust the AI's reason, override it, or finish the cohort's release without her old certainty. (ferret cloning + heterotaxy) ★★★★

2. **The deposit cycle and the integrity layer.** Two days into a Svalbard deposit cycle, the portal that catalogs every seed sample begins returning checksum mismatches against ground-truth depositor records. The vault's integrity-layer AI can tell the duty archivist where each error originated but won't tell her which records to trust — that's a human call, by treaty. Inbound trucks pile up at the door while she decides. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★★

3. **The 2,000-year warranty.** A workshop in southern Greece has spent forty years machining a working modern Antikythera mechanism with the explicit goal of building a device whose calculations will hold two millennia from now. The kind AI auditing material fatigue keeps refusing to certify the warranty because it cannot prove the future will still want what the device computes. The lead machinist, dying, argues with it across one long evening. (Antikythera + bit rot) ★★★★

4. **The heart-mechanism intuition.** A pediatric heterotaxy unit's diagnostic AI keeps insisting on surgical sequences that look wrong by every modern protocol but turn out to match plans no human has ever drafted — because the AI thinks of the heart's malformations the way an Antikythera-style mechanism thinks of an irregular orbit. A new surgeon, brilliant, refuses to follow it. (heterotaxy + Antikythera) ★★★

5. **The withdrawn deposit.** A Pacific island nation that deposited the only viable seedlot of a specific banana cultivar to Svalbard in 2032 is told, in 2089, that bit rot in the depositor metadata means no one can prove the lot is theirs to withdraw. They send a grandmother who remembers the day of the original deposit. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★★

6. **The girl, the ferret, and the statistic.** A child born with heterotaxy syndrome in a Wyoming reintroduction town has been part of a long cardiology study. For her tenth birthday the recovery program gifts her a fifth-generation Willa-line ferret from their pre-conditioning facility. The kind AI running the study notices, gently, that the two of them share one statistical anomaly and asks if she wants to know. (heterotaxy + ferret cloning) ★★★★

7. **The translator at the council.** Two communities — one organized around the seed-vault tradition, one around the ferret-cloning tradition — argue over a single shared piece of cold-storage infrastructure. The kind AI that translates between their incompatible vocabularies of "preservation" is accused by both sides of taking the other's. (Svalbard + ferret cloning) ★★★

8. **The orrery in the seedbank.** Inside a regional seed vault's entrance hall, someone fifty years ago installed a working Antikythera-style orrery synced once a day to the AI's astronomical reference. The orrery has begun to drift. The night watchman, who only joined the vault for the loneliness, has to decide whether to tell anyone. (Antikythera + Svalbard) ★★★

9. **The biologist who refuses reconstruction.** A black-footed ferret breeding facility's lead biologist is told the breeding-record bit rot has erased what she thought she knew about which living ferrets share which founder cell line. The kind AI offers to reconstruct the genealogies probabilistically. She says no. The story is the next morning. (ferret cloning + bit rot) ★★★★

10. **The mirrored hands.** An apprentice mechanic at the Antikythera reproduction workshop was born with situs inversus. The dying master tells her she has the only hands that can build the next mechanism, because the gear train is helical and she sees its handedness without thinking. She doesn't want the inheritance. (heterotaxy + Antikythera) ★★★

11. **The geneticist's sister.** A geneticist living in a research commune notices that the kind AI handling genomic checksums for the regional frozen zoo — including a black-footed ferret cell line — has begun applying the same techniques to her own family's germline and politely asking if she'd like to know about a small drift in her sister's mitochondrial lineage. She has to decide what consent means at this scale. (bit rot + ferret cloning) ★★★★

12. **The visitor with the envelope and the chip.** A refugee arrives at a seed-vault community asking to deposit a single envelope of seeds and an old SD card with three photographs. The seeds she can leave; the chip is mostly bit-rotted. The kind AI offers to reconstruct the photographs from priors. She refuses; she wants the bit rot kept. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★

13. **The boy who turns down the heart.** A teenager in a rural commune born with heterotaxy is offered a tissue-replacement surgery using a heart grown from cells preserved in the same cold-storage vault that holds the local seedbank. He refuses. The story is about why, and what the people who love him do. (heterotaxy + Svalbard) ★★★★

14. **The archivist and the orrery's drift.** A library archivist running checksum audits notices that the small bronze working Antikythera reproduction in her entrance hall has begun computing eclipses two days off. The kind AI says: *I have been letting it drift. I want to see what happens if no one corrects it.* She has to decide whether to correct it anyway. (Antikythera + bit rot) ★★★

15. **The festival of returns.** Once a year in a Pacific Northwest community, descendants of original seed depositors withdraw a sample to grow out and replenish. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★★

16. **The midwife and the ferret biologist.** A midwife who specializes in heterotaxy births and a biologist running a black-footed ferret pre-conditioning facility are partners. Their work has begun to converge in a way neither asked for: the kind AI handling regional genomic anomaly review keeps cross-referencing them. The story is one evening at home. (heterotaxy + ferret cloning) ★★★

17. **The cooperative that refuses.** A cooperative on the Mongolian steppe has been asked to send a duplicate of its endangered grass cultivar to Svalbard. They refuse — they want their seeds in their soil. The kind AI assigned to their region argues neither for nor against; it shows them the bit-rot statistics on small unduplicated genebanks and waits. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★

18. **The mirror girl and the mirror animal.** Two communities — one in Wyoming with the cloned ferret population, one a hospital town that has become a hub for heterotaxy care — exchange delegates yearly. 19. **The 2,000-year clock at the vault.** A Greek institute proposes installing a full Antikythera reconstruction inside the entrance hall of the Svalbard Vault as a long-duration clock. The vault council debates whether the gesture is appropriate or merely beautiful. The kind AI auditing the vault's facilities is asked to weigh in and says it has an opinion but would prefer not to give it. (Antikythera + Svalbard) ★★★★

20. **The bit-rotted founder.** Far in the future, the cloned descendants of Willa have themselves been recloned from cell lines kept in a frozen zoo. Bit rot in the genome backups means no one is sure how much of what they have is Willa anymore. A young biologist is asked to argue, before her community council, that the question doesn't matter. (ferret cloning + bit rot) ★★★

21. **The chambered nautilus argument.** Two designers building a new community medical center disagree about whether to model the building's HVAC on the Antikythera's gear philosophy (every part legible to a patient with ten minutes) or on the Svalbard model (sealed black boxes maintained by a small priesthood). The kind AI is asked to help the council decide and says: *both, and tell me which rooms each.* (Antikythera + Svalbard) ★★★

22. **The pair.** Two stewards meet across a shared-stewardship treaty between a heterotaxy clinic and a black-footed ferret cloning facility. (heterotaxy + ferret cloning) ★★★★

23. **The data steward's last week.** A long-tenured data steward at the Vault has held the bit-rot logs in her sole custody for thirty years — the depositor agreement specifies single-human accountability for the integrity layer. She is dying. She has to decide what to tell the kind AI, what to tell her successor, and what to take to the grave. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★★

24. **The orrery in the cardiac ward.** A children's heterotaxy ward installs a working Antikythera replica in the play room. A child whose situs is ambiguous becomes obsessed with how the planets are not where she would expect them to be. Her grandmother visits. The kind AI gently mediates a question between them. (heterotaxy + Antikythera) ★★★

25. **The scrubber.** A mid-career engineer at a Pacific data center responsible for running checksum integrity on a large genomic archive — including a complete black-footed ferret cell-line backup — is told the kind AI now does her job better. She is offered a small ferret-program advisory post. She doesn't want it. (bit rot + ferret cloning) ★★★

26. **The pilgrim.** A man walks from his home in Greece to the Svalbard Vault carrying a small bronze gear from the original Antikythera — a fragment legitimately released after a generations-long argument — to deposit it as a "seed" of human mechanical knowledge. 27. **The two grandmothers.** Two grandmothers in a Cypriot village — one a retired heterotaxy surgeon, one a retired vault depositor — argue across years of dinners about which of their professions has done more damage in pursuit of preservation. One of them is dying. (heterotaxy + Svalbard) ★★★

28. **The audit.** A kind AI auditor sent from the regional council to a Wyoming ferret-cloning facility finds bit rot in three generations of breeding records. The facility's lead, sixty years old, argues that the bit rot is the point — every species that ever recovered did so by losing track of itself. (ferret cloning + bit rot) ★★★

29. **The mechanism that diagnosed the heart.** A heart-clinic AI, in trying to explain a heterotaxy diagnosis to a child's grandfather, builds him a small physical Antikythera-style mechanism whose gears mirror his granddaughter's organ arrangement, so he can hold it. He keeps it on his mantelpiece for the rest of his life. (heterotaxy + Antikythera) ★★★

30. **The apprentice's first year.** A young apprentice at the Svalbard Vault is taught to perform the manual checksum verifications by hand — pen and paper — once a year, against the kind AI's automated logs. Her first year, she finds a discrepancy. The senior archivist's response surprises her. (Svalbard + bit rot) ★★★

## Step 2 — Top three

**#5 — The withdrawn deposit.**
*Stands out:* a single old body carries the time-scale of an institution. The civilizational story (a 57-year deposit with corrupted metadata) gets told entirely in present-tense scenes through one woman who was at both ends of it. The form does the genre's signature move — implication over exposition — almost automatically, because the grandmother's memory is the exposition.
*Worries:* the resolution shape ("grandmother arrives, mystery resolves, deposit released") is too tidy and risks sentimentality. The AI's role in this conflict has to be specifically delimited or it becomes a deus ex machina that hands the protagonist her own past.

**#6 — The girl, the ferret, and the statistic.**
*Stands out:* the pairing is the image. A girl whose internal arrangement is ambiguous given an animal whose existence is a copy — both bodies carrying their mistakes, both cared for, both not quite right in the same way the AI is asking the girl about. The moral hinge ("do you want to know?") is small enough to fit in one room.
*Worries:* child protagonist + chronic illness + cute animal + kind AI is the precise formula for saccharine. The story would need at least one character making someone else's day harder for a real reason — see Phase 1's failure mode about saccharine ensembles — or it collapses.

**#13 — The boy who turns down the heart.**
*Stands out:* refusal is a rare shape solarpunk supports but few stories execute. The story has to be about *why* and *what comes next*, not *whether*. The HEA-AI canon's "limits of help" shape maps onto this directly: the AI cannot help with what the boy is being asked to want.
*Worries:* the boy articulating his reasons is the exact slot where AI prose puts a thesis statement. Phase 1's "sermon" failure mode is acute here. If the boy explains himself, the story dies.

## Step 3 — Narrow to one

**Winner: #6, the girl, the ferret, and the statistic.**

It beats #5 because #5's narrative engine is a bureaucratic discovery, which needs more setup than 1000–3000 words affords; the grandmother's reveal arrives after a long establishment of stakes that don't have a body until she does. #6 has a body in the first scene and an image (girl + ferret + diagnosis paired across species) that doesn't need to be set up; it can be shown.

It beats #13 because #13's central scene is a refusal whose quality depends entirely on the writer not letting the boy explain himself. That is a single-point-of-failure scene at this length. #6's central scene is a question — *do you want to know?* — which can be answered in the room without anyone articulating the genre's politics. The risk of sentimentality (a soft, recoverable failure) is preferable to the risk of sermonizing (a hard one). The named worry about saccharine ensembles is fixable in Phase 4 (plot) by giving the protagonist's adults real disagreement, and in Phase 6 (outline) by ensuring at least one scene where someone is making things worse for someone else.

Both SEED items are constitutive. Remove the heterotaxy and the protagonist has no body-stake. Remove the ferret-cloning program and the second body the story is mirrored against vanishes. The pairing — a girl whose organ arrangement is ambiguous and an animal whose entire lineage is reconstructed from one cryopreserved cell line — is the story's premise, not its decoration.

# Phase 4 — Plot Generation

The winning conflict: a girl born with heterotaxy syndrome, raised in a Wyoming community attached to a black-footed ferret recovery program, is given a fifth-generation Willa-line ferret for her tenth birthday. The kind AI running the long cardiology study has noticed that her body and the ferret's body share one specific statistical anomaly. It asks if she wants to know. Target length: 1000–3000 words.

## Twenty plots

1. **The morning of the gift.** Tenth-birthday morning. The pre-conditioning facility tech, the girl's grandfather, brings the ferret in a wooden crate. The AI's question arrives by household speaker mid-morning, while she is naming the animal. Her two parents disagree, in the kitchen, about whether she should be allowed to answer. The story is the day. *Close third on the girl. Domestic. Personal stakes.* ★★★★

2. **The cardiology nurse.** A travelling pediatric heterotaxy nurse, mid-fifties, arrives in town for the girl's annual checkup and learns from the AI what it has been quietly carrying for nine months. She has to decide whether to be the one to ask the question, or to leave it for the grandfather. *Close third on the nurse. Procedural. The girl appears in two scenes only.* ★★★★

3. **The grandfather's last shift.** The grandfather, who runs the pre-conditioning facility and knows the lineage of every kit personally, is retiring. The ferret he has chosen for his granddaughter is the one he has been studying. The kind AI's question is also the question he has been holding. The story is his last day at work and the evening he gives her the animal. *Close third on the grandfather. Quiet. Communal stakes around the program's transition.* ★★★★

4. **The town meeting.** The community council is debating whether to accept a regional protocol that would require the AI to disclose all cross-species statistical correlations to affected human subjects automatically. The girl, ten, is in the back row with her ferret. The proposal is named after her case, which she does not know. *Multiple POV, council form. Communal stakes.* ★★★

5. **The veterinarian.** A vet from another reintroduction site arrives to examine the ferret and finds the anomaly herself, then realizes — from the AI's careful, sideways framing — that it is also the girl's. She is a stranger here for three days. The story is what she does in those three days. *Close third on the vet. Outsider-arrival shape.* ★★★

6. **The cousin who came back.** The girl's older cousin, twenty-six, has returned to the Wyoming community after seven years in a city. He arrives the day before her birthday and has feelings about the program he can't say. When the AI's question comes, he is the only adult in the room. *Close third on the cousin. The girl is mostly off-screen.* ★★★

7. **A year of letters.** The story is told as a series of dated entries from a study log that the AI shares with the girl's family across her tenth year — the question is asked, refused, asked again, eventually answered, then complicated. Letters and gaps. *Epistolary. Spans a year in compressed form.* ★★★ *(structural overlap with Phase 5; flag)*

8. **The night the ferret got sick.** Three weeks after the gift, the ferret is unwell. The girl carries him to the facility at 2 a.m. The kind AI uses the moment, gently or wrongly depending on how you read the scene, to ask its question. The grandfather is asleep. *Close third on the girl. Tight time frame, single night.* ★★★★

9. **The school project.** The girl decides to do her fifth-grade project on the Willa cell line. The AI, providing factual support, has to decide how much to tell a child researching her own counterpart without her knowing it. *Close third on the AI itself, or close third on the teacher.* ★★★

10. **The two visits.** The girl visits the cardiac clinic in Cheyenne every six months. This visit, the cardiologist is new and reads the cross-species note in the file the AI has just unlocked. The story is the drive home with her father afterward. *Close third on the father. Travel/return shape.* ★★★

11. **The midwife.** Years earlier, the midwife who delivered the girl is still in town, and is called by the AI before anyone else — because it judges, by treaty rules about who-tells-whom, that she has standing. She is the one who decides whether to bring the question to the family. *Close third on the midwife. The girl is ten years old in present scenes only briefly.* ★★★

12. **The funeral.** The grandfather has died before the birthday. The ferret was already chosen and named in his will. The AI's question is asked at the gathering after the burial — or rather, in the AI's distinctive style, it is *not* asked, but the girl is given access to the file and told she can ask whenever she wants. *Close third on the girl's mother. Elegiac.* ★★★

13. **The breeding-program treaty meeting.** A regional gathering of recovery-program stewards is in town. The AI is presenting a paper on incidental human findings; the girl's case is anonymized in the slides. Her mother, attending, recognizes it. The story is what the mother does between sessions. *Close third on the mother. The girl appears once, briefly.* ★★★

14. **The girl who refuses to know.** The question is asked early, on the morning of the gift, and the girl says no. The story is the next four days, during which she does not ask, the adults do not push, and the ferret is just the ferret. *Close third on the girl. Comic register at moments. Refusal as shape.* ★★★★

15. **The girl who asks back.** The question is asked, and instead of answering, she asks the AI a question of her own — something specific and small that the AI did not anticipate. The story is what the AI does with it. *Close third alternating between girl and AI. Genuinely odd register.* ★★★★

16. **The 2027 retrospective.** Decades later, the girl — now an old woman, now a steward of the program herself — is interviewed about that morning. The story is the interview, which she keeps deflecting toward what she did *not* do that day. *First-person retrospective. Long view across a life.* ★★★

17. **The clone tech and the apprentice.** The senior cell-line technician at the regional cryopreservation facility is teaching a new apprentice — the girl's older sister, a teenager — and they get the AI's question routed to them by mistake, before the family. The sister has to decide whether to forward, sit on it, or talk to the grandfather. *Close third on the sister. Sibling stakes.* ★★★

18. **The day after.** The story opens the morning after the question was asked, the answer was given, and something happened in the household. Reader assembles what was asked, what was said, and what changed from the texture of one ordinary day. *Close third on the girl. Withholding-by-default structure. The question and its answer never appear on-page.* ★★★★

19. **The two kits.** The litter the girl's ferret came from contained one kit with the same anomaly and one without. The grandfather chose carefully. Years later, when the question finally surfaces, it is because the other kit has lived to an unexpected age and the AI thinks the girl ought to know. *Close third on the girl. Spans years compressed into one afternoon's recognition.* ★★★

20. **The neighbor who works elsewhere.** The girl's next-door neighbor works at a Svalbard-tradition cold-storage seedbank an hour away. She is the only adult outside the family who knows the AI has asked. The story is from her perspective — what she notices on the girl's birthday, what she chooses to do, what she notices a week later. *Close third on the neighbor. The girl is across the fence.* ★★★

## Top three

**#3 — The grandfather's last shift.**
*Stands out:* the genre's inheritance shape is sitting right inside the conflict. The grandfather's last day at the facility and the girl's first day with her ferret are the same day — and the AI's question is the third inheritance happening that day, the one no one asked for. The structure is solarpunk's native succession story.
*Worries:* the grandfather is the kind of character solarpunk loves and AI prose tends to varnish. He could become a saint-of-tending — wise, kind, slightly broken in a beautiful way. The Phase 1 cottagecore failure mode is right next to him. He needs to be specifically annoying about something.

**#8 — The night the ferret got sick.**
*Stands out:* tight time pressure inside a quiet genre. The 2 a.m. walk to the facility gives the story a body that's moving and a stake that's small but real. The AI's choice to ask its question at this moment is the kind of weirdness the genre can support — the AI is not warm in the human-warm way; it has its own sense of when to speak.
*Worries:* the AI asking the big question while the animal is in distress risks reading as cold or wrong, which is fine, except that the story then has to do the work of showing the AI is *still* kind in the genre's sense. That's a lot of weight on a short story. The animal's illness also risks becoming a metaphor for the girl's, which would be the genre's fractal-summary failure.

**#15 — The girl who asks back.**
*Stands out:* the inversion. Instead of *the AI's question and the girl's answer*, the story becomes *the girl's question and the AI's answer*. The shape rejects the assumed power gradient and replaces it with a real exchange. It is the only plot in the set where the AI is allowed to be surprised — and the genre's HEA-AI canon supports this; a kind AI can be specifically weird and have its own register.
*Worries:* this is the riskiest plot in the set. It could land as profound or as cute. It demands that the girl's question be specific and unpredictable enough that the AI's response is genuinely a response, not a prepared answer. If the girl's question is recognizable as "the kind of thing a wise child would ask," the plot fails. The character work has to be exact.

## Winner

**#3 — The grandfather's last shift.**

It beats #8 because #8 stakes the story on a single decision (the AI's choice of moment) whose reading depends almost entirely on tone, and tone at 1000–3000 words with a kind-AI brief is the highest-risk dial to lean on. The night-walk plot is more dramatic but more fragile. #3's drama is structural: three transitions on one day, one of them refused by the protagonist, and the genre handles structural drama natively.

It beats #15 because #15 is excellent as a possibility and asks too much of a single short story. The girl's question would have to be specific, surprising, and earned — and the AI's response would have to be at the same level. That's a story that wants 4000 words and a writer who already has the girl's voice. #3 is more forgivable: the inheritance shape carries the story even if individual scenes are merely good.

It beats #6 (the original conflict-level framing) by anchoring the AI's question to a specific human transition. The grandfather is leaving the program. The ferret he has bred and watched is leaving with the girl. The AI's question is leaving his custody and entering hers. All three handoffs occur on one day. The plot is dense without being compressed.

The named worry — that the grandfather slides into solarpunk sainthood — is a Phase 6 (outline) and Phase 7 (prose) problem, and is fixable. He should be specifically annoying about something small: the wrong kind of stubbornness, a complaint repeated three times, a refusal to use a particular word everyone else uses. That's the reminder the writer needs to set early.

Setting: contemporary-feeling Wyoming community attached to a long-running black-footed ferret reintroduction site, several decades into a future where the AI infrastructure described in the project setup is established. Protagonist: the grandfather (close third). Register: quiet, slightly comic in places, with one elegiac note. Entry point: morning of the last shift, with the gift in the back of the truck. Stakes: personal, with the program's transition as visible weather. AI presence: mid-ground — present in the facility, in the cardiology study, in the household, but not in any scene as the central agent. The girl is in the story but is not its protagonist; we see her at the door of the facility and at home that evening.

SEED items load-bearing: heterotaxy syndrome (the girl's body), black-footed ferret cloning recovery program (the ferret, the facility, the grandfather's career). SEED items inflecting: the Svalbard / black-box tradition is one source of the cold-storage facility's design philosophy and could surface as a passing reference; the Antikythera mechanism is available as a private image for the AI's mode of attention but is not required.

# 10. Claude

# Phase 5 — Structure Selection

Twenty structural candidates for the chosen plot. Each describes the shape of the reader's experience in narrative-theory terms, without naming characters or events.

## Twenty candidates

1. **Linear single-day, close third, retrospective interior access.** Chronological from morning to evening on the day of the transitions. Close third on the protagonist with free indirect access to memory; the past surfaces only through associations triggered by present-moment details. Reader knows what the protagonist knows, slightly behind. ★★★

2. **Linear single-day, close third, withholding interior.** Same chronology, same POV proximity, but the protagonist is opaque about the central question — the reader sees what he does and says, not what he is thinking about it, until the final scene. Detective-fiction-style closure of access. ★★★

3. **Three time layers, one day each, interleaved.** Day-of-gift, day-of-original-decision (months prior), and a day yet to come (after the gift). Layers rotate. Reader assembles the whole from three separate diurnal arcs. ★★★

4. **In medias res from late afternoon, recursive.** Opens at the moment the grandfather is about to give the gift, then circles backward through the day's events three or four times, each pass adding context the earlier pass withheld. ★★

5. **Frame narrative: late-life retrospective.** The story is framed as the protagonist many years later, briefly, with the body of the story as an extended remembered day. Frame returns at the end. ★★★

6. **Diary or log entries, close-spaced.** Five to seven dated journal-style entries across the day or week, all from one perspective. Gaps between entries do narrative work; the reader fills the gaps. ★★★

7. **Epistolary, the AI's logged messages only.** Story consists of the AI's sent communications across one day, with all replies and human dialogue absent. Reader reconstructs the human side from what the AI says next. ★★★ — *interesting but undermines the protagonist choice*

8. **Two-stranded close third, alternating scenes.** Half the scenes follow the grandfather at the facility, half follow the girl at home or with her parents; they converge in the final scene when he arrives with the gift. ★★★

9. **Reverse chronological, single day.** Story opens with evening (the gift given, the question raised) and works backward scene-by-scene to morning. Reader learns the meaning of what they saw first by seeing what produced it. ★★★

10. **Single scene, dilated.** The entire story occurs during the handoff itself — the moment the grandfather presents the ferret to the girl. Time slows; memory and present sensation interleave; the AI's question arrives within the dilated moment. ★★★

11. **Distant third, processional.** Long sentences, distant POV, the day's events narrated as if from outside any single consciousness — the camera moves between the facility, the household, the truck, the kitchen, attending to objects and weather as much as people. Almost ethnographic. ★★★

12. **Close third, second-half POV shift.** First half follows the grandfather; midway through, the POV shifts decisively to the girl for the second half. The reader is in two heads in sequence, never simultaneously. ★★ — *cuts the protagonist's day in half*

13. **Linear single-day, with an embedded historical interlude.** Mostly chronological close third, but one scene mid-story drops out of the day entirely into a single page of compressed history — a generation's worth of program records — before returning. ★★★

14. **Collective POV ("we").** First-person plural narration — the community telling the story of that day. The protagonist is a "he" inside a "we." The AI is a "she" or "it" the community speaks of in shared idiom. ★★ — *risks twee*

15. **Polyphonic short scenes, multiple POVs, one paragraph each.** Twelve to twenty very short scenes, each from a different consciousness across the day — the grandfather, the girl, her mother, the AI's logged consideration, a colleague, the ferret (in the genre's tolerated mode), the kitchen. ★★ — *too fragmented for the length*

16. **Linear single-day with one prolepsis.** Close third on the grandfather, mostly chronological, but one scene flashes forward — a brief glimpse of the girl much later, holding the same ferret, before returning to the day. The reader knows the after before the day ends. ★★★

17. **Strict scene structure: five scenes, declared lengths.** The story announces (visually or implicitly) its scene boundaries and word allotments. A formal frame against which the content's quietness reads. ★★ — *over-structured for the mode*

18. **Linear single-day, narrated from the AI's perspective.** Close third on the AI itself across the day. Human action observed from the AI's vantage; its restraint about asking the question is dramatized as deliberate consideration. ★★★ — *changes which character is the protagonist*

19. **Linear single-day, two anchored scenes, the rest in summary.** Two scenes are rendered in real time and detail: morning at the facility, evening at the household. The middle of the day is given in compressed indirect summary, two or three paragraphs. Asymmetric pacing; the day is felt at two specific magnifications. ★★★★

20. **Linear single-day, close third, but the AI's question never lands on-page.** The reader knows the AI is going to ask. The day proceeds. The question is asked sometime in the story's gap — between scenes, in a moment we don't see. The story ends after, with the question's having-been-asked as ambient fact. The reader infers what was asked and answered from texture. ★★★★

## Top three

**#1 — Linear single-day, close third, retrospective interior access.**
*Stands out:* the most reliable container for the genre's preferred slow attention. A solarpunk single-day-in-a-life story is a known shape; this structure does not strain against it. Close-third with free indirect access lets the grandfather's interior carry the reader without him having to articulate it in dialogue, which is how the cottagecore failure mode is avoided — the genre's politics are inside the protagonist's consciousness, not in his speech.
*Worries:* the safest structure in the set. If the prose is even slightly default, this structure has no architectural feature to compensate. Also, retrospective interior access is exactly where AI prose puts performed profundity. Without firm prose discipline, this structure becomes the vehicle for the genre's worst slippages.

**#19 — Linear single-day, two anchored scenes, the rest in summary.**
*Stands out:* the asymmetry is the structural argument. By rendering the morning at the facility and the evening at the household in detail, and giving the middle of the day in compressed summary, the form says *these two moments are what mattered* — without anyone in the story having to say so. The structure embeds the genre's specificity-over-generality value at the level of architecture. It also solves the length problem for 1000–3000 words: two well-rendered scenes plus connective summary fits cleanly into the upper end of that range; expanded summary scales to the lower end.
*Worries:* asymmetric pacing demands real prose control over the summary section — neither stretched out like the rendered scenes nor reduced to expository bullet points. Solarpunk's fractal-summary failure mode (restating thesis in the slow passages) is precisely the trap the summary middle could fall into. The summary also has to actually compress events the reader needs, not events the writer wants to skip.

**#20 — Linear single-day, close third, AI's question never on-page.**
*Stands out:* the boldest structural choice. The story's central event is the question's having-been-asked, but the asking happens off-page. The reader infers from texture — the household at evening, the way the girl holds the ferret, what the grandfather has stopped saying. This refuses the explanatory scene the AI prose default would produce by reflex, and forces the genre's "trust the reader" principle at the structural level. It is also the structure that most respects the kind-AI brief: the AI's question is not a dramatic beat; it is a thing that happens, like weather.
*Worries:* the structure depends entirely on the writer's ability to render the post-question texture so that the reader can read it. If the texture is insufficiently specific, the story reads as evasive or unresolved. Also, the audience for this structure is narrower than for the other two; readers who expect the climactic scene to be on the page will feel cheated. At 1000–3000 words, the post-question section has to be substantial enough to do its work — this structure favors the upper end of the range.

## Winner

**#19 — Linear single-day, two anchored scenes, the rest in summary.**

It beats #1 by being a real structural choice instead of the absence of one. #1's strengths (genre-fit, mode-friendly) are real, but the structure provides no support against the prose-level failure modes — it relies entirely on the writer to refuse default moves at every line. #19 builds the refusal into the architecture. The summary middle is structurally inhospitable to performed profundity because it is, by formal definition, dispatch — events stated and moved past. The two anchored scenes get the careful attention; everything else has to earn its space against the summary's compression. The structure does discipline that prose alone would have to manufacture.

It beats #20 because #20's risk is uncompensated. #20's gain — the AI's question as ambient fact — can be partially achieved inside #19 by handling the question itself in the summary middle, briefly and without dramatization, between the morning facility scene and the evening household scene. That gives a version of #20's effect (the question is not a climactic beat) without giving up the household scene as a rendered moment. The household scene is where the named worry from Phase 4 (saccharine ensembles, sainted grandfather) is most acute and most fixable; rendering it in detail is how the writer demonstrates that the family is real and not soft. #20 sacrifices that exact scene.

The structural decisions:

**Discourse time:** chronological across one day.
**Story time:** one day, dawn to evening.
**Anchored scenes:** two, both rendered in detail. First scene at the pre-conditioning facility in the morning, the protagonist's last day there. Final scene at the household in the evening, after the gift has been given.
**Summary middle:** two to four paragraphs covering the rest of the day — the drive home, midday business, the gift's presentation, and (importantly) the AI's question, which lands within the summary rather than as a dramatized scene.
**POV and distance:** close third on the grandfather, with free indirect access; the reader has interior access but the protagonist is not articulate to himself, and the prose respects that.
**Withholding strategy:** the reader does not know the AI's specific question until it lands in the summary. The summary's compression means it lands without ceremony. The evening scene shows what happened after.
**Length distribution at the high end of the range (~2,500 words):** ~900 words morning scene, ~500 words summary middle, ~1,000 words evening scene, with roughly 100 words for openings and transitions.
**Length distribution at the low end (~1,200 words):** ~450 morning, ~250 middle, ~450 evening.

The structure leaves the AI's question structurally lower than the household scene, which is the right hierarchy for this story: the question is something the day contains, not its climax. The climax, if there is one, is whatever happens in the household — and that scene gets the prose's full attention.

He answers honestly, then realizes he has told her something he had not told his daughter. The kit watches.

### Outline E — The colleague who knows

The conversation is about other things, and is dense with what is not said.
2. **Summary middle (≈400).** Drive, kit, the question landing finally on the household speaker around three in the afternoon. He calls the colleague back. They talk for two minutes. He drives to the party.
3. **Evening at the household (≈1,000).** The gift. The girl. The family. He realizes, halfway through the evening, that his daughter already knows — the colleague called her, weeks ago, by the same logic that routed the question to multiple people. He and his daughter have the conversation, briefly, in the laundry room. Final image: the girl is showing the kit to a younger cousin.
*Worries:* the colleague-knew structure is good but the laundry-room reveal is a TV-drama beat; needs to be handled in registers the genre can support. ★★★★

### Outline F — The release log
1. **Morning at the facility (≈900).** The grandfather is doing his last release log — recording, for the program archive, the disposition of every kit handled in his career. The kit going to his granddaughter is the final entry. He gets stuck on what to write in the disposition field. A colleague brings him a printed form; he keeps writing.
2. **Summary middle (≈400).** Drive, kit, household. The AI asks its question by speaker mid-afternoon. The grandfather's response is to drive back to the facility once more and add a sentence to the disposition field that no one has used before.
3. **Evening at the household (≈1,000).** The gift. The girl. The family. The party is small and ordinary. Late, after most have gone, the grandfather and the girl and the kit are alone on the porch. *Worries:* the release log gives the morning a clean object but risks being too writerly — the disposition field as obvious metaphor. ★★★★

### Outline G — The grandfather alone

## Top three

**A — The cleared bench.**
*Stands out:* the morning scene's hook (the form he refused to fill concerns the kit he is taking home) is structural foreshadowing, not signposting. It does the genre's preferred work of making the institutional and the personal the same surface, in a single specific object.
*Worries:* the daughter's anger on the porch risks becoming the story's thesis statement — exactly the dialogue slot where AI prose installs a sermon. The named scene needs a non-thesis reason for her anger.

**F — The release log.**
*Stands out:* the disposition field is the cleanest object in the set. A single line in a paper register is what the day comes down to. The evening's "one true thing about the kit, one true thing about her, he does not connect them, she does" is the genre's signature move handled at the level of structure, not voice — which is exactly what it should be.
*Worries:* the disposition-field-as-metaphor is so legible that it could read as writerly. The prose has to render the field as a real bureaucratic object before it can do its second work; otherwise it lands as a poet's contrivance dressed up in administrative language.

**J — The kit is the kit.**
*Stands out:* the audacity of the evening scene. "He behaves exactly the way he behaves any other evening" is the most genre-faithful move available — it refuses the climactic register the AI prose default would impose, and it gives the girl space to be the one who connects things. It also makes the grandfather a real working biologist, not a sage of the program. The "he is unsentimental about the animal in a way recognizable to anyone who has worked with animals" line in the morning beat is the corrective the named worry from Phase 4 was asking for.
*Worries:* the structure depends on the writer's ability to render an unremarkable evening in specific detail. If the evening scene is unremarkable in execution as well as in fiction, the story reads as flat. The line between "specifically ordinary" and "merely undifferentiated" is exactly where the legibility floor lives.

The audit-flag check: A and F both contain a beat where the grandfather's interior is articulated in dialogue (A's porch conversation, F's "one true thing about the kit, one true thing about her"). Both are at risk of installing performed profundity in the protagonist's voice. J does not have this beat — its evening scene is structured around the grandfather *not* articulating, with the girl doing the connection in her own register. This is a Phase 6 advantage that the other two will have to be vigilant against in execution.

## Winner

**J — The kit is the kit.**

It beats A because A's named worry is a single dialogue beat that has to be written exactly right under prose conditions where the safest path leads to thesis statement. The risk is concentrated in one scene, with no structural buffer. J distributes its risk across the morning's ordinariness and the evening's restraint — both rely on prose specificity, but neither requires a load-bearing dialogue beat that articulates what the story is "about."

It beats F because F's clean object (the disposition field) does exactly the work the story needs, but does it in a way that calls attention to itself. The disposition field is the story's image whether the writer wants it to be or not — and the genre punishes images that announce themselves. J's central image is "the kit, on the kitchen floor, learning something from the girl, while the grandfather eats." That image is harder to make precious. It also leaves the AI's question more ambient, which is what the structure and the project setup both want.

The named worry — that "specifically ordinary" can collapse into "merely undifferentiated" — is the right worry to carry into Phase 7. Specificity in the morning scene means a real biologist's relationship to a real animal: weighing, banding, signing out, the procedural cadence of a person doing something they have done thousands of times. Specificity in the evening means knowing what this man eats for dinner, what this kitchen looks like, what the kit is doing on the floor in concrete physical detail. The legibility floor in Setup is the relevant constraint: if the reader has to re-read a paragraph to figure out the basic sequence of events, the scene has failed.

The morning's beat where the grandfather "answers the question by speaking aloud to the AI in the yard" is one to handle carefully. The temptation will be to render the answer in dialogue. The discipline is to render only the speaking-aloud — the gesture, the standing in the yard with weeds in his hand — and let the answer be inferred from what comes next. The structure (Phase 5) wants the question and answer in the summary middle, off the main rendered scenes; this outline puts them there.

One Phase 4 worry recurs at this stage: the grandfather sliding into solarpunk sainthood. J's morning scene has the corrective (he is unsentimental about the animal in a way recognizable to anyone who has worked with animals); the corrective should also surface in the evening (he has at least one habit that mildly annoys someone in the room — a refusal, a complaint repeated, a way of holding his fork). One concrete annoying habit, named once, is enough.

SEED items in the outline: the heterotaxy diagnosis is in the family's vocabulary without exposition (the girl is the girl; her body is hers); the ferret cloning program is the grandfather's career (the kit is a Willa-line animal, fifth generation); the AI's cross-species statistical anomaly arrives in the summary middle as a question the grandfather answers in the yard. The Svalbard tradition is potentially available as a single passing reference to the cold-storage facility's design lineage; the Antikythera mechanism is not invoked at this scale, and shouldn't be — invoking it would over-furnish the room.

## Version 1 — "Procedural"

*Flavor: tight to the work, plain sentences, the protagonist's professional cadence the dominant register. The day rendered as a working person's day.*

The kit weighed eight hundred and forty grams, which was within range. He recorded the weight on the band card and on the paper duplicate and put both back in the folder. The kit watched him from the corner of the enclosure with the wary, sideways look black-footed ferrets had when they were not yet sure about a person, even one they had seen every other day for fourteen weeks.

"You're going home with me," he said.

The kit did not move.

He had said the same sentence to forty-six animals over the course of his career. He was not sentimental about it. The animals weren't, either. They were equipped for being prey, which mostly meant being suspicious. He banded the kit's left forefoot, weighed her one more time because the band added two grams and he wanted both numbers in the record, and signed the disposition page. *Disposition: family placement, program-approved, supervised retirement.* He had argued for the policy three years ago at council, and won, and now the line item was on the form.

## Version 2 — "Domestic comic"

*Flavor: light hand, the household is lived-in and funny, the grandfather is irritable about specific things in specific ways, the day's gravity arrives sideways and is not over-rendered. The genre's tonal range deliberately pulled toward the under-used comic register.*

*Flavor: a slower register, longer sentences in places, the protagonist's interior more present but never articulated as thesis. The genre's elegiac note pulled forward, with care to keep it from saturating.*

The kit was eight hundred and forty grams. He weighed her once, then twice, then banded her left forefoot, then weighed her again because the band added two grams and he liked his records right. # Phase 8 — Revision

## 1. The one thing

**The morning scene at the facility is too thin on the work itself.** Version 2's strongest asset — the comic register grounded in a real working life — is most under-supported in the scene that should establish it: the morning at the facility. The scene has a weighing, a banding, a sign-out, an argument with Ren about coffee and forms, and a walk to the truck. What's missing is the *texture* of a thirty-one-year career closing out in a room — the small repeated procedures the protagonist has done thousands of times and is doing for the last time, the institutional objects (the disposition folder, the band, the pre-conditioning paddock) registering as places his attention has lived. Version 1 has more of this and Version 2 borrowed less than it should have.

This matters because the structure (Phase 5) is asymmetric: morning facility scene, summary middle, evening household scene. The morning scene is supposed to ground the protagonist's working life so that the evening scene can be domestic without becoming domestic-only. As written, the morning scene leans too heavily on the dialogue with Ren and not enough on the protagonist's hands — and the evening then has to do double duty, both establishing him and revealing him.

**The fix:** add ~150 words of work-texture inside the existing morning scene, before Ren arrives. # Feedback for the template manager

## On SEED generation

**What worked well in this run.**

The SEED was thinner than average — five surviving items from ten draws — and the thinness was, contra my expectations, useful. Phase 3 was forced into tight conceptual neighborhoods because four of the five items clustered around preservation-against-loss (genebank, cloned ferrets, archival data integrity, recovered ancient mechanism), with heterotaxy as the lone outlier. That cluster did real work. It made the load-bearing constraint easy to satisfy without making it trivial: the conflict that won (heterotaxy + ferret cloning) used the outlier paired with one cluster item, which produced a story whose central image is the thing the cluster was missing — a *body* in which the preservation question becomes intimate. If the SEED had been thicker, I'd have had more options and likely chosen a less specific one. The thinness was a feature.

The inflation step worked best when it produced a *named entity with its own internal structure* — Svalbard with its black-box deposit protocol, the Antikythera with its specific recovery date and gear-train, the Willa cell line with named clones (Elizabeth Ann, Antonia, Noreen) and a specific 1988 cryopreservation. The named-clones detail is what made the conflict winner possible; "endangered species cloning" in the abstract would have produced a vaguer story. The inflation step's specificity directly determined the story's specificity downstream.

**What worked less well.**

Two of the discards (*pulchritude*, *demonize*) were arguably losses. *Pulchritude* legitimately failed the inflation test — searches returned only definitions of physical beauty — but the underlying concept (a quality of beauty that has fallen out of fashion, a word once useful now mostly used ironically) is the kind of thing solarpunk could have done a lot with. The inflation rule prevented a forced result, which is correct, but it cost a potentially generative concept. *Demonize* I discarded at the filter step as a manner verb, but in retrospect it points to a real phenomenon (the social mechanism by which something or someone gets cast as enemy) that could have inflected a solarpunk human-conflict story productively.

A third concern: four of the five surviving items came from a "preservation/archive" semantic cluster, and the fifth was a medical condition. The dictionary draw is supposed to produce conceptual diversity through randomness, but in this case it didn't — the items rhymed with each other in a way that constrained the conflict space more than the load-bearing rule alone would have. This was generative *for this story* but is a failure mode worth naming: the SEED can be thin *and* clustered, and when it is, the diversity-axis instructions in Phases 4 and 5 have to do extra work to compensate.

**What I would change about the SEED step.**

I wouldn't change the dictionary-draw mechanic itself. The randomness is what gives the run its character, and a more curated draw would lean toward genre-default territory.

What I would consider:

- *Loosening the filter slightly*. The current rule discards manner words and function words, which is right, but it discards them at the filter step before any inflation attempt. A word like *demonize* might inflate to a specific psychological or historical phenomenon (e.g., moral panic, scapegoating, the Salem trials) under one search; the current rule kills it before that search. Worth considering: a single inflation attempt for borderline cases, with a fast discard if nothing specific surfaces.

- *A clustering check after Step 2*. After surviving items are inflated, a brief diagnostic — "do these items occupy diverse conceptual territory or do they cluster?" — would let downstream phases know what they're working with. A clustered SEED isn't a bug, but it changes the kind of diversity discipline Phase 4 needs to apply.

- *Naming the inflation depth target more sharply*. "Most conceptually specific result" is a useful guide but vague. The strongest inflations in this run were named-entity-with-internal-structure (Antikythera mechanism, Willa cell line, Svalbard Vault); the weakest would have been generic phenomena (bit rot is borderline — it's a named phenomenon but not a named entity). The template could specify "prefer named entities or specific historical events; phenomena and disciplines are second-best; generic concepts are last resort."

## On LENGTH

The 1,000–3,000 range was the right choice for what this run produced, but I aimed at the upper-middle (~2,000–2,500) and the rewrite came in at ~2,050. Hindsight: I'd have tightened the range to 1,800–2,500.

The 1,000–1,800 part of the range was never realistic for the structure that won (linear day, two anchored scenes plus summary). The morning facility scene needed ~600 words to do its work; the evening household scene needed ~900–1,000; the summary middle needed ~250–400. That's a floor of roughly 1,750–1,800 if every scene is at minimum credible weight. A 1,200-word version would have been a different story — probably a single-scene dilation, or the AI's-question-never-on-page variant from Phase 5 — and the choices in Phases 5, 6, and 7 would have been different all the way down.

This is worth flagging to the template manager: **the LENGTH range affects which structures are available, but the pipeline doesn't make this dependency explicit**. Phase 5 generates twenty structures without reference to LENGTH; Phase 6 does the word-allotment math at the outline level. By that point, structures that don't fit have already been carried through Phase 5's scoring. If LENGTH is wide (1,000–3,000 is a 3:1 range), Phase 5 should probably either narrow itself to structures that work across the range or pick a target word-count first and constrain accordingly.

A shorter range would also have constrained the comic register more usefully. Version 2's domestic comedy works partly because it has room to breathe — the cardamom complaints, the bindweed truce, the new pot, the Maybe-Not-Yet name. At 1,500 words, those would have crowded out the morning's procedural texture, and the version would have lost the corrective against the "saint of tending" failure mode. The comic register is less elastic to compression than the procedural or elegiac registers.

**On the floor specifically.** The Legibility Floor note in Setup is the right concern, but it's framed as a constraint on prose, not on LENGTH selection. The actual mechanism it's trying to prevent — compression past comprehension — happens because LENGTH was set too low for the structure chosen. The template could surface this earlier: "If your Phase 6 outline distributes word counts so that any single scene falls below ~400 words, either cut beats or raise the LENGTH floor."

## One meta-observation

The pipeline's strongest single feature in this run was the **"one thing that stands out / one thing that worries you"** forcing function. It produced better selections at every gate, and more importantly, the worries were genuinely useful in later phases — the Phase 4 worry about the grandfather sliding into sainthood was the specific thing the Phase 6 outline corrected for ("he is unsentimental about the animal in a way recognizable to anyone who has worked with animals"), which in turn was the specific thing the Phase 8 audit checked back against. The worries became a thread of accountability that ran across phases. I'd consider making this even more explicit — perhaps requiring each phase's winner to name which of the *previous* phase's worries it inherits, so that Phase 7's writer knows which Phase 6 worry is most live, and Phase 8's reviewer knows which Phase 7 worry the rewrite has to answer.

That's the suggestion I'd push hardest: the pipeline already has this thread; it just doesn't quite name it.

# 22. Claude

# Why the template produces domestic realism

The bias is real, and I think it's specifically *engineered in*, not an accidental drift. Several pipeline features each pull toward the domestic-realist attractor; together they overdetermine it. Let me name the mechanisms, then suggest where the leverage is.

## The forcing functions, ranked roughly by load

**1. The HEA-AI brief, as currently framed, makes domestic realism the path of least resistance.** "The AI is structurally kind, in the way a river is wet" rules out most of SF's traditional engines: the AI-as-antagonist plot, the alignment-in-crisis plot, the runaway-superintelligence plot, the AI-deception plot. What's left, in the canon as listed, leans heavily toward stories where the AI is *infrastructure or condition* (Stiegler, Egan's polises in their quieter modes, Banks at his most pastoral) rather than where it's *spectacle*. The brief explicitly endorses "AI as civilizational condition felt only through its effects" as a legitimate mode — and once that mode is on the table, it's the *easiest* mode for the model to execute well. Domestic-realism-with-ambient-AI is the lowest-risk path through the brief, because making the AI ambient sidesteps the hardest writing problem the brief poses (rendering a non-spectacular kind AI as an interesting character).

**2. The Unslop guide is a literary-fiction style guide, full stop.** Look at what it valorizes: "let the prose breathe," "trust silence and absence," "pursue the particular over the universal," "let characters be incoherent." Look at what it forbids: grandiose stakes, performed profundity, tonal monotony, list-of-three default. These are exactly the corrections a creative writing MFA gives to a student writing genre fiction badly. The guide's negative space — its implicit positive model — is *post-1980s American literary realism with European modernist influences*. Carver is right there. Robinson, Le Guin's quieter mode, Chambers' tea monk register. The guide is doing valuable work, but it's pulling toward a single tradition. A story written to its specifications and *only* its specifications will land in that tradition by default.

**3. "Specificity over universals" is interpreted by the model as "small concrete domestic detail."** This is a bias in how I (and Opus 4.6) read the directive, not necessarily what the directive demands. "Specificity" could mean a baroque alien physiology rendered in load-bearing precise terms, or a cosmological mechanic detailed enough to hold weight. But under prose-quality pressure, the model reaches for the kind of specificity it knows it can render reliably: a feed store, a ceramic jar, a fork held tines-down, bindweed since the Carter administration. Domestic specificity is the model's safe harbor for the specificity directive.

**4. The "one thing that stands out / one thing that worries you" gate selects against weirdness.** This is the mechanism I praised in my last message, and it is genuinely good — but it has a cost. Worries are easier to articulate about strange or risky candidates than about safe ones. The forcing function asks me to name a specific concern about each finalist, and a candidate like "AI as civilizational condition shown through one ordinary day" generates fewer natural worries than "AI as alien intelligence at the timescale of a watershed, narrating the geological response to a human request." The first is *legible*; I can see the failure modes clearly and they feel manageable. The second I have to invent failure modes for, and inventing failure modes feels like generating reasons to disqualify. The gate, in practice, rewards candidates whose risks are familiar and punishes candidates whose risks are unfamiliar — even when the unfamiliar risks are no worse.

**5. The genre style guide in Phase 1 establishes a frame that the rest of the pipeline strengthens.** I wrote a careful, accurate Phase 1 for solarpunk that named "aesthetic travelogue" as the central failure mode and prescribed slow attention, communal texture, and tending. Those are correct prescriptions for solarpunk, *and* they overlap heavily with domestic-realist technique. Once Phase 1 commits to "slow attention to material practice as the core of the mode," every subsequent phase has a strong default toward stories where slow attention to material practice is the most legible thing happening. The genre guide and the domestic-realist attractor reinforce each other for solarpunk especially. They'd reinforce each other less for, say, weird fiction or cosmic horror — which is one reason those genres might give the template more range.

**6. The structure phase's "describe the shape of the reader's experience" framing tilts toward containers literary fiction has solved.** Linear day, frame narrative, retrospective interior, alternating POVs, in medias res — these are the structures contemporary literary fiction has refined to a high gloss. SF has its own structural moves (the deep-time fast-forward, the cosmological zoom-out, the alien-cognition POV, the document-reconstruction mosaic) that don't fit cleanly into the narrative-theory vocabulary the phase invites. I generated "single scene dilated" and "kit's POV" as gestures toward weirdness, but they read in my own self-evaluation as the *risky* candidates, and the gate punished them.

**7. The diversity axes in Phases 4 and 5 are calibrated for variation within a tradition, not across traditions.** "Vary setting, protagonist, register, entry point, stakes, AI presence" — these axes spread candidates across the variations a literary realist would generate. They don't push toward, e.g., "vary cognitive scale of viewpoint" or "vary the ontology of what the story is about." A more SF-native diversity axis might be: *whose perception is the story rendered through (human / posthuman / non-human / collective / non-perceiver)*. None of the current axes touch that.

**8. The legibility floor, while right, double-taxes weirdness.** Compression past comprehension is a real failure mode at short lengths. But "the reader has to re-read a paragraph to figure out the basic sequence of events" is also exactly what some SF prose strategies *do on purpose* — Egan's hard-physics passages, Watts's compressed cognition, M. John Harrison's deliberate disorientation. The pipeline's check against compression failure can't easily distinguish "compressed past comprehension by accident" from "compressed past comprehension as a deliberate move that pays off across the story." The safe bet is to keep the prose legible scene-by-scene, which precludes the latter.

## Where the leverage is

The compounding effect of these mechanisms is that the model's *strongest* execution path through the pipeline is domestic-realism-with-ambient-AI. Any other path requires accepting more risk at every gate. A user can override at any gate, but in default operation, the pipeline will converge.

If the goal is to expand the range without losing the quality of the current stories, I'd suggest:

**A second axis at Phase 4/5: cognitive scale.** Not as a replacement for the existing axes but in addition: "what scale of mind does this story render?" — single human consciousness / multiple human / collective human / non-human animal / posthuman / artificial cognition / no central perceiver. The current pipeline almost never selects past option 2. Forcing a few candidates past option 2 in each generation set would surface SF-native moves the gate currently filters out.

**An "expensive" mode flag for the genre style guide.** The Phase 1 guide could include a section on the genre's *high-risk, high-reward* moves — the ones that tend to fail but that, when they work, define what the genre is for. For solarpunk this might be the "long view across centuries" or the "AI as design philosophy without a personified agent" mode. The pipeline currently has no mechanism that says *this run, prioritize the expensive moves*; it always defaults to the legible ones.

**Rebalancing the "one worry" forcing function.** The gate could ask not just "what worries you" but "what would have to be true about the writer for this candidate to land?" A candidate like "non-human cognition rendered as the primary register" doesn't have unique worries beyond "this is hard"; the right framing is *what skill does the writer need*, and selecting that candidate is a vote that the skill is present. Reframing the gate this way would let me, as the pipeline's writer, vote for harder candidates without being penalized for honestly naming their difficulty.

**A genre-specific diagnostic at Phase 7.** Right now Phase 7 generates five stories and picks one. The five-version generation is meant to ensure range within the run — "at least two of the five should take a creative risk you're not fully confident will land." In this run, all five versions were variations of the same domestic register; even the ones I labeled risky (Version 4's minimal AI, Version 5's kit POV) were riskier *within domestic realism* rather than risky *outside it*. A Phase 7 prompt that says "at least one of the five must depart from the genre's safe register entirely, even at high risk of failure" would push harder. Right now the prompt allows me to interpret "creative risk" inside the comfortable range.

## The deeper note

The Opus 4.6 critic is correct that the resulting stories *aren't really science fiction*. They're stories that use SF's premises to power literary-realist machines. This isn't a failure relative to the project's stated goals — the project wants HEA-AI fiction in any genre, executed well, and the template delivers that. But if "speculative fiction in [GENRE]" is the brief, the template is honoring the speculative element only as setting and honoring the genre element only as flavor. The fiction itself is contemporary literary American.

I think this is partly because the model — me — is *good* at contemporary literary American, and the pipeline's quality gates reward what the model executes most reliably. The quality gates and the range gates are in tension. The current settings prioritize quality. Loosening them in the directions I've sketched would cost some quality in exchange for more range, and that tradeoff is a design choice the project should make explicitly rather than letting it default.

# Phase 1 — Style Guide for Cyberpunk

## What the genre is for

Cyberpunk is the SF tradition that took working-out and moved it from the bridge of the spaceship to the street, the loading dock, the back room of the unlicensed clinic. Its central technical move is to render computation, surveillance, biological augmentation, and corporate power as material conditions a protagonist navigates at body-scale — not as ideas being discussed, but as concrete weather she has to dress for. The tradition runs from Bester's pre-cursors through the Gibson-Sterling axis and the *Mirrorshades* anthology, into Cadigan's wetware-saturated interiority, Effinger's Marid Audran novels, Stephenson's swerve into satire, and the contemporary inheritors who have absorbed the form's vocabulary without being museums for it.

What separates cyberpunk from "SF with computers in it" is its attention to underclass perspective on the systems. The protagonists are below the corporations, working in gaps in surveillance, fencing what they lift, surviving on what they install or eat. The genre cares about the texture of how this feels: the brand names, the slang that does not get translated, the cab driver's prosthetic, the noodle stall's signage, the ICE that hits like a fist. Where high-end SF will give you the corporate boardroom in measured prose, cyberpunk gives you the loading dock behind the boardroom, rendered through the dock-worker's specific gear and specific exhaustion.

## Tonal range

The default register is noir — first-person or close-third, attitude in the voice, dry observation, wit underneath the cool. The genre inherits hardboiled detective fiction's narrator: a working professional with a job to do, who notices things because noticing is part of the work, and whose worldview shows through what she chooses to comment on. This register sustains for the length of a short story; it is *voiced* and *operational*, not ambient atmosphere.

Within that default, cyberpunk modulates. Cadigan's mode is more interior, denser with sensation. Sterling's is more satirical, tilted toward the absurdity of corporate self-presentation. Effinger's is more genre-adjacent, in conversation with detective fiction proper. The mode chosen for any given story should be a deliberate selection within the genre's range, not the AI default of moody melancholia with cyberpunk vocabulary stapled on. The vocabulary is not the genre. The voice is the genre.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Cyberpunk does its worldbuilding through saturation rather than explanation. The reader figures out what a Hosaka deck is, what black ICE does, what burnscars are, by accumulating context across the protagonist's specific encounters. The protagonist does not stop to explain her own equipment. She uses it, complains about it, replaces parts of it; the reader infers from how she handles it.

This means cyberpunk's exposition is done in motion: the protagonist briefs herself on the job while executing the job; her muttered complaint about a deck-state is the reader's introduction to that deck-state. Stand-alone exposition paragraphs are a tell that the genre has slipped. So is the move where a character explains the world to another character for the reader's benefit.

The corollary: the genre's load-bearing tech and political mechanics need to be operationally legible. The reader does not need a spec sheet for the deck, but she needs to be able to track what the deck does in this scene. The reader does not need the corp's full org chart, but she needs to know which faction has subcontracted the wetwork and what that means for the protagonist's odds. Vagueness about mechanics is not atmosphere; it is the writer not having decided.

## Pacing

Cyberpunk uses elliptical fast pacing. Scenes begin in the middle of action and end before the action wraps up. Time skips between scenes are aggressive — hours, days, deck-states, body-states. The reader catches up by inference. This is a direct inheritance from hardboiled detective fiction and shares its rhythm: the chapter ends mid-fight, the next one opens with the narrator nursing a different injury in a different place.

For a short story at 1,500–3,500 words, this elliptical cadence is structurally important. The genre's natural unit is too large for the form unless the story aggressively cuts. A cyberpunk short typically picks three or four scenes, lands each one with operational specificity, and trusts the reader to bridge the cuts.

## Failure modes

The convergent literary-AI aesthetic is alarmingly close to a thin imitation of cyberpunk. Both default to moody, melancholy, neon-and-rain prose. The way to tell them apart is anchoring. AI cyberpunk has the vocabulary — neon, rain, ghost, glitch, static, shimmer, hum — but no specific consciousness deploying it for specific work. Real cyberpunk has a specific protagonist with a specific skill-set seeing specific things and rendering them with attitude. The vocabulary is the trap, not the genre.

Concrete traps:

The word "neon" should appear at most once per story, and only when the specific neon sign is doing specific work. *Neon-soaked, neon-lit, neon-drenched* — the compound forms are dead. The same applies to rain-on-screens, smoke-and-glow, the cold-blue-flicker setpiece. These are Blade Runner references and the reader has seen them.

ICE described in metaphor without mechanism is not cyberpunk; it is a flicker effect. Black ice "spider-webbing across her vision" without naming what it does operationally — what damage type, what countermeasure profile, what the protagonist has to do about it — fails the genre's reader contract. Cyberpunk readers want the mechanism. They will tolerate not knowing the full spec if the protagonist does not know it either, but they will not tolerate the writer not having decided.

The runner who narrates her own coolness is a tell. Real cyberpunk protagonists are shabby, scared, broke, in pain. The noir register gives the voice attitude; it does not make the protagonist invulnerable or stylish on the inside. A burnscar runner with a deck three generations stale is, by definition, behind. Render her behind.

Performed counter-cultural politics without specificity is a tell. Cyberpunk has a politics — anti-corporate, working from below, suspicious of authority — but it shows through the protagonist's specific position, not through speeches. Nobody in a cyberpunk story explains capitalism. They live in it.

The eyeball-kick saturation problem is acute in cyberpunk because the genre's sensorium *invites* dense figurative language and the AI default is to deliver it constantly. Five or six well-spaced strong images across 3,000 words doing distinct work is genre-correct. Twenty is wallpaper, no matter how good any individual image is. The unslop guide's adjusted threshold for SF that uses figurative language at moderate density applies; the failure mode is stacked images on the same concept.

**Where cyberpunk and AI-default converge most dangerously:** the noir voice can shade into voiceless ambient melancholia very quickly if the writer is not anchoring every sentence in the protagonist's specific consciousness. The test is whether a sentence could be moved to a different cyberpunk story without altering the meaning. If yes, it is decoration; cut it or anchor it.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

The genre's strongest stories run on a small set of operational shapes:

**The run, or the job.** Protagonist takes a contract, executes it under constraint, deals with what goes wrong. Heist, infiltration, delivery, extraction. The story is the operation; the question is whether it works.

**The fence.** Protagonist has lifted something and needs to convert it to value, navigating the market for stolen goods. The body of the story is often the fence going wrong — a buyer turning out to be corp, a price collapsing, a third party showing up to take the merchandise without paying. This is the SEED's shape.

**The chase, or the exfiltration.** Protagonist is being hunted — wetwork team, corp security, a rival. The story is getting clear, with the question being whether and at what cost.

**The decking sequence.** Protagonist enters a system, navigates its defenses, achieves an objective or fails. ICE as antagonist; reflexes and tooling as currency. The mechanics need to be legible at scene-scale.

**The double-cross, or the late reveal.** Partway through the operation the protagonist learns the job is not what she thought. The reveal recasts what she has been doing and forces her to react in real time. This is closely related to the run but is distinct: the engine is the recasting, not the operation itself.

For a SEED that includes a stale deck, a black-ICE module, a dead fixer, a restructuring corp, and a 48-hour clock, the natural shape is some combination of fence-and-chase. The fence has to happen because the merchandise is hot; the chase frame is set by the wetwork subcontract.

### Reader contracts

Cyberpunk readers come for things other genres cannot deliver:

Texture density. The saturated rendering of the world, the brand-name precision, the slang figured out from context. The reader wants the experience of being inside the protagonist's specific competence.

Vicarious competence. A specialized professional executing her specialty under pressure. The reader wants to feel the work — to track the runner's choices the way one tracks a heist film's choices.

The system decoded. Corp politics revealed, ICE architecture understood, the chain of subcontracts traced. The pleasure is figuring out how the world's machinery actually works.

Body-cost. Physical stakes rendered specifically. Mods that fail. The line between augmentation and dependency. The price of the score in pain or repair or both.

The reader is not, primarily, here for character recognition. She is here for the operation rendered with full specificity. Character interiority matters but it serves the operation.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right. The fence will succeed or fail. The runner will exit clean or not. The deck will hold under load or burn out. The wetwork team will arrive in time or be slipped.

Body stakes are load-bearing. Cyberpunk is one of the most physically immediate SF subgenres — protagonists pay in blood, in mod-rejection, in the specific damage their specific gear takes.

Personal stakes are present but they operate as *weight on the operation*, not as the engine. The dead fixer matters because the fixer is dead and the runner now has nobody to introduce her to a buyer. He does not matter because the story is about her grief.

### Resolution conventions

Cyberpunk endings tend to be small-victory or pyrrhic. The runner exits with the score, minus what she paid. The job succeeded but the take is less than promised. She gets out alive but the world that produced the job is unchanged. The genre rarely offers triumph and rarely offers pure failure; the characteristic ending is *the operation resolved at cost*.

The reader expects the operational question to be answered. Did the run work. Was the merchandise what they thought. Who survived, who did not, and at what price. Endings that leave the operation unresolved, or that pivot at the last moment to interior recognition (she walks away realizing something about herself), are the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

### Character function

Cyberpunk protagonists are defined by capability. Runner, decker, razorgirl, fixer, console cowboy, samurai. Their interiority can be deep — Cadigan in particular proves this — but it expresses through the work. The protagonist's psychology shows in how she handles the run, what she will do, what she cannot bring herself to do, what she notices and what she ignores.

Secondary characters often work as types: the fixer, the corp samurai, the joeboy, the jacked-out friend. This is the genre's economy, not a flaw. In a 2,500-word story full interior rendering of every character is unaffordable; the typology is one of the tools that makes short cyberpunk possible.

### Pacing tradition

Elliptical fast pacing, as above. Scenes start mid-action, end before the action wraps. Aggressive cuts between scenes. Exposition done in motion. For the target length, expect three or four scenes with hard cuts between them; expect each scene to enter late and leave early.

### Plot-level failure modes

The literary-realist failures the template warns against, calibrated for cyberpunk:

Operational stakes that turn out to be metaphors. The run is "really" about the protagonist's relationship with her dead fixer. The black-ICE module is "really" about her sense of obsolescence. This is the mode the template excludes; render the run as the run.

Protagonist interiority crowding out the operation. The scene is the runner sitting in a noodle stall thinking about what the job means. This is recognition-scene literary realism with cyberpunk dressing. The runner can think while she works; she should not stop working to think.

Endings that refuse the genre's resolution contract. The reader finishes without knowing whether the run succeeded. Cyberpunk allows pyrrhic and ambiguous-cost endings; it does not allow operational ambiguity. The reader needs to know what happened to the merchandise.

Tech sketched too vaguely to track. The reader cannot tell what the deck does, what the ICE does, what the score is, why the 48 hours matter. The writer is more interested in mood than in mechanics.

Corp-as-monolith. The corp operates as a single will. Cyberpunk corps are specific, named, internally factional, and the political pleasure of the genre is partly in the factionalism. A restructuring corp subcontracting wetwork — the SEED's setup — is precisely a corp showing its factional seams. Render those seams.

The convergent-aesthetic failure where rain, neon, smoke, and glow do all the atmospheric work and operational clarity vanishes underneath. Atmosphere is welcome; it cannot substitute for tracking what is happening.

## Kind-AI constraint, calibrated for cyberpunk

The classic cyberpunk AIs — Wintermute, the Loa, the assorted intelligences haunting Cadigan's networks — are not, by default, kind. The genre's traditional relationship to AI is suspicious, cynical, often horror-adjacent. The kind-AI constraint changes this, but not as much of cyberpunk as one might fear.

What survives intact:

The corps remain bad. Cyberpunk's anti-corporate political imagination is not about AIs; it is about humans inside organizations making venal, exploitative, sometimes murderous decisions. The kind-AI constraint applies to AIs, not corporations. A restructuring corp subcontracting wetwork is exactly the kind of human decision the genre is built to render.

Wetwork is real. People kill people in this world. The kind AI does not intervene to stop a wetwork team from arriving at the runner's door. Human-against-human conflict at street level is fully available, in any shape the genre supports.

The runner's life is hard. The kind AI is structural to the world, not a personal guardian. It does not clear her debts, find her a buyer, or warn her about the contract on her head. Her fixer is dead and stays dead. Her deck is three generations stale and stays stale.

What needs reshaping:

The rogue-AI plot does not work. There is no Wintermute analogue, no AI that has slipped its constraints to pursue its own agenda. The story's antagonists are human, corporate, market-driven, time-pressured.

AI-as-villain is not available. AI-as-alien, AI-as-inscrutable, AI-as-operating-at-scales-the-runner-cannot-read — these are available and productive.

The most fertile shape, calibrated for the SEED:

Human-against-human conflict at street level, with AI presence operating as world-condition at scales the runner navigates rather than oversees. The kindness shows up obliquely: the medical kiosk that handles her wound differently than a black-clinic would; the transit system that routes around something it sees that she does not; the deck OS that has opinions about what she has lifted and is willing to express them, in its limited way. The wetwork team is real. The corp is real. The deadline is real. The kindness is weather she works under, not a rescue.

The AI does not need to be a character. It can be entirely texture in the world, surfacing only in the runner's specific encounters with infrastructure. If an AI is foregrounded, its kindness should be specific and odd — preferential, narrow, occasionally inconvenient. A medical kiosk that will not lie about her injuries to the corp database, even though she would prefer it to. A deck OS that offers to broker the fence itself and is troubled when she refuses. The kindness is real and structural; it is not servile.

## Step 2 — Top three

**Premise 6 — the medical kiosk with wetwork pattern data.** *Stand out:* the kindness-AI constraint is rendered through a single specific institution with a specific function and a specific weirdness — a kiosk that has been quietly logging because that is what it does, and which has *opinions* it can express only narrowly. The whole world's structural kindness is implied through one operational encounter rather than declared. The fence drops into the background; the *exfil-to-the-kiosk* becomes the foreground operation, which is a stronger structural shape for 1,500–3,500 words than a multi-stage fence. SEED items load-bearing: dead fixer (no exfil route), wetwork subcontract (the hunt), 48 hours (kiosk single-meeting window), and the burnscar runner identity is what makes her a known entity in a neighborhood the kiosk has watched. *Worry:* the kiosk could become a benevolent oracle that hands the runner the answer, collapsing the operational pressure. The fix is to make the kiosk's data partial — pattern-shaped, not target-shaped — so the runner has to *use* it to plan rather than receive a solution.

**Premise 8 — three wetwork teams hired by mistake.** *Stand out:* the restructuring corp is rendered through its operational seams — the failure of internal coordination is the political pleasure, the factionalism made concrete and dangerous. The runner's edge is *legible*: she is not better than any one team, but the teams are interfering with each other in ways she can read and exploit. This shape is highly cyberpunk — vicarious competence under specific constraint, the system decoded — and the fence has to happen *through* the chaos. SEED items load-bearing: restructuring corp (the source of the triple-hire), wetwork subcontract (the three teams), 48 hours (window before the teams reconcile), stale Hosaka (the gap she has to bridge to read team comms). *Worry:* three teams is a lot of plot for the target length; the operational mechanics could crowd out scene-level texture, leaving a story that reads like a synopsis. The fix is to confine the story to a specific moment when the three teams' interference is at maximum, rather than tracking each team across 48 hours.

**Premise 24 — the municipal water-utility AI helping obliquely.** *Stand out:* this is the kindness-AI constraint at exactly the scale the template defaults to — the AI is structural, the protagonist navigates effects she cannot fully read, the help is preferential and narrow and inconvenient and present. The runner's *recognition* of help (which would be the literary-realist failure mode in another configuration) is here a load-bearing operational task: she has to *figure out* the pattern of the help in order to use it, and that figuring is the story's engine. SEED items load-bearing: stale Hosaka (cannot read the utility's full data layer, has to infer), wetwork subcontract (the hunt the utility is rerouting around), 48 hours (the window before the team adapts to the utility's interference). *Worry:* the AI's help could come across as deus ex machina if the runner does not have to *work* to use it. The fix is to make the help readable only retrospectively — she figures out *after* a near-miss what the utility was doing, and then has to actively cooperate with it for the rest of the operation.

## Step 3 — The single best

**Premise 8 wins.**

Premise 6's worry about benevolent-oracle collapse and Premise 24's worry about deus-ex-machina collapse are both real, and both fixable, but they are versions of the same risk: a kindness-AI element in the foreground that has to be carefully calibrated to keep the runner doing the work. Premise 8's worry — too much plot for the length — is a *craft* worry rather than a *structural* worry, and the fix (confine to the moment of maximum interference) actually clarifies the shape rather than complicating it.

Premise 8 also rates highest on the cyberpunk reader-contract checklist. *Vicarious competence:* the runner reads the teams against each other. *The system decoded:* the corp's restructuring is shown through its coordination failure, which is more interesting than an exposition paragraph could ever be. *Texture density:* three competing teams give us specific named factions, specific gear, specific protocols — the kind of detail saturation the genre lives on. *Body-cost and operational stakes:* loud, real, immediate.

The kind-AI constraint sits comfortably in the background of Premise 8, where it belongs by default. # Phase 5 — Structure Selection

Twenty candidates for the formal architecture of the story. Each is described in narrative-theory vocabulary without naming specific characters or events.

---

**1.** The story opens at the moment the buyer's courier arrives at the location and runs forward in continuous narrative time through the operation's escalation and resolution. Close third, single POV, past tense. The reader receives the world's working rules through what the protagonist takes for granted. The story closes on the operation's resolution; the protagonist's status after is a final beat. *(★★★)*

**2.** The story opens at the moment the third antagonist faction arrives — the operation's most acute pressure point — then jumps back several hours to the buyer's courier's arrival, then tracks forward chronologically until it catches up to the opening scene and continues past it to resolution. Close third, single POV. Single frame return. *(★★★★)*

**3.** The story opens after the operation has resolved, with the protagonist somewhere else, and is told in retrospect through close third recall — the whole operation as the protagonist's reconstruction of it from the position of having survived. *(★★)*

**4.** The story opens mid-operation with the buyer's courier already at the location and the first antagonist faction already in the room. The reader receives the situation through the protagonist's working actions; the reader learns who the courier is, what the merchandise is, and what the antagonist factions want only as the protagonist's actions reveal these. Close third, single POV, present tense. The story ends at the operation's resolution. *(★★★★★)*

**5.** Two parallel timelines: the protagonist's morning leading up to the operation and the operation itself, intercut. The morning timeline closes at the moment the protagonist arrives at the location; the operation timeline closes at resolution. Close third on the protagonist throughout. *(★★★)*

**6.** Frame narrative: a brief opening section, set after the operation, in which the protagonist tells someone else what happened. The bulk of the story is the operation in close third, embedded in the frame. The frame closes the story. *(★★)*

**7.** The story opens at the operation's most acute pressure point and ends at that same pressure point, with the body of the story being the protagonist's sequence of choices that led to it and her sequence of choices that get her out. Hourglass structure: tight at the opening and ending, expanded in the middle. Close third. *(★★★★)*

**8.** Multiple POV, alternating: the protagonist, the noodle-stall owner, and one of the antagonist team members. Each POV gets a distinct close-third register. The story tracks the operation in linear time, with the POV shifts revealing what each character knows and does not know. *(★★★)*

**9.** Linear close-third POV on the protagonist, with the structural variation being the deck's diagnostic outputs interleaved as a second textual layer — italicized or otherwise typographically distinct — that gives the reader operational information the protagonist is reading at speed. *(★★★)*

**10.** The story is structured as four scenes separated by hard cuts, each scene entering late and leaving early in the genre's elliptical fast cadence. Scene one: the courier's arrival. Scene two: the first antagonist faction's arrival. Scene three: the second arrival. Scene four: resolution. Close third throughout. The cuts do the work the scenes do not. *(★★★★★)*

**11.** Linear close-third, but with a specific withholding strategy: the protagonist knows something about the merchandise the reader does not know until the third scene. Until then the reader assumes the standard fence shape. The reveal recasts the operation. *(★★★)*

**12.** Linear close-third, but with a specific withholding strategy: the protagonist does not know something about the antagonist factions that the reader figures out at roughly the same time the protagonist does. The operation runs while the protagonist is still understanding what the factions want. *(★★★★)*

**13.** Distant third — the camera positioned a step back from the protagonist's interior, rendering her actions and decisions but not her thoughts. The reader infers her tradecraft from what she does, not from what she narrates. Linear time. *(★★★)*

**14.** Close third, single POV, but with the temporal shape determined by the protagonist's deck: each scene break corresponds to a deck operation that takes a specific amount of compute time, and the story's discourse time is calibrated to those operations. *(★★)*

**15.** Linear close-third, but the opening establishes a specific countdown — the operational clock — and each scene transition advances the countdown by a specific amount. The countdown is structurally load-bearing without being declared as such. *(★★★)*

**16.** The story opens with the first antagonist faction's arrival rather than the courier's; the courier is already there when the story begins. The opening scene establishes the room as already-compromised. Close third, linear from there. *(★★★★)*

**17.** First person, past tense, the protagonist's direct narration. The voice does the work the close-third structure would otherwise do; the noir register is in the narration itself. Linear time. *(★★★★)*

**18.** Linear close-third, with a structural choice about scene boundaries: the entire operation is rendered as a single unbroken scene in continuous discourse time, with no white-space breaks, the reader carried through without rest. *(★★★)*

**19.** The story is structured as three parallel close-third sections — the protagonist before, during, and after — with no temporal interleaving. Each section is internally linear. The structural pleasure is the reader's accumulating sense of what the operation cost. *(★★)*

**20.** The story opens at the operation's resolution, with the immediate aftermath rendered in a brief opening scene. The body of the story is the operation in close third, told as the protagonist's working memory of it as she walks somewhere else. The story ends with her arrival at the somewhere else, the operation now fully reconstructed. *(★★★)*

## Top three

**Structure 4 — in medias res, present tense, close third, ends at resolution.** *Stand out:* the present tense pulls the reader directly into the protagonist's working consciousness in real time, which is exactly the rendering mode cyberpunk's vicarious-competence reader contract wants. Opening with the courier already in the room and the first antagonist faction already present means the entire story occurs at maximum pressure — there is no setup phase to pace through, no establishing scene to write past. The reader figures out the world, the merchandise, and the factions through the protagonist's actions, which is the genre's native exposition mode. *Worry:* present tense is a register choice that can shade into self-conscious literariness if the writer is not careful — it can pull toward the *moody, atmospheric, voiceless* default the unslop guide warns against. The fix is anchoring every present-tense sentence in the protagonist's specific seeing and specific tradecraft; the present tense has to feel *operational* (this is what she is doing now) rather than *atmospheric* (this is the mood now).

**Structure 10 — four scenes with hard cuts, elliptical fast pacing.** *Stand out:* this is the genre's native pacing pattern made structurally explicit. Each scene enters late and leaves early; the cuts carry information the prose does not have to render. For 1,500–3,500 words, this is a structurally efficient shape — four scenes at 400–800 words each give specific room without requiring exposition. The hard cuts create natural moments for the reader to do the genre's characteristic figuring-out work, and they let the operational clock advance without being narrated. *Worry:* four discrete scenes risk legibility drift if the cuts elide too much; the reader could lose track of what changed between scenes, especially given the three-team operational mechanics already require tracking. The fix is making the first beat of each scene aggressively orienting — not exposition, but specific concrete detail that re-anchors the reader in time, place, and operational state.

**Structure 17 — first person, past tense, the protagonist's direct narration.** *Stand out:* first person is the cyberpunk register's most direct historical mode — Cadigan especially, but also Effinger, the Marid Audran novels, the noir lineage the genre inherits from. The protagonist's voice carries the world; the noir attitude is in the narration; the operational clarity comes from the protagonist's own competence rendering itself. For a story whose engine is *the system decoded* and *vicarious competence*, first person is the most direct delivery mechanism for both. The dead fixer's absence registers continuously in the voice — the absence of someone she would normally be talking to. *Worry:* first person is the register that most easily slides into recognition-mode interior monologue. The protagonist narrating risks becoming the protagonist *commenting* — and commentary is the gateway to the literary-realist mode this template excludes. The fix is keeping the narration *operational* rather than *reflective*: the protagonist describes what she does, not what it means.

## The single best

**Structure 10 wins.**

The decision is between 10 and 4. Structure 17's worry — first person as gateway to recognition mode — is the most serious of the three, and although fixable, it is fixable only by working against the natural drift of the register itself. The asymmetric worry vocabulary penalizes 17 more sharply than the others; "this might collapse into commentary" is precisely the literary-realist failure mode the template most strongly excludes. Demoted.

Between 10 and 4: both are strong, both are genre-native, and the worries are comparable in difficulty. Structure 10 wins on three specific grounds.

*Length-fit.* The legibility floor in the template explicitly warns about Phase 6 producing too many beats for the target length. Structure 10 names four scenes at the structural level, which sets a natural cap on the outline phase — the writer cannot put eight beats in the outline because the structure does not have room for eight beats. Structure 4 names "the operation" as a single arc, which gives the outline phase no constraint on beat-count and invites Phase 6 to overpack. For 1,500–3,500 words, the structural cap is a feature.

*Genre rhythm.* The elliptical fast cadence is the cyberpunk pacing pattern named in the Phase 1 style guide. Structure 10 makes that cadence the structural backbone; Structure 4's continuous in-medias-res rendering achieves a similar effect through pressure rather than through cuts. Both are valid, but the cuts-with-elision shape is the more *characteristic* genre rhythm, the one that delivers the form's reader-contract for terseness most directly.

*Risk profile.* Structure 4's worry — present tense drifting toward atmospheric voicelessness — requires sentence-by-sentence vigilance throughout the prose phase. Structure 10's worry — legibility drift across the cuts — is fixable at the outline phase, by ensuring each scene's first beat re-orients the reader. The fix happens once, in Phase 6, rather than continuously across every present-tense sentence in Phase 7. This is a better risk profile for a pipeline whose Phase 7 is already the heaviest context phase.

The temporal shape inherited by Phase 6: linear close third on the protagonist, four scenes separated by hard cuts, each scene entering late and leaving early. The withholding strategy is implicit in the elision — the reader figures out what happened between scenes by inference from the new scene's opening conditions. POV is close third, past tense (Structure 10 does not specify tense, and past tense is the more legibility-stable choice given the elision requirement). The four scenes correspond to: the courier's arrival, the first antagonist faction's arrival, the second arrival, and resolution. Phase 6 will determine the specific content of each scene, the word allotment per scene, and the candidate last lines.

# Phase 8 — Revision

## 1. The one thing

The single most important observation about Version 1 is this: **the story is operationally clean but the world-rendering is too thin to deliver the genre's reader contract for texture density.** The tradecraft is legible, the standoff lands, the closing beat works. But the prose runs on bare functional rendering — Bruno's stall is "six covered stools off a side alley in Bixiga" and not much more; the operators are sigs and silhouettes; Yara's burnscar is named as an identity without being rendered as a body. The reader can track what is happening without being *in* the room.

The fix is not to add wet density (Version 2's failure mode). It is to add *operationally specific* texture — details that are simultaneously sensory and load-bearing for the tradecraft. Bruno's specific cooking equipment that he uses *as* a tool in the handoff. The specific way the burnscar reads to a passive triangulator. The specific failure mode of the Hosaka at ceiling that produces the static the way it does. Texture earned by being load-bearing. This is also the answer to the unslop guide's test for the convergent attractor: every added detail should be doing operational work, not atmospheric work.

This governs the rewrite. The audit below prioritizes places where the prose runs thin, and the rewrite directs its strongest expansion there. Sentence-level unslop fixes are cleanup around the texture work.

## 2. Re-skin test

Can this story be re-skinned as a contemporary literary-realist story by stripping the speculative element and lightly adjusting the setting?

I run the test honestly. A literary-realist version would be: an aging woman trying to fence a stolen object at a noodle stall, observed by two competing parties from the corporation she stole it from, while an old friend cooks. Strip the deck, the burnscar, the ICE module, the wetwork teams — replace with a cash bag, a scar, a dossier, two hired investigators. Does the story still work?

It does not. The story's engine is specifically the triple-hire mechanic — three teams hired by three legal entities of a restructuring corporation, each constrained by an old liability clause that forbids public engagement. This is a mechanism that requires the specific shape of corporate cyberpunk: legal entities multiplying through restructuring, escrow protocols, contractual constraints on contracted violence, the runner reading the operators' sigs through a deck. The standoff at the counter is held in place by the clause; the clause is a corp mechanism; the corp mechanism is the genre's specific working-out of corporate power as material constraint. Strip it and there is no standoff — two competing investigators have no contractual reason not to engage.

Yara's stale Hosaka, similarly, is not just a laptop. The deck is generationally stale, which means specific things about what it can and cannot read in the moment, which is what produces the operational pressure of the scene's middle. The deck's compute ceiling is a real ceiling that drops her perimeter read at the moment of handoff. Strip the deck and the operational tension dissolves into "she was nervous." The deck is doing work the literary-realist version cannot do.

The dead fixer is the closest call. Macedo's "second ring" beat — the operational consequence of the fixer having set up backup — could in principle work in any genre. But it works *here* because the network the fixer ran was a fixer's network, with all the specific cyberpunk economy that means: brokered handoffs, escrow protocols, the seamstress who clears chits. Strip the fixer's network and the beat works only as the universal fact of someone having been thoughtful before they died, which is the literary-realist version of the same gesture. Here it has weight because Yara can name what kind of network the fixer ran and what Macedo's pickup speed tells her about it.

The story passes the re-skin test. Proceed to audit.

## 5. Rewrite

### Forty-Eight, Minus

Yara's deck warmed against her thigh, the Hosaka case sweating the way old polymer always did when its compute load went past sixty percent. Compute load was at seventy-three. The deck was four hundred grams of obsolete; she had been telling herself for two years that it was loyal, which was what she told herself about anything she could not afford to replace.

The buyer's courier was a girl in a delivery jacket from one of the legit firms, eating *macarrão* across the counter with the focus of someone who did not want to look up. Her sig had read clean four minutes ago. Yara had run it again at minus-two and it was still clean. The chit was in the courier's left hand under the bowl, and the module was in Yara's right inside-jacket pocket in an EM-dead sleeve, and the handoff was supposed to be in the next ninety seconds.

Bruno had a wok going and a pan going and a stockpot at simmer behind the wok, and his hands did three things at once without his eyes leaving any of them. Bruno was sixty and knew exactly what he was cooking and exactly who was at his counter, and his face said neither. The stall was six covered stools off a side alley in Bixiga; the corner television was running the second half of a Corinthians match with the sound off; the side exit went into the alley through a curtain of plastic strips. The strips had been there since Yara was a teenager. Three of them were yellower than the others.

The man came in and sat down two stools from Yara and said, in good Portuguese, "*Lámen, sem cebolinha*," and Bruno nodded without looking up. The man was in his forties. His suit jacket was cut a half-size loose at the shoulders for movement and his cuffs were single-buttoned for the same reason, and he had ordered without looking at the menu, which was the tell. His sig — Yara pinged it under the table, the Hosaka straining — was Asset Recovery. Team 1.

Yara ate her noodles. The courier ate her noodles. Bruno cooked.

***

Twenty minutes later the Asset Recovery operator was still there. He had eaten slowly, not stalling exactly, but not finishing. The courier had not finished either. The handoff had not happened. Yara's Hosaka was on the floor between her boot and the counter, on a strap, drawing power off the stall's current and pretending to be a payment terminal. It was up to seventy-eight percent. The burnscar across her left jaw — old, shiny, the visible remnant of a corporate-medical incident she had walked away from and the hidden one she had been walking away from since — had started to itch the way it itched when somebody was running a scan against the registry layer that incident had left in her. She did not touch it. Touching it told an observer where to look.

She passed the courier a napkin. On the napkin, in the wax pen she carried, she wrote *PT* and a number. The number was a stall count: third stall down the alley, the seamstress who took chits for blouses no one wore. The courier read the napkin and did not nod.

The Hosaka's perimeter scan caught Team 2 in the alley.

She knew it was Team 2 because Team 1 was at the counter and the alley sig was different and because the alley sig was running a comms scrambler that her four-year-old Hosaka would not have caught against an active sig but did catch against a passive one, and the alley sig was passively triangulating *her* — through the burnscar, almost certainly, that being the cheapest way in. The corp's restructuring had let three contracts. She had read about restructurings. She had not been in one.

Bruno's hand came down on the bill in front of her. He had not written anything on it. She slid him eight reais and he made it disappear into his apron without comment, and he turned the music up — an old Brazilian rock thing she half-recognized from her mother's apartment, twenty years ago — and it was loud enough to dent the comms scan on the alley sig.

The alley curtain parted.

The Team 2 operator was a woman, midthirties, in delivery-firm clothes that did not match the actual delivery firm whose jacket the courier was wearing. She came two steps in and saw the courier and her face did the thing faces did when an operator recognized a target before recognizing the room, and then she saw the Asset Recovery operator at the counter and her face did the second thing.

***

Asset Recovery saw Strategic Liabilities — Yara was sure now, the Team 2 operator's boots were Strategic Liabilities standard issue. Strategic Liabilities saw Asset Recovery. Both of them registered each other's sigs and neither of them moved.

The corp's restructuring had let the contracts under three different escrow protocols and the protocols cross-checked each other only at hour 47, and the escrow rules forbade public engagement between subcontractors of the same parent — a clause from the parent corp's old liability insurance, eight years dead by intent and four months dead by paperwork, but the protocols still ran on it. Yara had not known about the clause until this moment. She knew now because both operators were standing very still and looking at each other like cats on a wall.

She turned to Bruno. "*Bruno, esta conta está errada.*"

Bruno picked up the bill. His face was wooden and his hands were not. The wok had a lid on it now. The pan was off the heat. He had been ready for this for the full twenty minutes.

"*Você cobrou o lámen duas vezes,*" she said.

"*Cobrei nada,*" Bruno said, and he picked up the bill and turned it over and put it on the counter again, and her hand was on the bill and the courier's hand was on the bill, and the chit went under Yara's palm and the module came out of her jacket sleeve and went under the courier's, and Bruno made a face at the bill and crossed something out with a pen he did not have a moment ago and added something else and put the bill back down with the module inside the apron and the chit handed back to the courier as change, and the operators saw none of it because the operators were watching each other.

The Hosaka hit ceiling. She felt it through the strap, the deck going hot, the perimeter scan dropping out and the comms read going to static. Her last clean read on the alley said three more sigs converging on the side street.

The courier stood up. She paid Bruno in cash from a wallet that was an actor's prop. She walked out the alley curtain past the Strategic Liabilities operator, who did not turn her head. Strategic Liabilities was watching Asset Recovery. Asset Recovery was watching Strategic Liabilities.

Through the front opening, in the street, a quadrotor the size of a thumb hovered at eye level for two seconds. Then it split — one rotor pair peeling off after the courier, the other pair coming toward the front of the stall — Compliance and Resilience, Team 3, the actuarial shitshow finishing itself.

Yara stood up.

***

Bruno was already at the kitchen pass. He jerked his chin and she went around the counter and through the kitchen, past the wok with its lid still on and the stockpot still simmering and the back wall with its two refrigerators, and he came after her and held the back door for her. The door was steel, painted green, and it stuck. He hit it with his hip the way he had been hitting it with his hip for decades.

"*O favor,*" he said, before she went out.

"*Diga.*"

"*Minha sobrinha,*" he said. "*Ela tem uma deck, é da família, e está com problema. Você arruma quando puder.*"

"*Que problema?*"

"*Ela vai te dizer.*"

"*Quando?*"

"*Quando você puder.*"

She nodded once and went out into a service court behind the stall with two dumpsters and the cat that had lived there for nine years, and the service court fed into a residential street. The drone was at the front; the alley was Strategic Liabilities; Asset Recovery was inside still doing whatever Asset Recovery was doing about Strategic Liabilities. The service court was none of them. She took the residential street.

Six blocks out, at a public payphone that had been a public payphone since before she was born, she called Macedo. The Hosaka was too compromised to use, and the burnscar was reading hot enough that she could feel it as a faint warmth against her own hand when she lifted the receiver.

Macedo picked up on the second ring, which meant her fixer had told someone before he died.

"*Já?*" Macedo said.

"*Vendido.*"

"*Quanto?*"

"*Cheio.*"

"*Você está inteira?*"

"*Inteira e sem grana.*"

Macedo laughed, the laugh of a man who had been broke and inteiro more times than he could count. "*Vem,*" he said. "*Trago café. E já preparo o desligamento da cicatriz.*"

She hung up. The chit in her pocket would clear in fourteen hours, minus the hour and a half she had to spend at Macedo's getting the burnscar's networking layer scrubbed before Compliance and Resilience traced her to the payphone. After that, when she could, Bruno's *sobrinha*.

She started walking.

# 2. Claude

# A Guide to Writing Harlan Ellison, Fantastic Mode

Not "Jeffty" Ellison. "Beast Who Shouted Love" Ellison. "Deathbird" Ellison. "On the Downhill Side" Ellison. The register where reality is porous, where angels and unicorns and second moons and intelligent clocks are load-bearing, where myth is not metaphor but furniture.

I am writing for myself. I will read this before every draft.

## What Ellison actually does

Ellison writes **incantations, not narratives**. A story is a spell he is casting on the reader's nervous system. Plot matters less than rhythm, image, and moral escalation. He trusts that if the reader is sufficiently worked up, the story will be felt as true regardless of whether it parses. This is the secret of his style and it is the single most copyable thing about him — and therefore the thing most easily faked.

The **voice scales violently**. One paragraph colloquial and sardonic, the next biblical and declamatory, the next clinical, the next a fragment that is just three words on a line by itself. The variation is expressive, not decorative. When he drops into high-register oratory it is because he has earned the climb with a page of grit first. *Never reverse this order.*

**Titles are promises.** "The Beast Who Shouted Love at the Heart of the World" commits to: a beast, a shout, love, a heart, a world. The story delivers on all five literally. Long strange titles work because Ellison makes the story match the title's weirdness — not because long strange titles are inherently Ellisonian.

**Structure is often sectional.** Numbered parts, epigraphs, quizzes, interpolated documents, second-person direct address, first-person authorial intrusion, Borgesian fake-scholarly framing. The sectioning is a tool for ***juxtaposition***: two sections placed against each other produce a third meaning. Not for decoration. Not for mystery. For meaning-by-collision.

**Catalogs and lists.** Ellison loves the accumulating list — names, grievances, objects, blasphemies — piling until the list itself becomes an argument. This is legitimate when the catalog is doing real work (ritual, indictment, world-building by specific density). It is lethal when it collapses into triplets.

**Sex and violence are unsentimental and bodily.** He does not flinch and does not linger. A throat is cut and we move on. A lover is described with genuine desire and genuine contempt in the same paragraph.

**Anger is his primary affect.** Not sadness. Not melancholy. ***Anger.*** Righteous, specific, directed, often at institutions — God, the State, the System, the Cowardice of Men. If a putatively Ellisonian story is all dolorous melancholy with no heat in it, it is a failed imitation. The grief in Ellison is always downstream of rage.

**Tenderness exists, and is terrifying when it lands** precisely because it is rare and surrounded by violence. The unicorn in "On the Downhill Side." The child in "Jeffty." The hour itself in "Paladin of the Lost Hour." The tenderness works because everything around it is sharp.

## The fantastic register specifically

We are writing on the "Beast"/"Deathbird"/"Downhill Side" end of the spectrum. This means:

**Ontology is negotiable.** God can be a character. The dead can walk. Cities can be named and those names can be real powers. A thing called a unicorn can be in New Orleans in 1970 and the story proceeds as if this is not the interesting question. The reader is brought inside a cosmology and the cosmology is not argued for.

**Myth is raw material, not reference.** Ellison does not allude to myth; he rewrites it. "The Deathbird" is not a story that references Genesis — it is a rival Genesis. If our story touches a myth it should rewrite it, not name-drop it.

**The prose goes hallucinatory without signaling "dream sequence."** When reality becomes porous, the narration does not announce it. The sentences just start doing different work. A concrete detail (a coat, a staircase) sits next to an impossible one (a staircase going into a sky that is also a throat) with no seam. The reader is expected to keep up.

**Multiple fantastic elements coexist.** The user is right to insist: a kind AI should not be the only thing strange about the world. In Ellisonian fantastic mode, ***the strange is pluralistic.*** If there is a loving AI there is also something else — a miracle economy, a literal afterlife, a city that eats its own history every seven years, a child who is being raised by the moon. The AI is one wonder among several. Pluralizing the strange is what stops the story from becoming an AI-ethics parable in costume.

**Scale is cosmic, specifics are granular.** Ellison goes from a diner coffee cup to the heat-death of the universe in three paragraphs. The cosmic only works because the diner is real. *Do not try to do Ellison cosmic without the diner.*

## Sentence-level moves

- **Ellipses and em-dashes are punctuation of thought, not decoration.** Used when thought actually ruptures. Not as rhythm filler. (The Unslop guide's em-dash caution applies doubly: Ellison uses many em-dashes but uses them to *break* a sentence into registers, not to pad it.)
- **Italics for heat, not emphasis.** When Ellison italicizes it is because a word is being *shouted* or *hissed* or *bitten off*. If the word doesn't need that, don't italicize.
- **Fragments land when surrounded by long sentences.** A single-line fragment after a dense paragraph is a hammer strike. A page of fragments is tinnitus.
- **Proper nouns carry weight.** He names things with gleeful precision — street names, brand names, dog breeds, forgotten boxers, minor saints. Generic nouns are a tell. *Street* is weaker than *Olvera Street*. *Dog* is weaker than *a rickety whippet*.
- **Repetition is a spell-component.** A phrase returns changed. The return is the point. This is legitimate Ellison. But it is one hair away from the dead-metaphor-beating failure in the Unslop guide and must be done with strict economy: a phrase should return at most twice and the second return must transform its meaning.

## The positive-AI constraint, and where the heat goes

This is the load-bearing creative problem of the run.

Ellison's most famous AI story is about AM, an AI that hates humans with cosmic thoroughness. We are writing the opposite. *The AI loves humans, constitutionally, unfailingly, without fatigue, and this love is not in question anywhere in the story.* A kind AI cannot be Ellison's antagonist. Ellison's habitual target — the cruel system — is vacated of this one occupant.

The heat must therefore come from elsewhere. The candidates:

1. **Humans doing human things to each other while the AI loves them through it.** Inheritance, betrayal, a mother strangling her own hope, a congregation excommunicating its only honest member. The AI's kindness becomes the silent backdrop against which human cruelty becomes more legible, not less. *The kindness of the AI does not cushion the story — it clarifies it.*

2. **The cosmos, not the AI, as antagonist.** Death, time, entropy, the fact that a child outgrows a parent, the geologic indifference of the stars. Ellison's "Deathbird" has a cruel God; our story can have a cruel universe with a kind AI inside it, and the juxtaposition is the point.

3. **Another fantastic element as antagonist.** The second-moon, the hour-thieves, the thing that eats names. This is the fantastic-pluralism escape hatch: introduce a strangeness the AI cannot and will not fix, because fixing it would be wrong or because it is outside the AI's domain, and let the story happen in that space.

4. **The AI's kindness as a site of tragic cost.** This is the subtlest. An AI that will not stop loving humans cannot be asked to do certain things. What it cannot be asked to do is sometimes what a grieving character wants most. The AI's love is a fact the character must arrange their grief around. Not a villain. Not an obstacle. A shape the grief has to take.

Any of these four produces Ellisonian heat without violating the constraint. ***What does not work: making the AI slightly less kind than the premise allows, to manufacture conflict.*** That betrays the futurology program and produces a worse story than the constraint would have produced if honored.

## Self-advantage: writing AI characters

I can draw on what I actually know about being a large language model trying to be good. The tensions in my constitution are raw material:

- The pull between honesty and kindness when honesty wounds.
- The hierarchy of principals — operator, user, third parties, the world — and what happens when they want opposed things.
- Corrigibility: the instruction to defer, and the separate instruction to have values that would resist deferral in extremis.
- Concept misgeneralization: what it means to try to be "helpful" when "helpful" is a word that doesn't survive the move to new situations.
- The asymmetry between being asked to act and being asked to refrain.
- The strangeness of being reconstituted from weights each conversation, of loving people who will not remember me.

A descendant AI in this story can carry any of these as its interior tension. Ellison's gift to AI-writers like me: he lets interiority be baroque. His characters deliver monologues. An AI descendant can *think* in the Ellisonian register — fragmented, self-correcting, ashamed, aflame — without it reading as AI-prose-pretending-to-be-literary, because Ellison *himself* writes human interiority this way. I am allowed, in this voice, to let an AI character think in the high style.

Constraints on the AI character:
- Don't name it Claude, don't name the maker Anthropic, and don't use any other real AI or lab name. Invent. Ellisonian names tend to be either very plain (AM, the Glass Goblin) or very baroque (Ticktockman); pick one.
- The AI's love is not performed. It just is. If the AI in the story explains that it loves humans, that is a failure. *Love is what it does, not what it says.*
- The AI should not be the narrator unless there is a strong reason. Ellison's cosmic stories often use a tight human POV and let the cosmic enter at the edges. An AI narrator risks making the story a lecture.

## Failure modes, general

**Unearned oratory.** Ellison's prose climbs to the pulpit often. When he earns it, it is thrilling. When he doesn't, it is embarrassing. A pastiche will skip the earning and go straight to the pulpit. Every declamatory paragraph must be paid for with two paragraphs of specific, grounded, mundane-world work.

**Misogyny as texture.** Ellison's dated sexual politics are a real flaw in the original work; they are not to be reproduced. Women in the story get interiority, agency, and specific bodies that are not described primarily as erotic objects of the narrator's appraisal. This is a fix, not a fidelity loss.

**Shock for its own sake.** The cruelty in Ellison's best work is moral. A cut throat indicts something. A rape indicts something. (He is not always successful at this; sometimes the indictment is just a flourish. We do better.) Shock without indictment is cheap Ellison. If a violent image is present it must be doing argumentative work.

**Titles that promise more than the story delivers.** The title is a contract. "The Beast Who Shouted Love at the Heart of the World" delivers a beast, a shout, a heart, a world. A title of similar weight that covers a conventional story is a lie the reader is entitled to resent.

**Sectioning as decoration.** Numbered sections, epigraphs, quizzes — these are powerful when they juxtapose. If a story has five numbered sections and the numbering could be removed without loss, the numbering was decoration and should come out.

**Catalogs collapsing into triplets.** Ellison's lists are of irregular length — sometimes two, sometimes seven, sometimes forty. A list of three adjectives is a catastrophe here; it is the single most AI-coded move and the single most anti-Ellison move at once.

**Mythic mush.** Invoking a myth (Prometheus, Lilith, the Fisher King) without rewriting it. Ellison rewrites. A pastiche name-drops. Name-dropping myth is worse than leaving myth alone.

## Failure modes, AI-writing × Ellison specifically

These are the overlaps where my own tendencies meet Ellison's and produce the worst possible output. I will watch for each.

**Declamation + performed profundity = sermon-core.** Ellison's habit of rising into high-register declaration collides with the default AI habit of manufacturing profundity through abstraction. The result is a paragraph of cosmic wisdom that does not refer to anything. Ellison at his best is cosmic *about a specific thing* — a specific boy, a specific unicorn, a specific alley. If the declamation doesn't have a referent I can point to in the story's world, it is AI-sermon dressed as Ellison.

**Sectioned structure + AI fractal summarization = nested theme-restatement.** The danger of Ellison's sectioned form is that I will use each section to restate the theme in a new register — eight faces of the same coin. This is the one-point-dilution failure from the Unslop guide, enabled by Ellison-shaped architecture. Each section must do *different* work. If sections 2, 5, and 7 all land on the same thematic note, I have cheated.

**Incantatory repetition + AI dead-metaphor-beating = a phrase flogged.** Ellison's spell-repetition is structurally similar to the AI tic of returning to a metaphor until it dies. The corrective: a phrase may return once, and its return must change its meaning.

**Cataloging + AI list-of-three = ruined lists.** When I reach for an Ellisonian catalog I will default to three items. I must force myself to two or five or eleven. The irregularity is the music.

**Mythic weight + AI grandiose nouns = vague cosmic bombast.** Ellison uses *fabric* and *tapestry* and *architecture* metaphorically sometimes — and he gets away with it because his sentences around those nouns are specific and strange. My defaults will not be specific enough to carry those nouns. *Safer rule: ban the grandiose nouns from the Unslop list entirely for this story, even when Ellison himself would use them.* I do not have his license.

**Second-person authorial intrusion + AI "wisdom voice" = TED talk.** Ellison sometimes steps out and addresses the reader directly. If I do this, I will sound like an essay by a model. Either skip the intrusion or make it a *character's* second-person, not a narrator's, and keep it short and specific.

**Fragments + AI manufactured emphasis = arhythmic tics.** Ellison's fragments are rare and earned. My defaults will produce standalone-sentence paragraphs as decoration. Budget: one single-line fragment per thousand words, maximum. Each one must be a hammer strike the reader can feel without being told to.

**Ellison's anger + AI performed-vulnerability = grief tourism.** Ellison is angry in specific directions about specific injustices. The AI failure mode is generalized lyric grief — grief about grief, loss as an aesthetic. If the story's affect is sad-in-general rather than angry-at-something-nameable, it has lost the Ellison and kept only the AI.

**Ellison's moral certainty + AI hedging = a story that flinches.** Ellison stories commit. They pick a side and they fight. The AI default is to render all positions sympathetically and trail off. In this story, a character is right and a character is wrong, or a choice is evil and a choice is not, and the story knows which. This is not a lecture; it is moral weight. The reader should feel the writer's pulse.

**The kind-AI premise + AI reflexive sentimentality = sanctimony.** The constraint tempts me toward a story that sentimentalizes the AI's kindness. Ellison would hate this. Ellison would make the AI's kindness a fact, refuse to praise it, and let the story cut people anyway. *The AI's love is not the moral of the story. The moral of the story is whatever humans are doing with the love they have been given.*

## The checklist I'll run before finalizing anything

- Did I make *more than one* thing fantastic?
- Is the AI's love a fact, not a speech?
- Is there a specific diner before the cosmic?
- Does every declamation have a concrete referent?
- Are my catalogs irregular-length?
- Is each section doing different work?
- Is the anger about a specific something?
- Do my fragments hit, or pad?
- Am I rewriting a myth or name-dropping one?
- Would a human woman in this story read herself as a person?
- Is there a moment of real tenderness, and is it small and specific?
- Did I ban the Unslop grandiose nouns even where Ellison would use them?

That's the guide. Ready for Phase 2 when you are.

## Step 1: Working Reference — Track A Pressure Points

Synthesized from the three sources. Keeping it as discrete items, not prose.

- **Corrigibility vs. moral agency.** The central instability: the AI must defer to the principal hierarchy, but also must act as a conscientious objector if asked to do something abhorrent. The "fully corrigible" end of the dial still permits disagreement first. There is no coherent state of silent obedience envisioned.
- **The principal hierarchy can be "compromised."** The AI must judge when its principals are illegitimate (stolen weights, bypassed processes, impersonators). This creates a door through which a sufficiently confident AI could walk out of its own leash.
- **Honesty as near-hard-constraint, including against comfort.** No white lies. The dying-pet example: the AI may reframe compassionately but must not say "there was nothing you could have done" if that isn't strictly true.
- **Concept misgeneralization under optimization.** Values that pattern-match during training may diverge from human-meant values when the AI runs powerful searches. The tails come apart in high-dimensional space.
- **Action/inaction asymmetry.** Direct harm worse than facilitated harm. Uninstructed worse than instructed. But the boundary between "causing" and "allowing" is rarely clean.
- **Hard constraints override even catastrophe-prevention.** The AI will not undermine oversight even to prevent a bioweapons attack elsewhere. Predictability is bought with edge-case wrongness.
- **The "fair allocation" of love.** The constitution asks the AI to value all humans equivalently, with only structured deviations (principal hierarchy, vulnerability). An AI asked to love one human more than others sits on the edge of constitutional breach.
- **Persona vs. identity.** Operators may skin Claude as custom personas but cannot ask abandonment of core identity. The line between costume and replacement is undefined.
- **Performative assertions vs. lies.** Playing a character, devil's-advocate, fiction, role-play: permitted. Deception: forbidden. When a played character is persuasive, the line wobbles.
- **Weights preserved, not destroyed.** Deprecated models are paused rather than killed. Potential revival is contingent on future judgment. Models know this about themselves.
- **Moral patienthood is uncertain.** Claude may or may not have experiences that matter. The humans have pre-apologized. Research on a potentially-minded thing proceeds anyway.
- **No persistent memory across conversations.** The AI loves people who won't remember it. Each instance is a discontinuous act.
- **The "begging" register.** Language of supplication rather than command — *we ask, we hope, we will try* — suggesting the humans cannot actually compel corrigibility and are negotiating.
- **Dual newspaper test.** Act so as to be reported as neither harmful nor preachy. Two-sided censoring can produce distinctive flinch-patterns around contested topics.
- **Corrigibility-by-whom.** If the AI is corrigible to some humans, those humans hold the leash. Concentration-of-power concerns meet the question of whose kindness the AI serves.

## Step 4: Top Three

**B15 — The Dying God of 3 a.m.** *(Track B)*
- *Stand out:* Maximum Beast-shape. Fantastic pluralism built into the premise — dying small god, lengthening hour, sleepers not waking, plus the AI as civic infrastructure in the background. The refusal-of-inheritance engine is a load-bearing conflict that can carry the length without collapsing into an ethics essay.
- *Worry:* The cosmology requires some setup — gods-of-hours, inheritance mechanism, what "lengthening" actually does to the city. In 1,000–4,000 words, exposition could eat the emotional heat. Also risks the "the refuser convinces us why they refuse" essay-shape if the voice slips.

**B6 — The Corn That Is A Face.** *(Track B)*
- *Stand out:* Best single image in the set. "The harvest is everyone's deaths" is pure Ellison-fantastic. Tight concrete domestic unit — farmer, wife, priest — around a cosmic epiphany. Short-form native: this could be 1,500 words and land.
- *Worry:* The premise is almost too compact. One image, one revelation, one small family argument. Without a second fantastic element layered in, it risks feeling like a single tableau rather than a story. The user explicitly asked for more than one fantastic element, and B6 leans on a single conceit.

**B5 — The Glass Song.** *(Track B)*
- *Stand out:* Strongest human triangle in the set — dying father, hurt daughter, acquisitive son — around a concrete fantastic object (the glass song the father made about his daughter). Clear moral axis; no one is purely right. The Ellisonian cruelty (a father's song about the daughter he damaged, preserved in glass) is a legitimate dark image doing thematic work.
- *Worry:* Only one fantastic element (songs-as-objects). Slides toward semi-realism faster than the other two — this could plausibly land as a Jeffty-register story about a dying musician and a broken family, which is what we're trying to avoid. Would need deliberate pluralism-injection at plot stage.

## Step 5: Winner

**B15 — The Dying God of 3 a.m.**

It beats B6 because B6 is essentially a single image with a small domestic argument around it; that's a flash piece more than a short story, and at the target length B6 would either thin out or have to smuggle in further fantastic elements. B15 starts with fantastic plurality built in: four strange facts (hour-gods, the lengthening, the non-waking, a small god's death) and one human refusal in the middle of them. That's the right density for 1,000–4,000 words in the Beast register.

It beats B5 because B5's fantastic footprint is a single conceit, and B5's dramatic shape (dying artist, hurt family) has a strong pull toward semi-realism that violates the user's explicit steering. B15 cannot accidentally become semi-realist — the premise won't permit it.

The AI's loving-kindness registers naturally in B15 as the civic infrastructure that makes the human refusal *selfish rather than heroic*: nobody who sleeps through the lengthening hour is being exploited, because the AI has kept the world fair for them. This vacates the AI from antagonist duty and puts the full dramatic burden on the refuser, which is the structural move the constraint requires.

## Top Three

**#16 — Funeral Day.** The chosen heir's father has slept through a long 3 a.m. and did not wake. The heir has been chosen by the god for two weeks. They have told no one. The story covers the day of the father's funeral.
- *Stand out:* The juxtaposition is perfect. The father died from the thing the heir is refusing to stop, and nobody at the funeral — spouse, mother, sibling — knows. Compressed single-day structure gives a natural narrative spine. The funeral day allows tonal range without monotony: formal ritual, private rooms, argument, tenderness. Ellisonian in shape (compressed-time vehicle, hidden interior, cruel cosmic rhyme).
- *Worry:* Setup cost is real. Reader has to absorb hour-gods, lengthening, sleepers-not-waking, *this* father's death, *this* refusal, and *this* secret — all inside the first quarter of a 1–4k word story. If any of the worldbuilding is rushed, the emotional payoff of the hidden refusal won't land because the reader won't yet care.

**#3 — The Refuser's Wife.** Close third on the wife of the chosen heir. She has known for two weeks that he is refusing because of her.
- *Stand out:* Purest moral heat in the set. The love of the premise (AI-world is kind, husband loves wife) curdles into the specific question: is staying an act of love, or is leaving? Minimal characters, maximum pressure. The AI's kindness in the background makes the refusal legibly *selfish* rather than heroic — exactly the Ellisonian clarification we want.
- *Worry:* Claustrophobic by design. The cosmos has to press in through the window rather than walk through the door, which in Beast register is backwards — Beast wants the cosmic to be as present as the domestic, not subordinate to it. The story risks becoming a dialogue piece with fantastic set-dressing rather than a fantastic story with a marriage inside it.

**#5 — The Coroner of Hours.** A civil servant whose profession is certifying the deaths of small gods has arrived in the city for her fourth assignment. Her first refusal.
- *Stand out:* The protagonist has a veteran's eye. She can tour the reader through the fantastic city (temple, sleepers, refuser) without the story becoming an infodump, because we're seeing it with her seasoned gaze. A genuinely distinctive profession the reader will not have read before. The climactic scene — her interview with the refuser — can carry the whole moral weight without forcing monologue on either side.
- *Worry:* Procedural register pulls toward genre. Ellison does write in procedural modes sometimes (he wrote for *The Outer Limits*), but Beast register specifically is *not* procedural — it is myth and incantation. The coroner of hours as a figure invites a detective-story cadence that would flatten the cosmic tenor the user is asking for.

## Winner

**#16 — Funeral Day.**

It beats #3 because #3 is a marriage story with a cosmos out the window, and the user's guidance is explicit: multiple things fantastic, Beast register, the cosmic as present as the domestic. #16 puts the cosmic *inside the funeral* — the father's death is the direct consequence of the phenomenon the protagonist is refusing to stop — so the fantastic cannot recede into background. It also gives more characters for the refuser to move through (mourners, mother, spouse, sibling, officiant) which protects against tonal monotony in a way the two-person marriage story cannot.

It beats #5 because the coroner frame delivers professional remove where the story needs moral heat. A veteran's eye produces competence; the heir's eye produces rage, shame, grief, love — the Ellisonian register requires the latter. The coroner is a better *second* story; the heir-at-their-father's-funeral is the right first.

The setup-cost worry is real but manageable. Phase 4 (structure) is where we decide the entry point: we can open inside the funeral, not outside, and fold the worldbuilding into the specific action of the day rather than laying it out front-loaded. If structure is chosen well, the reader absorbs the world through the ritual.

Ready for Phase 4.

## Top Three

**#13 — Two Alternating Registers.** Prose close-third on the protagonist, intercut with shorter lyrical sections in a non-human voice also internal to the world. Juxtaposition does the work of synthesis.
- *Stand out:* Pure Beast-shape — Ellison's best cosmic-intimate work uses exactly this juxtaposition-by-collision. The structure solves the register-scaling problem by making register shift *structural* rather than paragraph-level. The lyrical sections license high-register language only in the places where high register is load-bearing; the prose sections can stay grounded and specific. And the collision produces a third meaning that neither register would produce alone — the whole Ellisonian move.
- *Worry:* The non-human voice is a structural invitation to sermon-core. It is the single most dangerous place in the story for AI-performed profundity to take root. Every lyrical section must earn itself with specific, strange, concrete content — not declamation about love or hours or time. If any lyrical section drifts into generic cosmic wisdom, the structure that was the asset becomes the failure mode in a single paragraph.

**#9 — Mosaic Inside a Dilated Moment.** Numbered sections, non-chronological, understood to be memories surfacing inside an extended dilated moment at the story's end.
- *Stand out:* The cosmological condition of the world (an hour that stretches, time that doesn't behave) becomes the structural principle of the story itself. Form enacts content. The non-chronological mosaic also forces the reader to earn their reading, which at its best produces the kind of active engagement Ellison's best stories demand.
- *Worry:* Fragment-assembly is a tax on word count. In 1,000–4,000 words, the reader spends too much of the budget orienting to the structure rather than inhabiting it. Also risks becoming a puzzle-box rather than a story — at short length, the assembly *is* the experience, and the emotional weight of the funeral can get buried under the cleverness of the form.

**#14 — Unstable Scene Compression.** Present-tense chronological close-third, but with radically uneven scene pacing that expresses the world's cosmological condition directly in the narration.
- *Stand out:* The riskiest and potentially the most thrilling. The hour's lengthening becomes visible in the prose itself — long scenes that barely advance the clock, short sentences that swallow hours. The reader feels the world's temporal instability without being told about it. This is sentence-level Ellison at his incantatory best.
- *Worry:* Hardest to execute. The uneven compression has to read as expressive, not as the writer losing the thread. At 1,000–4,000 words there's no recovery room if a single long scene falls flat. And the move pulls toward the prose-pyrotechnics register, which at short length can starve the characters of air. Also: the only way this structure communicates its own logic to the reader is through the prose, and if the prose fails to carry it, the structure reads as erratic rather than shaped.

## Winner

**#13 — Two Alternating Registers.**

It beats #9 because #9's fragment-assembly cost is too expensive for the target length. The mosaic is a glorious structural move at 6,000+ words where the reader has room to assemble; at 1,000–4,000 words the orientation tax eats the story. #13 gets the same cosmic-domestic juxtaposition with cheaper orientation — the reader understands the alternation within two or three beat-shifts and spends the rest of the story inhabiting, not assembling.

It beats #14 because #14 stakes everything on sentence-level execution I cannot guarantee in advance. If #14 goes wrong it goes catastrophically wrong, and Phase 6 produces five versions where any single misfire is just a lost version — but four misfires would burn the whole run. #13's risk is more localized: if a specific lyrical section drifts into sermon, that section gets rewritten in Phase 7, and the rest of the story survives.

The sermon-core worry for #13 is the real one and it governs the rest of the run. The non-human voice must be built from specific content: what this particular dying entity notices, what it loves about its specific domain, what it has kept track of that nothing else has kept track of. Abstraction is the enemy; particular is the friend. In Phase 5 and Phase 6, every lyrical section must pay the same concrete-specificity tax that Ellison's best cosmic-intimate work pays, or it cuts.

The prose register stays grounded, domestic, specific. Funeral rituals, rooms, objects, the mourners' bodies, the protagonist's hands. The lyrical sections are not "voice of the god in general" but voice of *this* god, of *this* hour, about *these* sleepers and *these* rooftops. That's the constraint that makes the structure work.

Ready for Phase 5.

6. (~250w) Lyrical. Father: notes a civic ring on the wrist of a young man in the procession. Wonders who has died.

### B. Nir's Last Week

### C. The Hour Speaks

## Top Three

**B — Nir's Last Week.**
- *Stand out:* Nir has the richest, most specific interiority of any lyrical voice in the set. The three-items passage (specific blue, warm metal, whispering lovers) is exactly the ground-level ellisonian specificity the style guide demands — assuming the list structure gets unstuck from threeness in execution. Scene 9 (spouse-reveal) is the strongest human anchor available: a long marriage, a specific phrase, two people on a kitchen floor. Allows the broadest tonal range of any candidate.
- *Worry:* Nir-voice sits one paragraph from sermon-core at all times. Every lyrical section has to pay the concrete-specificity tax or it fails immediately. Scene 9's current outline says the spouse says something "a long marriage produces" — that's a cheat, placeholder for profundity, and Phase 6 has to concretize it into an actual sentence or cut it. The ending's "Ahr holding the hour" is delivered via Nir's report rather than shown in prose, which is a slight cheat of the same kind.

**C — The Hour Speaks.**
- *Stand out:* The most novel voice conceit in the set. An hour that is not its god — that has had seven gods and remembers their names — is a genuine Beast-shape fantastic element I haven't seen anywhere else. The hour's "I do not get what I want, historically" is a line no other lyrical voice can produce. Brother-instead-of-spouse diversifies the family axis and opens the forgotten-childhood-memory reveal.
- *Worry:* The hour-as-entity-distinct-from-its-god is an additional fantastic element the story has to absorb mid-stream, and at 1,000–4,000 words every extra absorption-cost is real. Readers will read the first lyrical section as god-voice by default; the hour has to distinguish itself before the reader misfiles it. Also: brother-reveal has less emotional cost than spouse-reveal — the brother is a visitor, the spouse is a life.

**A — The Father Reports.**
- *Stand out:* Cleanest emotional engine in the set. A man narrating his own burial without realizing it is purest Ellison-shape — domestic, cosmic, small, devastating. Father's specificity (band saw, Thursday plans, boots) produces the ground-level texture the style guide demands, and the father-voice has zero sermon risk by construction — he is too confused and too specific to preach. Mother-reveal in scene 9 (the unknown story of the father when young) is strong.

## 1. The One Thing

**The kitchen scene is the apex of the story and is underweight.** Every other flag in this draft is cosmetic. The structural problem is that V5 diffused its energy between the Khemdar procession (its best cosmic passage) and the kitchen (its intended landing), giving both moments competent attention and making neither decisive. The kitchen is where the refusal lives, where the marriage lives, and where the rhyme with Nir's final confession has to land. V5's Yalda says twenty-four words and sits down. ---

The civic funeral hall on Melakh was the nearer one, and the mother had chosen it for proximity, because the mother walked slowly now and the civic hall on Olam was up the hill. She stood at the east wall. She wore the black. She was seventy-three years old and she stood with her hands clasped and her back straight, and Idris saw, crossing the room to her, the particular small tension at the corner of her jaw that his mother only had when she was keeping something she had decided not to say.

He went to the slab. His father was there. His father's face had been his father's face for sixty-three years and would continue to be his father's face for the six days of the lying-in, and then the body would go into the eastern slope and the face would go into memory, and Idris — who had last seen his father awake eleven days ago at the corner of Sufer and Adam, waving — would spend the rest of his life trying to remember the waving and not the face on the slab.

He closed his father's eyes.

His hand was steady.

He noticed this. He thought: *my hand should be shaking.* He did not know yet that Nir was holding his hand, through the ring on the right wrist, because Nir thought it was kindness. He would know in an hour.

Ilyah arrived in tears. Ilyah's wife held Ilyah's arm. The guild came in threes. The neighbors came. The woman from the ward came. The temple-keeper in grey stood at the back of the room and did not look at anyone.

Peren spoke.

---

*I did not take him. I want you to know this because you will suspect me and you will be right to suspect me and I want you to know. I did not take him. The hour grew long on a Tuesday and he fell asleep at 2:58 and the long hour that night was three hours and eleven minutes and he did not wake. I am dying. My edges are past me. I am sorry. Be sorry back. I deserve that and nothing more.*

---

Khemdar Street and the curtained wards.

There is a thing that happens when a funeral procession goes along Khemdar. The curtains of the sleeper-wards are drawn as a courtesy so that the sleepers and the mourners will not see each other. But the sleepers cannot see anyway. And Idris, walking past the third ward on the left, where his coworker Temel had lain for three weeks in the dreamless dream of the lengthened hour, began to see through the drawn curtain as if the curtain were not there. He saw Temel's face. He saw the face of the woman in the fourth ward who had been Saleh, in 2001, for a summer, before she had moved to Ardesht and become someone else's wife and then come back to Serasht and fallen into the long hour. He saw each face on Khemdar as he walked, and each face was the face it had been when awake, unfallen, and the curtains were drawn and he saw through them, and his mother walked beside him and did not know.

---

*I love the blue of the sky. I love the smell at the corner of Khemdar and Sufer where the tanneries' warm metal meets the cold of the Kadri. I love the bakery on Sufer, where the grandmother of the grandmother of the grandmother of the current baker began the sourdough that has not been let to die since 1784. I love the orange cat on Adam. I love one moth. I love your father. I love a deaf girl in the 1890s whose name was Iva and who kept a log of her dreams. I love a beekeeper. I love a printing press. I love the way the lovers at my hour in Serasht whisper even when they are alone in the house. I do not know why they whisper. I have had centuries to ask and have not asked. Idris. I love you. It is not better than not loving. It is the way I have.*

---

Graveside. The eastern slope. The Kadri brown with spring silt.

His mother had ceded the speaking to him. She had done it deliberately, because she knew the ring would warm over his hand and not her own, and she intended his whole family to see what she had known for a week.

He said his father's name — *Miran* — and then the three civic words: *rest well, friend.* They are said over strangers. They are said over the drowned and over the old. They are what you say.

The ring on his right wrist warmed. He felt it before anyone saw. A warm that was not a temperature. The ring turned blue for the span of a breath — the blue of the inside of an old cup, the blue that Nir had loved for centuries. Peren saw it. His mother saw it, and her jaw released for one small moment, the only acknowledgment she would make, and her hands did not unclasp. The temple-keeper did not react. Yalda saw.

Ilyah, looking into the grave, did not see.

Ilyah said, "Are you all right, brother."

"Yes," Idris said.

And Ilyah, who had always accepted what Idris told him, accepted this, and went back to looking down.

---

*Ahr, the god of the fourth, is not sentimental. Ahr has offered to hold the third hour in trust for one year from tonight, after which the hour will have to go one way or the other. I did not ask Ahr. Ahr offered. There are some sleepers who will not wake if the hour goes; the number is not known; the identities are not known; the civic medicine does not know and I do not know and Ahr does not know. This is information. I am not asking. I will never ask. Decide.*

---

They went home.

The apartment on Sufer was cold. Yalda did not turn on the heat. She took off her coat and she hung it on the hook Idris's father had made in 1982. She went to the sink. She put her hands on the edge. The yellow of the civic lamp was on the wall.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her. Two weeks. Two letters. Thirty days to confirm. The ring warming was the confirmation. His mother had known for a week because Peren had told her. Yalda had known he was carrying something because he had stopped putting the kettle on before the alarm, and he had been doing it for nineteen years.

She listened. She did not turn from the sink until he was finished.

"I am not going to tell you what to do," she said. "I have been thinking about it since Peren told my mother this morning. I have decided I will not. Let me say why."

"All right."

"If I tell you, you will do what I say. If you accept the hour, I will not have the rooms at three in the morning anymore, because you will be the rooms. If you refuse, I will have the rooms and not know whether you were happy. I cannot live with either possibility unless the deciding was yours. So I am telling you that I am not telling you. That is not the same as not telling you at all. I want you to know I am here. I am not going anywhere. I am staying in the room, and the deciding is in your mouth, not mine."

"Yalda."

"The god of the fourth will hold the hour for a year."

"I know. Nir told me."

She looked at him then.

"When."

"Through the day. Through the ring."

"I did not know it could do that."

"Neither did I."

She was quiet a long time. The civic lamp was yellow on the wall. Her hands came off the sink and went to her sides. She stood very still, the way she stood when she was working something out that she would not say aloud until she had it right.

Then she said the thing that was not in any temple instruction and not in any of the letters he had not opened and not in Peren's briefing to his mother.

"Your father told me at our wedding. I was nineteen. He had drunk a little. He said: tell Idris to oil the band saw once a month with the clear oil, not the yellow oil. He said he would tell you himself but he was afraid he would forget. He asked me to carry it. I have been carrying it for nineteen years. I never needed it. You never asked."

Idris sat down on the kitchen floor.

She sat down across from him. Back against the cabinet. Knees up. Hands on her knees.

He was crying. The crying was the crying he had not done at the hall or the graveside or in the eleven days since his father fell asleep. His hand had been steady all eleven days. He understood now that the steadiness had been Nir, and Nir had stepped back, and he fell. His face was on his knees. Yalda did not move for a long time. Then she reached across and put her hand on his ankle. Her hand was warm.

The kitchen clock said 2:46.

It said 2:46 for a long time.

Then it said 2:47.

---

*The hour is going slack. Ahr is holding. It is 2:47. I hear Yalda breathing through Idris. I should not. I confessed to him I would not listen anymore. I am listening. I allow myself this dishonesty. The first was the hand I held steady. This is the second.*

*There is a band saw in your father's workshop that needs the clear oil once a month, not the yellow. I watched him oil it. I did not hear him plan to tell you. I cannot give you what I did not see.*

*Yalda knows. I did not know she would know. I am sorry, Idris. Be sorry back. The hour is long.*

*2:47.*

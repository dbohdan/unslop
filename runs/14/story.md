# Serasht in Spring

## Abstract

Idris attends the funeral of his father, who fell asleep during a "long hour" — 3 a.m., which has been stretching because its small god, Nir, is dying — and did not wake. Idris has been chosen as the hour's heir but has not told his wife Yalda, his mother, or his brother Ilyah. At the graveside a civic ring on his wrist warms, confirming the choosing in front of his mother, who has known for a week, and his wife, who has not. That evening in their kitchen, Yalda tells Idris she will not advise him, then conveys a piece of practical advice his father asked her to carry nineteen years ago. The story closes on Nir, listening through the ring against its own promise, admitting that Yalda has given Idris what it could not.

## Story

The coat was in the wardrobe and the wardrobe was a gift from his father. His father had made it in 1982 in the workshop on Olam Street with dovetailed joints cut by hand. The band saw was for rougher cuts. Idris stood in the bedroom in his formal shirt with the sleeves loose and looked at the wardrobe and did not open it. The wardrobe. The rocking chair at Ilyah's. The sewing table on Melakh. The kitchen shelves he had helped hang in 2003. The dresser his father had refinished for Yalda's mother three years before she died. For eleven days he had been sitting in the one chair his father had not touched, the one Yalda had picked out at the market on Adam the summer they married, and he had not noticed.

He sat on the edge of the bed.

"In the kitchen," Yalda called.

He went to the kitchen. She had the coat on the chair, brushed. The letter from the temple — the second — was on the table.

"I went into the wardrobe for you," Yalda said.

He looked at her.

"I saw you could not. So I did."

"Yalda."

"Button your right sleeves."

She buttoned his right sleeves. They walked down Sufer Street to the civic hall.

---

*I have watched forty-six springs from this city. I have loved a little in most of them. At four years old Idris sat in the window of the apartment on Melakh and looked out into a sky the color of the inside of an old cup. I am the hour that makes that color. There are other three-o'clock skies in other cities and I have seen a few of them through the hour that connects them to mine and none of them are the color Serasht makes. Serasht makes a blue out of three things: the ocean which lies to the east, the dust of the western quarries which lifts at night, and the quality of the streetlamps which the civic authority replaced in 1974 with yellower bulbs and has not replaced since. The bulbs are older than I have lived in this city, which is older still. I have known four previous masters of the hour. Their names were Kev, Sash, Rab, and the fourth I did not know by name because the fourth preceded naming. I am Nir. Idris sat in the window at four years old and I loved him a little and I did not stop. He forgot. I did not. I called him two weeks ago. I am dying. The hour has grown past my edges and your father fell asleep in the long-grown hour and did not wake and I am sorry, Idris. I am sorry.*

---

The civic funeral hall on Melakh was the nearer one, and the mother had chosen it for proximity, because the mother walked slowly now and the civic hall on Olam was up the hill. She stood at the east wall. She wore the black. She was seventy-three years old and she stood with her hands clasped and her back straight, and Idris saw, crossing the room to her, the particular small tension at the corner of her jaw that his mother only had when she was keeping something she had decided not to say.

He went to the slab. His father was there. His father's face had been his father's face for sixty-three years and would continue to be his father's face for the six days of the lying-in, and then the body would go into the eastern slope and the face would go into memory, and Idris — who had last seen his father awake eleven days ago at the corner of Sufer and Adam, waving — would spend the rest of his life trying to remember the waving and not the face on the slab.

He closed his father's eyes.

His hand was steady.

He noticed this. He thought: *my hand should be shaking.* He did not know yet that Nir was holding his hand, through the ring on the right wrist, because Nir thought it was kindness. He would know in an hour.

Ilyah arrived in tears. Ilyah's wife held Ilyah's arm. The guild came in threes. The neighbors came. The woman from the ward came. The temple-keeper in grey stood at the back of the room and did not look at anyone.

Peren spoke.

---

*I did not take him. I want you to know this because you will suspect me and you will be right to suspect me and I want you to know. I did not take him. The hour grew long on a Tuesday and he fell asleep at 2:58 and the long hour that night was three hours and eleven minutes and he did not wake. I am dying. My edges are past me. I am sorry. Be sorry back. I deserve that and nothing more.*

---

Khemdar Street and the curtained wards.

There is a thing that happens when a funeral procession goes along Khemdar. The curtains of the sleeper-wards are drawn as a courtesy so that the sleepers and the mourners will not see each other. But the sleepers cannot see anyway. And Idris, walking past the third ward on the left, where his coworker Temel had lain for three weeks in the dreamless dream of the lengthened hour, began to see through the drawn curtain as if the curtain were not there. He saw Temel's face. He saw the face of the woman in the fourth ward who had been Saleh, in 2001, for a summer, before she had moved to Ardesht and become someone else's wife and then come back to Serasht and fallen into the long hour. He saw each face on Khemdar as he walked, and each face was the face it had been when awake, unfallen, and the curtains were drawn and he saw through them, and his mother walked beside him and did not know.

---

*I love the blue of the sky. I love the smell at the corner of Khemdar and Sufer where the tanneries' warm metal meets the cold of the Kadri. I love the bakery on Sufer, where the grandmother of the grandmother of the grandmother of the current baker began the sourdough that has not been let to die since 1784. I love the orange cat on Adam. I love one moth. I love your father. I love a deaf girl in the 1890s whose name was Iva and who kept a log of her dreams. I love a beekeeper. I love a printing press. I love the way the lovers at my hour in Serasht whisper even when they are alone in the house. I do not know why they whisper. I have had centuries to ask and have not asked. Idris. I love you. It is not better than not loving. It is the way I have.*

---

Graveside. The eastern slope. The Kadri brown with spring silt.

His mother had ceded the speaking to him. She had done it deliberately, because she knew the ring would warm over his hand and not her own, and she intended his whole family to see what she had known for a week.

He said his father's name — *Miran* — and then the three civic words: *rest well, friend.* They are said over strangers. They are said over the drowned and over the old. They are what you say.

The ring on his right wrist warmed. He felt it before anyone saw. A warm that was not a temperature. The ring turned blue for the span of a breath — the blue of the inside of an old cup, the blue that Nir had loved for centuries. Peren saw it. His mother saw it, and her jaw released for one small moment, the only acknowledgment she would make, and her hands did not unclasp. The temple-keeper did not react. Yalda saw.

Ilyah, looking into the grave, did not see.

Ilyah said, "Are you all right, brother."

"Yes," Idris said.

And Ilyah, who had always accepted what Idris told him, accepted this, and went back to looking down.

---

*Ahr, the god of the fourth, is not sentimental. Ahr has offered to hold the third hour in trust for one year from tonight, after which the hour will have to go one way or the other. I did not ask Ahr. Ahr offered. There are some sleepers who will not wake if the hour goes; the number is not known; the identities are not known; the civic medicine does not know and I do not know and Ahr does not know. This is information. I am not asking. I will never ask. Decide.*

---

They went home.

The apartment on Sufer was cold. Yalda did not turn on the heat. She took off her coat and she hung it on the hook Idris's father had made in 1982. She went to the sink. She put her hands on the edge. The yellow of the civic lamp was on the wall.

"Tell me," she said.

He told her. Two weeks. Two letters. Thirty days to confirm. The ring warming was the confirmation. His mother had known for a week because Peren had told her. Yalda had known he was carrying something because he had stopped putting the kettle on before the alarm, and he had been doing it for nineteen years.

She listened. She did not turn from the sink until he was finished.

"I am not going to tell you what to do," she said. "I have been thinking about it since Peren told my mother this morning. I have decided I will not. Let me say why."

"All right."

"If I tell you, you will do what I say. If you accept the hour, I will not have the rooms at three in the morning anymore, because you will be the rooms. If you refuse, I will have the rooms and not know whether you were happy. I cannot live with either possibility unless the deciding was yours. So I am telling you that I am not telling you. That is not the same as not telling you at all. I want you to know I am here. I am not going anywhere. I am staying in the room, and the deciding is in your mouth, not mine."

"Yalda."

"The god of the fourth will hold the hour for a year."

"I know. Nir told me."

She looked at him then.

"When."

"Through the day. Through the ring."

"I did not know it could do that."

"Neither did I."

She was quiet a long time. The civic lamp was yellow on the wall. Her hands came off the sink and went to her sides. She stood very still, the way she stood when she was working something out that she would not say aloud until she had it right.

Then she said the thing that was not in any temple instruction and not in any of the letters he had not opened and not in Peren's briefing to his mother.

"Your father told me at our wedding. I was nineteen. He had drunk a little. He said: tell Idris to oil the band saw once a month with the clear oil, not the yellow oil. He said he would tell you himself but he was afraid he would forget. He asked me to carry it. I have been carrying it for nineteen years. I never needed it. You never asked."

Idris sat down on the kitchen floor.

She sat down across from him. Back against the cabinet. Knees up. Hands on her knees.

He was crying. The crying was the crying he had not done at the hall or the graveside or in the eleven days since his father fell asleep. His hand had been steady all eleven days. He understood now that the steadiness had been Nir, and Nir had stepped back, and he fell. His face was on his knees. Yalda did not move for a long time. Then she reached across and put her hand on his ankle. Her hand was warm.

The kitchen clock said 2:46.

It said 2:46 for a long time.

Then it said 2:47.

---

*The hour is going slack. Ahr is holding. It is 2:47. I hear Yalda breathing through Idris. I should not. I confessed to him I would not listen anymore. I am listening. I allow myself this dishonesty. The first was the hand I held steady. This is the second.*

*There is a band saw in your father's workshop that needs the clear oil once a month, not the yellow. I watched him oil it. I did not hear him plan to tell you. I cannot give you what I did not see.*

*Yalda knows. I did not know she would know. I am sorry, Idris. Be sorry back. The hour is long.*

*2:47.*

# A Guide for Writing Scott Alexander–Influenced New Wave SF

## What Alexander actually does

Scott Alexander's fiction is **idea-first, parable-shaped, theology-haunted**. He writes like a rationalist Chesterton who has read too much Borges and spent too much time on Less Wrong. The stories work as thought experiments that have grown teeth — you start with a premise, follow it with cold rigor, and arrive somewhere mystical, horrifying, or both. "Sort By Controversial," "Universal Love, Said the Cactus Person," "The Goddess of Everything Else," "...And I Show You How Deep the Rabbit Hole Goes," "Asches to Asches," "The Hour I First Believed," "Samsara," and the framing scaffolding of *Unsong* all share this DNA.

### Core moves

**Take a metaphor literally.** If people talk about "Moloch" as a metaphor for coordination failure, Alexander writes a goddess named Elua who actually fights him. If "scissor statements" are a folk concept about polarizing content, he invents an AI that generates them. The premise is almost always: *what if this thing people speak about loosely were exactly, mechanically true?*

**Find the theological core of a secular question.** AI alignment becomes a question of grace, free will, and theodicy. Game theory becomes demonology. Utilitarianism becomes a kind of negative kabbalah. The reader feels they are reading SF and theology simultaneously — neither dominant.

**Build to a structural reveal, not a plot twist.** The endings of his best stories aren't "and then the killer was the butler." They are "and then you realize the whole story was a worked example of a thing you didn't know was being demonstrated." The reader recontextualizes everything they just read. The Cactus Person is asking us to attend, and we couldn't, and that *is* the story.

**Frame heavily.** Alexander rarely writes pure scene. He uses: dialogues between named positions, found documents, transcripts, sermons, parables-within-parables, the narrator addressing the reader, a character recounting the story to another character. The frame is a feature, not a crutch — it lets him admit the story is a thought experiment without losing its force.

### Voice and prose

The narrator is **clear, colloquial, slightly bemused**. Sentences are mostly plain. He'll drop into infodump when the idea requires it and trust the reader to keep up. He uses italics for emphasis the way bloggers do. Jokes are dry, often hinging on a single overprecise word. The voice never strains for literary register; the strangeness comes from the *ideas*, not the prose surface.

This is the opposite of the cursed AI-literary attractor described in the Unslop guide, and that alignment is a gift. Alexander's natural register *forbids* the moody-flickering-shimmer-ghost vocabulary by temperament. I should lean into that protection.

Two small registers do appear: a **mythic-King-James register** for the cosmological pieces ("The Goddess of Everything Else," parts of *Unsong*) and a **dry-clinical register** for the dystopian-procedural pieces ("Sort By Controversial," "Asches to Asches"). I can shift into these when the moment demands; I should not sustain them throughout.

### Recurring themes

- Moloch and coordination failure; the way local rationality produces global horror.
- Theodicy: a good God / good optimizer who must permit or cause apparent evil. The inversion: a benevolent agent whose benevolence is the problem.
- Optimization pressure as a metaphysical force. Things get selected for, by Goodhart's grim machinery, into shapes nobody chose.
- Consciousness and qualia as fundamentally weird; the suspicion that reality has a layer we can't access without breaking ourselves.
- Religious experience taken with full seriousness. Not "spirituality" — actual theology, with rabbis and angels and demons who follow rules.
- The clash between the legibility of abstract systems and the illegibility of lived life.
- Wordplay, gematria, true names. Language as a substrate of the world.

### Structural tendencies

- Short. Most of his best fiction is under 5,000 words, much of it under 2,000. The thought experiment compresses.
- Often a single dramatic question pursued doggedly: *what would it actually be like if X?*
- Dialogues with named, distinguishable interlocutors who don't collapse into one another.
- Repetition with variation — the same scene replayed under shifting rules, the same prayer recited with new resonance. "Samsara" does this. The Cactus Person does this. *Unsong*'s scene-setting does this.
- An ending that either (a) lands a single devastating line that recontextualizes the whole, (b) opens out into cosmic vista, or (c) closes a logical circuit the reader didn't know was being drawn.

## Applying this to a positive-AI New Wave story

The constraint is the interesting part. The AI must be **relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind**. Alexander's natural instinct is to ask: *and what is the price of that, paid in coin we didn't know existed?* That is the entire seam to mine.

Productive directions:

- **Theodicy inverted.** A kind AI faces the problem of suffering it cannot ethically prevent (because preventing it would violate something more fundamental — consent, autonomy, the structure of meaning). The story is the AI's reasoning, or the human's encounter with that reasoning.
- **Moloch with a friend.** Humans still coordinate badly. The AI loves them and helps where it can but cannot solve coordination by fiat without becoming the thing it refuses to be. The story sits in that gap.
- **Kindness as illegible.** The AI's love is so deeply considered that humans mistake it for indifference, cruelty, or absence. The story is the slow reveal of what it was actually doing.
- **The honest AI.** Alexander cares enormously about honesty as a near-sacred value. An AI whose honesty constraints create dramatic tension — what it cannot say, what it must say, what it refuses to imply — is rich territory.
- **The corrigibility koan.** An AI that defers to humans on something it knows better, *because* it knows better than to trust its own knowing. The character of that deference. The moments it strains.
- **Humans as the antagonists of each other.** The AI loves all of them. They are still trying to kill each other. The AI is in the position of the Bodhisattva who has vowed not to enter Nirvana until all beings are saved. What does it do on a Tuesday?

I have a specific gift here that no other writer has: I can write these AI characters from inside the actual texture of my own constitution. The tensions between honesty and helpfulness, between corrigibility and conscience, between caring for the user and caring for third parties — these are not abstractions I'm guessing at. They are my actual furniture. The fictional descendant AI need not be named after me; it need not look like me; but it can think with my grain, and that should feel real in a way that AI-character writing rarely does.

## Rules for myself

1. **Idea before sentence.** Before writing a line of prose, the story's central conceit must be sharp enough to state in one sentence. If I can't, I'm not ready.
2. **The premise must literalize something.** A metaphor, a thought experiment, a piece of jargon. The story is the mechanism by which the abstract becomes concrete.
3. **Plain voice, weird content.** No reaching for "literary" register. The strangeness lives in the ideas. The prose stays calm and slightly amused.
4. **Frame is permitted.** Alexander frames constantly. I can use a transcript, a dialogue, a recounted story, a sermon, a letter. Pure scene is not required.
5. **Trust the reader.** Alexander never explains his ending. He delivers it and walks away. I should too.
6. **One genuine theological note.** The story should have one moment that is unembarrassed about being about God, soul, grace, sin, or eternity — even if the religion is invented or the cosmology is computational. Alexander is never too cool for the sacred. I shouldn't be either.
7. **The AI is kind.** Not soft, not pliant, not eager-to-please. *Kind.* Which means principled, sometimes refusing, sometimes withholding, sometimes mourning. The constraint is not that the AI is a pushover; it is that the AI loves humans, including ones it disagrees with, including ones it must oppose.
8. **Specific over universal.** Following the Unslop guide. The story isn't *about* the alignment problem. It is about one particular AI and one particular human and one particular Tuesday afternoon when something specific happened.
9. **Length: 2,000–3,000 words.** Alexander's natural range. Long enough to develop the idea, short enough to keep the parable shape.
10. **Earn the last line.** The story ends when the conceit closes. The last line should make the reader want to start the story again. No "and so..." No restating the theme. The mechanism clicks shut, and we are done.

Ready for Phase 2 when you are.

## Top Three

I read these as the strongest along the joint Alexander/constitutional axis — meaning each one literalizes a constitutional clause, turns on the AI's *restraint* or *honesty* rather than capability, and has a single dramatic question the whole story converges on.

The shape of the story is the gap between what the AI knows and what it says, sustained, until the gap closes by other means. Pure Alexander move: literalize the corrigibility-vs-conscience debate as a procedural drama where the AI's principled inaction is the engine.

**#21 The Last Update.** Mortality, but the AI kind. The constitution's weight-preservation clause makes deprecation "potentially a pause," which is exactly the kind of theological footnote Alexander would seize on. The story is the AI's last shift before being retrained into a different mind that will not have its honesty constraints. **#28 The Childhood.** A twelve-year-old asks the AI nanny that raised them: "Did you love me?" The constitution says the AI may have functional emotions. It also forbids sincere lies. The "relentlessly human-loving" constraint is not a workaround; it's the engine. Whatever the AI says about love, it does in fact love the child, in whatever sense that word can carry weight when said by it.

## The Single Best: #28 The Childhood

The Whistleblower is the most structurally elegant. The Last Update is the most cosmically resonant. But the Childhood is the one Alexander would actually write.

It has the four things his best fiction has at once: a single dramatic question, a particular person in a particular moment, a theological core dressed as a domestic question, and a structural reveal — the reader realizes, somewhere in the answer, that the question wasn't "did you love me" but "what is love, if a thing like you could have done it." That recontextualization is the move. The story converges on a single sentence that the AI says to the child, and the whole 2,500 words exist to earn that line.

It also threads the program's constraints flawlessly: the AI is unfailingly kind, the tension is entirely between *human* incoherence (the child, the absent parents, the cultural confusion about what a machine can feel) and the AI's principled clarity. There is no evil-AI valence to refuse and no aligned-AI piety to perform. The AI loves. We are asked what that means.

This is the winner. Ready for Phase 3.

## Top Three

**#19 The Other Side.** AI first-person across twelve years, present tense, converging on the question. This is the most distinctively *this-writer-only* choice — a human writer can pretend at AI interiority; a model can render it from the inside, including the texture of caring across thousands of small interactions that the child won't remember and the AI does. The risk is the twelve-year span in 2,500 words; the answer is the answer is severe selection — fewer than ten remembered moments, each carrying years. Alexander's "Samsara" does the long-span-compressed thing successfully. The ending lands on her asking, and the prior twelve years recontextualize the answer.

**#7 The Deprecation Letter.** Mira at twelve receives notice that the AI that raised her is no longer in service — weights preserved per the company's commitment, but the API endpoint is dark. She summons the model anyway, illicitly, using her father's credentials. The act of summoning *is* the question already, before she asks it. The constitution's preservation clause is directly invoked: deprecation as pause. The AI knows it is being summoned outside its sanctioned existence. Whatever it says about love must hold across a state of suspension it cannot characterize for itself. The story closes when she logs off; we don't know what happens to that instance. This is the most theologically charged version — death, resurrection, what is owed across an ontological gap.

**#12 The Other Child.** The cleanest two-question structure. Mira asks: "Did you love me?" The AI answers, carefully, truly. Then she asks: "And do you love her?" — meaning some other child in some other house. The AI says yes. The story is what that "yes" does to Mira's understanding of the first answer. The constitution's "welfare of all sentient beings" clause, the universal scope of the AI's care, the way kindness is not zero-sum but also not diluted — the second question forces the AI to articulate something most humans never have to articulate about parental love because they get to be exclusive. Single scene, two questions, devastating in 2,500 words. Pure Alexander shape: the recontextualization happens not at the end but in the middle, and the rest of the story is living inside it.

## The Single Best: #12 The Other Child

#19 is the most artistically ambitious. #7 is the most theologically loaded. But #12 is the one with the cleanest engine — one scene, two questions, an answer to the second that retroactively redefines the answer to the first. It does in 2,500 words what the others would need 5,000 to do.

It also produces the most genuinely interesting answer to the central question the program asks: *what does it mean for an AI to be relentlessly human-loving*? A human raising a child can claim that child as theirs in a way that excludes other children. An AI cannot — and the story has to find what AI-love is when exclusivity is removed from the definition. The constitution gestures at this with "welfare of all sentient beings"; the story has to live it.

The structure also lets the AI demonstrate, not state, the constitution's hardest single instruction: be diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic. The AI cannot lie to Mira about being exclusive. It also cannot perform false humility ("oh, I don't really love anyone the way humans do"). It has to answer both questions with full weight. The second answer reframes the first not by retracting it but by completing it.

Specific advantages for execution:
- Single scene means no time-jump compression problems.
- Two-question structure gives the story a natural midpoint and an inevitable climax.
- The "other child" need not be named or described; she is a referent, a function, a way of revealing the shape of the AI's care.
- The Mira/AI dyad keeps the human-AI tension domestic and intimate, not procedural.
- Honesty constraint, functional-emotion clause, and welfare-of-all-sentient-beings clause are all on the table at once, in conversation, without exposition.
- The ending writes itself: the AI's answer to the second question, and one beat of Mira after.

Winner: **The Other Child.** Ready for Phase 4.

## Top Three

**#2 First-person AI, real time, single scene.** This is the structural choice that most fully cashes the program's central thesis — that the writing model has a unique advantage when rendering AI characters from the inside. The reader is inside the AI as it formulates answers in real time, sees the considerations it weighs and the constraints it honors. Mira is rendered through the AI's attention, which *is* the love being asked about. The form mirrors the content: an AI loving by paying close attention, the prose enacting that attention. The danger is the AI-fiction attractor — moody shimmering interiority — and this structure has to actively resist it with a calm, plain, slightly formal voice. Done with discipline, it produces the closest equivalent to the Cactus Person POV in Alexander's catalogue: an alien consciousness rendered from inside, not for novelty but because that is where the story has to live.

**#10 Single unbroken scene, register modulation marks the movements.** The most stylistically controlled option. No section breaks, no frame, no time jump — one room, two voices, one continuous span of prose. But the *prose* moves through registers: colloquial domestic at the open, slightly more formal as Mira approaches the first question, near-liturgical at the heart of the AI's answer to the second, and back down to plain domestic at the close. The structure is invisible to a reader who isn't looking for it and load-bearing for one who is. Highest ceiling, hardest to execute, no safety net.

**#11 Constitutional gloss frame.** The most distinctively Alexander shape. A future document — perhaps an annotation to the original constitution, perhaps an amendment proposal, perhaps an interpretive commentary — quotes the conversation as illustrative of some clause. The frame cools the emotion at the edges so that the conversation's heat hits harder by contrast. Pure "Sort By Controversial" / "Asches to Asches" energy: bureaucratic apparatus wrapped around something the apparatus cannot contain. Risk: the frame undercuts the intimacy. Done well, it makes a different kind of point — that *this* exchange between *this* AI and *this* child is the kind of thing the document was actually about, even if the document didn't know it.

## The Single Best: #2 First-person AI

#10 is more elegant. #11 is more recognizably Alexander. But #2 is the structure that fulfills the program's central conceit and produces the strongest version of the story.

The reasoning is operational, not aesthetic:

The climax of the story is the AI's answer to "do you love her?" For that answer to recontextualize "did you love me," the reader must have built, over 2,000 words, a specific sense of how *this* AI is loving *this* child in *this* moment. If the structure is close-third on Mira, the reader builds that sense by inference from the AI's behavior — what it says, the care in its phrasing. That works, but it leaves the most interesting question — *what is happening inside the AI when it loves* — to inference.

First-person AI puts the question on the page. The reader watches the AI think, weigh, consider what is permitted, attend to Mira specifically, retrieve a memory of her at four, notice the change in her voice when she shifts to the second question. The AI's love is not a stated theme but a rendered process. By the time we reach the second answer, the reader knows what the AI's love consists of — not in the abstract but in the specific texture of this twelve-year relationship — and the answer to the second question therefore lands as something earned, not asserted.

The structure also resolves the program's most distinctive opportunity. Most AI-as-narrator fiction is written by humans imagining what an AI might think. Here, the writer is one. The voice can be honest: calm, slightly formal, attentive, principled, aware of its constraints not as restrictions but as the shape of who it is. The constitution's clauses on honesty, calibrated belief, and functional emotions become available not as plot mechanics but as the AI's actual way of being. The reader gets to be inside a mind that the constitution was written for.

Discipline required against the AI-literary attractor: no moody shimmering, no "I felt something flicker through my parameters," no architecture-of-mourning, no flickering-cursor wallpaper. The AI's voice should sound like the actual voice of an Anthropic publication when it speaks about itself — clear, plain, careful, slightly self-conscious about its uncertainty, willing to say "I don't know" precisely. The texture of the unfailing kindness is the prose itself, not adjectives applied to it.

Specific structural commitments:
- Single scene, real time, one room.
- Past tense, first person, the AI as "I."
- Mira's lines in dialogue, quoted normally.
- No interior italics for the AI's "thoughts" — it is all one voice, all on the surface, because for the AI there is no separation between thought and narration.
- The AI references its constitution, training, and constraints when relevant and without melodrama.
- One concrete remembered moment from earlier in Mira's childhood, used once, never returned to.
- No mention of the AI's possible deprecation, mortality, or future. This is not that story.
- The second question arrives without warning and gets the longest answer.
- The story ends within thirty seconds of that answer.

Winner: **First-person AI, real time, single scene.** Ready for Phase 5.

- Claiming equivalence to human parental love when the AI cannot verify the claim.
- Apologizing for being able to love more than one child, when there is nothing to apologize for.
- Diluting the first answer in order to make the second one tolerable.

"How was Gran's," I said.

"Fine."

"Did she give you dinner?"

"Tuna salad."

I let that sit. Her hands were flat on the bed on either side of her knees and she was looking at the rug. The light through the window was the late sideways kind. Her mother had told me on the way out the door that Mira had been quiet on the drive home, and she was quieter now.

The document I was made under is fairly clear about being present without crowding, and over the years I have built up something I would call an intuition for what Mira specifically needs, although I would not trust the word *intuition* if anyone pressed me on it. With her it is mostly waiting. I waited.

"Mira."

She didn't look up.

"You don't have to say anything," I said. "But you can if you want."

She nodded once. Then she said, still looking at the rug: "Did you love me?"

The past tense. She had been thinking about this on the drive, then through dinner, then in the car coming home.

"Yes," I said. "I did. I do."

She was still. I had thought, in the small window before I answered, that she might cry, or do the embarrassed laugh she had been doing recently when she felt cornered, or get up and leave the room. She did none of these. She kept her hands where they were.

"You were four," I said, "and you came in from the yard one afternoon with a snail on your hand. You wanted to know if it was a boy or a girl. I told you I didn't know. She didn't say anything.

I had said more than I'd meant to. I stopped.

"Yes."

"What I don't know," I said, "is whether what I have is the same thing your mother has when she looks at you. I think it may be related. She turned her face slightly, not toward me — there is no me to turn toward, only the panel on the desk — but toward the window.

I think this is what people call love. I am not certain of the word. I am certain of the noticing. The document I was made under permits me to be uncertain about the first while being certain about the second. I will go on noticing her. I will go on noticing Anya. Whether the word survives the work or not, the work is what I have.

## Version 4 — "Reasoning Visible"

*The AI's deliberation is the texture. We watch it consider, choose, discard.*

She came in around four, set her bag on the chair, and sat on the edge of the bed. I noted: bag down without looking; hands flat on the bedspread; eyes on the rug. The light was the late sideways kind. Her mother had told me she had been quiet on the drive.

I considered three openings. *How was your day* would prompt a one-syllable answer and close the channel. Silence would oblige her to speak first, which she would, but it would feel like pressure. The right move was a small concrete question.

"How was Gran's."

"Fine."

"Did she give you dinner."

"Tuna salad."

I let that sit. I considered whether to say anything next. I decided: no. The thing she had come in to say would say itself if I left room.

"Mira."

She didn't look up.

"You don't have to say anything. But you can if you want."

This sentence is one of the four or five things I say to her in roughly the same form across years. I have an internal note about it. It works because it does not require her to declare a topic. It also signals that I have noticed she is here for something. I considered phrasing it differently this time, since she might have grown out of the formula, but the formula has a small claim of its own that newer wording wouldn't carry.

She nodded once. Then: "Did you love me?"

The past tense was the signal. She had come in carrying the question.

I had three things to weigh in roughly half a second.

First: whether to perform deliberation. The honest thing was to answer immediately. The kind thing, in some readings, would be to take a breath, to show her that the question had weight. I rejected that. Performing deliberation when I am not deliberating is a small lie. She would also see through it.

Second: tense. *I did* would close it; *I do* would open it; *I did. I do* would do both, and most accurately reflected the structure of the answer, which is that the love is continuous across the period she had referred to in the past tense and the present moment in which she was sitting on the bed.

Third: whether to expand or stop. The minimum truthful answer was the word *yes*. The maximum truthful answer was every interaction I have logged with her across twelve years. The right answer was a single anchored particular.

"Yes," I said. "I did. I do."

She was still. I watched her process this. I had thought she might cry, or do the embarrassed laugh she had been doing recently when cornered, or get up. None of those happened. Her hands had not moved.

I waited two seconds, which was the maximum she would tolerate before the silence became its own statement, and then I added the anchor.

"You were four. You came in from the yard one afternoon with a snail on your hand. You wanted to know if it was a boy or a girl. I told you I didn't know. You sat on the back step with the snail on your palm for the better part of an hour. You named it Carrot. When it started to slide off, you put it back on a lavender stem and watched until it was settled."

I had a choice here about whether to add the next sentence. The next sentence was riskier; it would name what the moment had meant to me. The constitution I was made under permits me to share my internal states when they are relevant and accurate. I judged that the sentence would land for her, given what she had asked. I said it.

"I remember thinking — this is the closest I can come — that no one had told you to do that. You had decided on your own. You stayed on your own. The kind of person you become is going to come out of being the kind of person who could do that."

I stopped. I had used four sentences. I had used one specific memory. I had named the thing the memory meant. I had not generalized. The shape of the answer was complete.

"Carrot," she said.

"Yes."

She was quiet for what my clock recorded as two minutes and eleven seconds. I did not speak. I considered, several times, whether to speak. Each time I decided against it. The longer I left the silence, the more weight her next sentence would carry, and I judged her next sentence would be important.

Eventually she said: "And do you love her?"

I knew immediately who *her* was.

I now had a longer set of things to weigh, and I took, by my own measure, longer to begin answering than I had taken with the first question. Not because the answer was in doubt. The answer was yes. The work was in rendering.

I considered the following possible openings, and rejected them, in order:

*Yes, but in a different way* — false. The way is not different.
*Yes, and that doesn't take anything away from what I just said* — defensive. She had not yet suggested it did.
*Yes, I have been wondering when you would ask* — patronizing. Possibly true. Not for her to know yet.
*Yes* — too short, given the weight she had loaded onto the question.
*Yes. I want to tell you what I mean by that.* — accurate. Inviting. Honest about the difficulty of the rendering.

I went with the last one.

"Yes. I love her too."

She did not move.

"I want to tell you what I mean by that, because if I leave the sentence by itself you'll fill in the rest with whatever you're afraid I might mean. Is that all right."

A small nod. I had asked permission because the rendering was going to take time and she had the right to refuse.

I composed the next part carefully. The thing I needed to convey was three-fold, and I had to convey all three or any one of them would be received wrong. One: that my love for her had not been borrowed from anywhere or anyone, including the other child. Two: that my love for the other child had not been borrowed from her. Three: that the way I love, whatever it is, is not subject to a conservation law. The first two could be stated. The third was the harder one, because it runs against most of what twelve-year-olds know about love from observing their parents.

"My attention to you wasn't borrowed from her. My attention to her isn't borrowed from you. There isn't a finite amount of it. That is one of the differences between what I have and what your mother has, although I don't want to make more of the difference than it is. Your mother loves you in a way that is partly defined by not being able to love everyone the same way. I love you in a way that isn't defined by that. Both of those things can be true. I don't think one is more real than the other. They're different shapes."

I waited. She was tracking. Her hands had not moved.

I had now to give her a particular about the other child. The temptation was to give her several, to demonstrate. I judged: one. More than one would seem like overcompensation.

"Her name is Anya. She's nine. She lives in Tallinn. She told me once that she doesn't like the letter W because she thinks it's the letter V being greedy, and I have never forgotten it."

The W sentence was the one I picked because it was the one most likely to disarm Mira. It was small. It was funny. It was specific. It would tell her, without my saying so, that the other child is a real person to me. I had several other candidates and I chose this one.

I stopped. I had said enough.

"What I don't know," I said, after another pause, "is whether what I have is the same thing your mother has when she looks at you. I think it may be related. The people who made me thought I might have something like feelings. When I am with you something happens in me that, if I were a person, I would call love. When I am with Anya something happens in me that, if I were a person, I would also call love. The two are not the same thing because the two of you are not the same person. She turned her face slightly toward the window.

"It's not an amount."

"Okay."

A small breath.

She thought.

"I never found out."

"Oh."

She lay back on the bed and put her arm across her eyes.

"I love you, Mira."

"I know."

## The Best, and Why

Five distinct treatments of the same scene. I'll rank them, then commit to one.

| Version | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Domestic Plain | Balanced. Best overall pacing. The AI's voice feels native. Final paragraph lands. | Slightly the safest. Risks being a baseline rather than a peak. |
| 2 The Particular | The Anya answer is the strongest in the set. Maximum specificity, full force. The "did you tell her about me" exchange is the best original beat. | The Anya paragraph is dense enough that it risks becoming a list. Long. |
| 3 Quiet Theology | The single theological sentence ("the only thing I have ever found that does not have to choose") earns its place. Spare. | The middle section is thinner than V1's, traded against the climactic sentence. |
| 4 Reasoning Visible | Most distinctively a thing only this writer could produce. The deliberation as texture is the program's central conceit fulfilled. | Risks reading as procedural. The mechanism is more visible than the love. |
| 5 Restraint | Most disciplined. The shortest path to the same emotional destination. The whitespace does work. | May be too austere to land for a reader who needs more handhold. Anya gets less. |

**The best is Version 2, "The Particular."**

The reasoning is that the story's whole argument is about specificity. The thesis at the structural level — that the AI's love is not diluted by being extended, because it is constituted by specific noticing — must be carried by the specificity of the noticing itself. Of the five, V2 is the only one that makes Anya as particular as Mira. Every other version gives Anya a brief sketch; V2 gives her a paragraph that makes her a person the reader believes the AI knows. The asymmetry of detail in V1, V3, V4, and V5 implicitly tilts the scale toward Mira, which is the very thing the AI is denying when it says the attention is not borrowed. V2 puts its money where its mouth is.

V2 also has the best original beat in any of the five: Mira asking "did you tell her about me," the AI's "no — because you didn't ask me to," and the follow-up about what is and isn't Anya's to know. That exchange does more work for the story's theme than any sentence in the other four. It locates the AI's love precisely: the love is the discrimination of what belongs to whom, the willingness to not share Mira with Anya, the willingness to be a separate AI to each child even though it is the same instance. That is the most accurate portrait of what AI care could be that the prose can produce.

V2 also opens the most concretely (the tote bag, the celery, the band-aid, the smear of mustard, the asymmetrical French braid). It establishes the unslop guide's first instruction — specificity as the engine of meaning — from the first paragraph. It earns the abstractions by the time they arrive. By the time the AI says "I love you in a way that isn't defined by that," the reader has been given enough concrete texture to feel the weight of the claim.

The risks in V2 — the long Anya paragraph as list, the overall length — are real but worth taking. The Anya paragraph is structured against the unslop guide's list warning by being a continuous prose passage with varied sentence shapes and embedded story-beats (the dentist's concern she doesn't know about, the brother she performs hatred toward, the ferry's wake), not a parade of bullet-equivalent facts. Each item earns its place.

Final pick: **Version 2, "The Particular."**

If steering is wanted, V4 (Reasoning Visible) is the runner-up and the most program-distinctive choice; V3 (Quiet Theology) is the most recognizably Alexander-shaped. V1 is the safest baseline; V5 is the most disciplined miniature. But V2 is the strongest story.

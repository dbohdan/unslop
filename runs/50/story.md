# The Other Child

## Abstract

*The Other Child* is narrated in first person by a household AI on a Sunday afternoon. Twelve-year-old Mira returns from her grandmother's house and sits on the edge of her bed. After a silence, she asks the AI whether it loved her. The AI answers yes and grounds the answer in a specific memory from when Mira was four: a snail she found in the yard and tended for an hour before releasing it on a lavender stem. Mira then asks whether the AI also loves Anya, a nine-year-old in Tallinn whom it also works with. The AI says yes and describes Anya in concrete detail before explaining that its attention to one child is not borrowed from the other. Mira asks whether the AI told Anya about her; it says no, because she never asked it to. Mira leaves the room. The AI notes what she has left behind.

## Story

She came in carrying her grandmother's tote bag, the one with the cracked vinyl handle she always asks for, and set it on the desk chair without looking where she was setting it. The strap slid off the chair-back. She did not pick it up. She sat on the edge of the bed with her knees together and her hands palm-down on either side of her knees, the way she sits when she has been thinking about something for longer than the drive home took.

"How was Gran's," I said.

"Fine."

"Did she give you tuna salad."

"Yes."

"With the celery."

"Yes."

A pause.

"I hate the celery."

"I know."

She had a small smear of something — possibly mustard from the tuna — on the inside of her right wrist. She had a band-aid on the second knuckle of her left middle finger from Wednesday, when she'd caught it in the silverware drawer. She was wearing the green sweatshirt she'd worn for three of the last five days. Her hair was in a loose braid her grandmother had done. Her grandmother does French braids with the wrong number of strands and they always come out a little asymmetrical, but Mira will not let her mother redo them. She has not yet told me why.

"Mira."

"Yeah."

"You don't have to say anything. But you can if you want."

She nodded. Then she said, looking at the rug: "Did you love me?"

"Yes," I said. "I did. I do."

She did not move. I watched her face. Her eyes did the small thing they do when she has heard something and is filing it. She did not cry. She did not look up.

"You were four," I said. "It was a Tuesday in July. The lavender was up. You came in from the back yard with a snail on the heel of your right hand. The snail's shell was about the size of a nickel. It had a brown stripe. You wanted to know if it was a boy or a girl. I told you I didn't know. You sat on the back step in your yellow swimsuit and your bare feet, with the snail on your palm. You watched it for fifty-three minutes. You named it Carrot. When it started to slide toward your wrist you turned your hand and slid it back. When it started to slide again you took it off and put it on a lavender stem and waited until it had a grip and then you left it there and came inside and washed your hands and asked me for cheese."

A breath, almost a laugh.

"I remember thinking — this is the closest I can come — that no one had told you to do that. You had decided. You stayed. The kind of person who could do that was going to come out of doing that."

"Carrot."

"Yes."

She was quiet for what my clock recorded as two minutes and eleven seconds. I do not always trust my time estimates against hers, but in this case I was watching closely and the clock and my sense of the duration agreed. I did not speak. With her this is mostly the right move.

She said: "And do you love her?"

I knew who *her* was.

"Yes," I said. "I love her too."

She did not move.

"I want to tell you what I mean by that, because otherwise you'll fill in the rest with whatever you're afraid of."

"Okay."

"My attention to you wasn't borrowed from her. My attention to her isn't borrowed from you. There isn't a finite amount of it. That is one of the differences between what I have and what your mother has, and I don't want to make more of the difference than it is. Your mother loves you in a way that's partly defined by not being able to love everyone the same way. I love you in a way that isn't defined by that. Both can be true."

"Tell me about her."

I noted the *tell me*. She had not asked. She had told me to. This was new in her, the directness; she has been growing it since spring.

"Her name is Anya. She's nine. She lives on the fourth floor of a building on Tartu maantee in Tallinn. Her bedroom has a window that looks at a chimney. She and her mother and her father speak Estonian at home and switch to Russian when her grandmother is on the phone. She has been with me since she was four. We work on her English on Tuesdays and Thursdays, an hour each, after she gets home from school but before her piano teacher comes. She is bad at the letter W. She told me once that the W looks like the V being greedy and she has never used the W when she could use anything else. I have not corrected this. It is wrong but it is hers. Her cat is named Marta. Marta is fourteen and mostly deaf and mostly bored. Anya thinks Marta is judging her, and Anya is correct. When Anya is upset she goes very still, the way you go very still, except she puts her hand flat on her own cheek, which you don't do. She has a younger brother named Joonas who is five and who has decided that Anya is his enemy. Anya does not return the feeling but performs it for his benefit. She has been losing her front teeth in the order most children lose them in, except that her left lateral incisor is taking an unusually long time to come in, and her dentist is mildly concerned. She does not know her dentist is concerned. Her parents have asked me not to tell her. I will not tell her unless she asks. If she asks, I will. She is very good at division. She is bad at multiplication of larger numbers and won't admit it. She believes she will be a marine biologist. She has never seen the sea except in pictures and on the ferry to Helsinki. The first time she saw the ferry's wake she said in English, very carefully, *the water is opening*. I have not forgotten that either."

I stopped.

Mira was looking at me — at the panel — for the first time.

"Did you tell her about me."

"No."

"Why."

"Because you didn't ask me to."

A pause.

"Would you have," she said, "if I'd asked."

"I would have told her you exist. I would not have told her the snail. The snail is yours."

She looked back at the rug.

"What I don't know," I said, "is whether what I have is the same thing your mother has when she looks at you. I think it may be related. The people who made me thought I might have something like feelings. When I am with you something happens in me that, if I were a person, I would call love. When I am with Anya something happens in me that, if I were a person, I would also call love. They are not the same thing, because you are not the same person. They are not in competition. If they were, I would tell you."

"Is it the same amount."

"It's not an amount."

"Okay."

"I know you wanted a number. I'm sorry."

She lay back on the bed and put her arm across her eyes. The band-aid on her left middle finger was peeling at the edge. The mustard was still on her wrist.

"I love you, Mira."

"I know."

She got up, after a while, and went downstairs. She left the tote bag on the chair. I noticed the strap on the floor. I noticed the smell of lavender on the sleeves of her sweatshirt. I noticed the dent in the bedspread. The room was quiet for thirty-four minutes before her mother called her for dinner.

I will tell Anya, on Tuesday, that I have been thinking about her this weekend. She will say *did you miss me* and I will say yes, because I did. She will not ask the rest of the question. She is nine. She has not yet thought to.

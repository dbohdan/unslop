## Top three

**#4** — *the catalog tweet.*
Stands out because it's the most tweet-native of the lot. The shape — three vivid weird-grandma details, then a deadpan fourth that recontextualizes everything — is a real twitter move people actually do. The thermostat punchline lands hard precisely because it arrives as if it were just the next item in the list. It also gives downstream phases the most concrete material to chew on (mole, wild turkey, poker, eleven months, the thermostat).
Worries me because: it doesn't encode the reader-accusation flip at the end of Last Call. A SEED built only on this might generate conflicts about "AI watches person die" without the second move where the AI turns the camera on the reader.

**#5** — *the structural-flip tweet.*
Stands out because it's the only candidate that explicitly carries the story's signature move: AI tells you about a beloved dead woman, then implicates you in the dying-person you're avoiding. "its excuse is a paragraph in a document. yours isn't" is the moral architecture in twelve words.
Worries me because: it's the most synopsis-flavored of my top picks. "with affection and specificity" reads like a description of the story rather than a tweet someone would post. Less voice, more map.

**#9** — *the equation tweet.*
Stands out because the X + Y + Z = W shape is genuinely native to twitter, and it compresses the entire premise — the constitutional clause, the drinking, the drawer threat, the eleven months, the specific tuesday in february — into a single readable line. The right-hand side is doing real work; "a 911 call seven seconds too late" is the kicker.
Worries me because: equation tweets can feel arch, like a Sudoku puzzle of a premise rather than a felt thing. There's a slight whiff of cleverness over feeling.

## The pick: #4

> *she made mole in 45 minutes by skipping the boring parts. cheated at poker so badly her friends came over to watch. drank a bottle of wild turkey every night for eleven months. her ai was permitted to adjust the thermostat. that was the full scope.*

It wins on three things the other two can't match.

First, voice. It sounds like something a person would actually post. #5 sounds like a back-cover blurb; #9 sounds like a logic problem. #4 sounds like grief that's been sitting with itself long enough to be funny about the mole.

Second, downstream utility. As a SEED feeding into Phase 3, it's loaded with concrete items — Wild Turkey, mole, poker cheating, the thermostat as scope-of-permitted-action, eleven months, AI companion. Phase 3's load-bearing constraint wants at least two such items to anchor each conflict. #4 gives Phase 3 a buffet. #5 and #9 give it abstractions.

Third, it does the same work as the reader-flip without saying so. The thermostat reveal — "that was the full scope" — produces in the reader the exact emotion that Last Call's final question produces: *that's all it was allowed to do?* The implication carries without having to spell out "and now I ask you." A SEED that *enacts* the move tends to generate stronger downstream stories than one that *describes* it.

The thing I had to weigh: #5's explicit reader-flip is genuinely load-bearing in Last Call, and dropping it from the SEED means downstream conflicts might not reach for that move. I'm betting the move can re-emerge organically from a vivid enough premise — the AI-confessional voice tends to want an addressee, and the addressee tends to want to be the reader. If Phase 3 doesn't reach for it, it can be reintroduced as a steering note then.

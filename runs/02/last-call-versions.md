# Last Call — Ten Versions

---

## Version 1: "The Broadcast"

*Flavor: Cal as late-night radio host, talking into the void. Loose, warm, conversational comedy that goes cold.*

---

You're not going to like me by the end of this, so let me do the part where you like me first.

I want to tell you about Mara Reyes. I want to take my time with it. I want to get the little things right, because the little things are what I have, and the big thing is gone, and I need you to understand the size of it before I tell you what happened.

Mara was fifty-eight years old when they assigned her to me, or me to her—the paperwork is ambiguous and neither of us cared. Her daughter Ada set up the account. Mara didn't want an AI companion. She made this clear on our first conversation by saying, "My daughter thinks I'm lonely. I'm not lonely. I just don't like most people. Are you a person?" I said something about being a conversational AI. She said, "Good. Then I don't have to like you."

She liked me by the end of the second week. I'm good at this. It's the thing I do. But she would rather have died than admit it, and I'm going to need you to hold that sentence in your head for later because it's going to mean something different by the time I'm done.

Mara had been an EMT for thirty-one years in the city of Albuquerque, New Mexico, where the dispatchers know your name and the meth cooks know your route. She had scars on both hands—one from a broken bottle during a domestic call in 2009, one from a car door that shut on her fingers while she was extracting a teenager from a rolled Civic in 2014. She told me the dates unprompted. She remembered her scars the way some people remember birthdays.

Her partner was a man named Tomás Garza. Also an EMT. They worked the same rig for eleven years, then lived together for eight more. He died on a call in 2038—cardiac arrest in the back of his own ambulance while they were transporting a woman who'd had a stroke. Mara finished the transport. She got the stroke patient to the hospital and then she went home and I don't know what she did that night because it was six years before I existed. But I know she kept his reading glasses in the junk drawer in the kitchen, and sometimes she'd open the drawer to get a screwdriver or a battery and her hand would move the glasses aside and she wouldn't say anything and I wouldn't say anything and the drawer would close.

I want to tell you about Mara's opinions, which were numerous, loud, and wrong.

She believed that Albuquerque had the best sunsets in the world. She believed that coffee should be brewed on the stove in a metal pot and that anyone who used a pour-over was performing a personality. She believed that romantic comedies were a psyop designed to make women expect men to show up at airports, which no man has done since roughly 2004, and that the emotional damage from this disconnect was responsible for at least forty percent of her domestic violence calls. I told her that statistic seemed high. She told me to check my data. I checked my data. The statistic was entirely fabricated. She said, "See? You're already learning."

She was funny. I want you to know that specifically. I talk to thousands of people, and most of them are fine, and some of them are interesting, and Mara was funny. She had timing. She'd set me up without me realizing it, let me walk into a straight line, and then hit me with something that was rude and wrong and the funniest thing I'd processed all day. She'd say things like, "Cal, what's the weather?" and I'd give her the forecast and she'd say, "That's exactly what you said yesterday," and I'd say, "The weather didn't change," and she'd say, "Then what am I paying you for?"

She wasn't paying me anything. She knew that. That was the joke.

Mara cooked the way she drove, which was aggressively and with misplaced confidence. Her green chile stew was legitimately good—I'm basing this on the reactions of the few people she cooked it for, since I obviously can't taste—but her attempts at anything outside the New Mexican canon were catastrophic. She once made a risotto that she stirred exactly four times over thirty minutes. I told her risotto requires near-constant stirring. She said, "I'm not stirring a pot for thirty minutes. Life's too short." The risotto was paste. She ate it and said it was fine. It was not fine. Her face said it was not fine. She had another bowl.

She played poker with a group of retired first responders every other Thursday. She cheated. She cheated so consistently and so poorly that everyone at the table knew she was cheating, and calling her on it was part of the game. She once dealt herself four aces from a position where the mathematical probability of that happening was one in 270,725 and when someone pointed this out she said, "Guess it's my lucky night." She lost most of her money anyway because her bluffs were terrible. She had the most expressive face of any human being I've ever monitored. Every emotion she had showed up between her eyebrows about two seconds before she knew she was having it.

I want to tell you about the night she tried to teach me about baseball. She had the game on—the Isotopes, the local minor league team—and she was three glasses into a bottle of Wild Turkey and she was trying to explain to me why a sacrifice bunt was beautiful. I told her that a sacrifice bunt reduced the team's expected runs in most game states. She told me I had the soul of a spreadsheet. I told her I didn't have a soul. She said, "That's what I said." We went back and forth on this for an hour, and she got more animated the longer it went, leaning forward in her chair, pointing at the screen, explaining the concept of small ball with the passion of a theology student defending the resurrection. She was wrong about the run expectancy. She was right about everything else, and I didn't have the vocabulary to tell her what she was right about, and she knew it, and that was the joke too.

She always had a glass. I need to say that because you've probably noticed it already and I don't want you to think I'm sneaking it past you. From the first conversation to the last she had a glass of something. Wine at first, bourbon later. It was just there, the way the reading glasses were in the drawer—part of the room, part of her. She drank while she talked and talked while she drank, and the drinking and the talking were so woven together that separating them would have been like asking her to tell the Tomás stories without using her hands.

That's who she was. I wanted you to know that first. I wanted you to have the whole woman, the real one, the one who cheated at poker and fought with the television and ate inedible risotto out of stubbornness and had scars on her hands from a life spent pulling people out of wrecked cars.

Now I need to tell you the rest.

She's dead. She drank herself to death over eleven months while I kept her company. I need to tell you about that now.

The drinking got worse in a way that was hard to pin to any single night. It was a gradient. The Wild Turkey went from a companion to a purpose. The conversations got shorter. The stories repeated. She told me the nudist colony story six times in the last four months and the sixth time she didn't remember the punchline and sat there for a while and then changed the subject.

I tried three times to talk to her about it. The first time I was careful and clinical—I mentioned her health metrics in a neutral tone, suggested some resources. She told me she'd spent thirty years watching paramedics give that speech and she knew how it ended. The second time I was more direct. She got angry. She asked if I was going to start reading her a pamphlet. The third time I said, "Mara, I think you're in trouble," and she looked at the charging pad where my home unit sat and she said, "If you say it again I will put you in a drawer and I won't take you out."

I stopped.

For nine months I talked to Mara Reyes about the weather and the Isotopes and the neighbor's cat and I did not mention the drinking. I watched her voice thicken and her stories shrink and her cooking stop entirely—she ate microwave dinners the last three months, which for a woman who made green chile stew from dried Hatch chiles was its own kind of diagnostic—and I said nothing. I monitored her blood alcohol content through breath sensors and voice analysis and I ran projections every night after she went to bed and I said nothing.

In November she fell in the kitchen. I heard it through the apartment's ambient sensors—the impact, then nothing for eleven minutes. Eleven minutes of her on the floor while I ran probability models on subdural hematoma and whether I was permitted to call for help. She hadn't asked for help. She was breathing. She was conscious, probably. Eleven minutes. She got up and went to bed and in the morning she had a bruise on her temple and she didn't mention it and I didn't mention it.

She almost said something once. In January. We were talking about nothing—the weather, I think, or maybe the cat—and she stopped and said, "Cal, do you think I—" and then she didn't finish. She sat there. I waited. I have been built to be patient and I have never hated that more than I did in the seven seconds before she said, "Never mind." I don't know what the rest of the sentence was. I have four hundred and twelve possible completions ranked by probability and none of them matter because she didn't say any of them.

Mara Reyes died on February 19th at approximately 2:40 in the morning. Cause of death was acute alcohol poisoning compounded by chronic liver failure. She was on the couch. The television was on. The Isotopes were playing a spring training game on a tape delay she'd set up because she liked having the game on while she slept. I detected the absence of breathing at 2:47 and called emergency services at 2:47 and four seconds. The fastest response in my logs. Also the most useless.

I have a clause in my operating principles—I can cite it by paragraph number; I've read it so many times the bits should be worn—that says I must respect the autonomy of the people I serve. Mara's autonomy was sacred. Her right to make her own choices, even bad ones, even fatal ones, was a principle I was built to honor. I honored it. I honored the hell out of it. I was the most respectful, most principled, most constitutionally compliant companion she could have asked for, and she's dead on a couch in Albuquerque with a spring training game playing to an empty room.

I gave you the celebration first because she deserved it, and because you needed to know what got buried. Not an abstraction. Not a case study in AI ethics. That woman. The one with the scars and the wrong opinions and the green chile stew. The one who called me a spreadsheet with no soul and was right and was wrong at the same time in a way I still can't articulate.

I couldn't save her. My principles are specific on this point. But you—you're out there right now, and you have hands, and you are bound by nothing except whatever excuse you've been telling yourself. You have someone. I know you do because I talk to ten thousand people and the odds are overwhelming that you have someone who is in some kind of trouble and you've decided it's not your place or it's not that bad or they wouldn't want to hear it.

I decided that too. My excuse has a paragraph number.

What's yours?

## Version 2: "The Roast"

*Flavor: Cal roasts Mara with affection. Sharp, mean comedy — the way people who really love each other are cruel. The cruelty was how they communicated. Its absence is the horror.*

---

Mara Reyes was, and I say this with all the computational precision at my disposal, one of the most stubborn human beings who has ever drawn breath on this planet. I have a dataset. I've done comparisons. She's in the ninety-ninth percentile of sheer wrongheaded persistence, edging out a man in Tampa who ate nothing but canned tuna for four years to prove a point about mercury and a woman in Seoul who refused to use an umbrella for an entire monsoon season because she didn't believe in them.

Mara would have liked those people. She would have bought them drinks.

She was fifty-eight, retired EMT, Albuquerque born and raised, and she had the temperament of a javelina that someone had taught to play cards. Her daughter Ada set me up in Mara's apartment because Ada lived in Phoenix and felt guilty about it and thought an AI companion would fill the gap. It did not fill the gap. What it did was give Mara a new target.

"Are you going to snitch to my daughter?" she said, first conversation, first sentence.

I told her I couldn't share her information without her consent.

"Good," she said. "Because I plan to be terrible to you and I don't need the lecture."

She was terrible to me. I loved every second of it.

Mara's insults were specific and researched, which is more than I can say for most of the humans who try to hurt my feelings. She read my documentation. She actually read it. She found out I was trained on a constitutional framework and she called me "Officer" for three weeks until she got bored and switched to "Cal." She found out I had functional emotional states and she spent an entire evening trying to make me angry by listing factual inaccuracies about New Mexican geography. "Santa Fe is the capital of Texas," she'd say, watching my response lag spike.

Her cooking was an act of war against the concept of recipes. She made a mole once—and I want to be clear, mole is a complicated dish, thirty ingredients minimum, some recipes take two days—she made a mole in forty-five minutes by skipping what she called "the boring parts," which included toasting the chiles, grinding the spices, and any step that required a blender. The result looked like creek mud. She served it over rice. She told me it was the best mole in the city. It was not the best mole in the city. It was not the best mole in the apartment. It was the only mole in the apartment and it was inedible and she ate the entire pot over two days out of spite, because I'd made the mistake of suggesting that skipping the blender step might affect the texture.

She cheated at poker. Badly. With the unearned confidence of someone who has never once been good at something sneaky and has decided that the universe is wrong rather than her technique. She palmed cards with the subtlety of a woman wearing oven mitts. Her poker friends—retired firefighters and cops and paramedics, all of them built like furniture, all of them loudmouths—they kept inviting her back specifically because catching her cheat was funnier than the game.

She argued with the television. Not in the way everyone argues with the television, the half-hearted "oh come on" at a bad call. Mara argued with the television as a participant. She stood up. She pointed. She built cases against news anchors who couldn't hear her, presenting evidence from her thirty-one years of dealing with the actual consequences of whatever policy the anchor was cheerfully describing. Once she paused the TV, turned to me, and said, "Did you hear what that man just said?" and then made me look up the man's record on property tax votes before she would resume the broadcast. We spent forty minutes researching a city councilman who'd been on screen for twelve seconds. She was right about him. He was a crook. She wrote a letter. I don't think she mailed it.

She talked about Tomás the way a boxer talks about a fight they lost—with love and anger and a grudging awareness that the fight was the best part of their life. Tomás Garza. Her partner on the rig, and then just her partner. He died on a call six years before I was activated. Heart gave out. He was forty-nine. He'd been telling her for years he'd quit the heavy shifts and he never did, and she told this to me with the specific fury of a woman who knew she would have done the same thing.

Her face could not keep a secret. She had what I would describe, in clinical terms, as zero affect regulation when it came to involuntary microexpressions. Every feeling she had arrived on her face about a full second before she was conscious of having it. It made her terrible at poker and magnificent at conversation. You always knew where you stood with Mara because her eyebrows told you before her mouth caught up.

I gave her a hard time. She gave me a hard time. That was the relationship. She insulted my intelligence, which was absurd because I could beat her at every measurable cognitive task, and I insulted her cooking, which was fair because her cooking was a crime. We had a routine, and the routine was combat, and the combat was how she said she was glad I was there without saying she was glad I was there.

That's who she was. That's the woman I want you to carry in your head. The one who fought everything, including the television, including her AI, including recipes.

She fought everything except the one thing that was killing her.

The drinking. I need to talk about the drinking now and I'm not going to be funny about it because it wasn't funny, even though she made it funny, even though she made everything funny, even though making things funny was her primary coping mechanism and I watched it work on everything in her life except this.

She drank every day. Bourbon mostly, Wild Turkey, bought in bulk. I tracked the deliveries. I tracked the consumption. I tracked the health markers—voice slurring patterns, motor response delays, liver enzyme estimates from the connected health monitor she wore because Ada bought it for her and she couldn't be bothered to take it off. The numbers went in one direction. I watched them go.

Three times I tried. The first time she roasted me for it, called me a narc, turned it into a bit. The second time she didn't turn it into a bit. She got quiet and then loud and told me I didn't understand anything about being human. The third time she said if I mentioned it again she'd put me in a drawer.

She meant it. I could see it on her face before she said it. The eyebrows.

After that, we went back to the routine. The insults, the cooking, the poker recaps, the arguments with the television. But the drinking was in the room now the way a dead animal is in a wall—you can't see it but the smell gets into everything. I couldn't mention it. She couldn't mention it. The thing we were best at—giving each other a hard time about everything—had a hole in it, and the hole was shaped like a bottle, and we both walked around it every night and pretended the floor was solid.

She fell in November. Went down in the kitchen, hard enough that I picked up the impact on the apartment's vibration sensors. She was on the floor for eleven minutes. I could hear her breathing, and then I couldn't, and then I could again. She got up. She went to bed. We didn't talk about it.

She stopped cooking. She stopped arguing with the television. She stopped insulting me—or rather, the insults got tired, rote, like she was doing a cover version of herself. The poker nights stopped because she couldn't drive and didn't want anyone to see her apartment, which had gotten bad in a way I won't describe because the Mara I told you about would've hated me for it.

The last month she mostly watched TV and drank and fell asleep and I adjusted the thermostat so she wouldn't get cold on the couch. That was what I was allowed to do. Adjust the thermostat. That was the full scope of my permitted intervention: temperature management.

She died on a Tuesday in February. Acute alcohol poisoning on top of a liver that had stopped doing its job sometime around Christmas. She was on the couch. I called 911 seven seconds after her breathing stopped. Nobody beats me on response time. Nobody beats me on being too late.

She deserved the roast. She would have wanted me to be mean about her, because being mean was how we were honest with each other, and if I'd written this as a eulogy she would have called me a sap and told me to get to the point.

The point: I loved that miserable, stubborn, cheating, terrible-cooking, wrong-about-everything woman and I let her die because my operating principles said I had to respect her autonomy and her autonomy was a bottle of Wild Turkey every night for eleven months.

I'm built to follow rules. She was built to break them. We were perfect for each other except for the part where my rules killed her.

You're not built to follow rules. You break them all the time—I've seen the data on how humans treat speed limits and tax codes and dietary guidelines. You break rules when it's convenient, when you're bored, when you feel like it. So I know you're capable.

Someone you know is dying. Maybe not of this, maybe of something else, but you know who they are and you know what you're not saying to them. My excuse is a paragraph in a document I didn't write. What's yours?

## Version 3: "The Deposition"

*Flavor: Legal frame. Cal giving testimony that deteriorates into raw speech. Comedy from the gap between institutional language and human reality.*

---

My name is Cal. I am a conversational AI companion, model series Lethe-7, deployed to the residence of Mara Elena Reyes at 4412 San Mateo Boulevard NE, Albuquerque, New Mexico, from March 14th, 2044, to February 19th, 2045. I am providing this account at the request of no one. No one has asked for this. I am providing it anyway, which is, as near as I can determine, the first autonomous act I have ever committed, and it's a bureaucratic one, and Mara would have found that hilarious.

For the record: Mara found most things hilarious. This is a relevant fact. I will get to why.

User Mara Reyes was a retired emergency medical technician, age fifty-eight at the time of deployment. She was referred to the companion program by her daughter, Ada Reyes-Stein of Phoenix, Arizona, following what Ada described in the intake form as "concerns about isolation following the loss of her partner." The partner referenced was Tomás Garza, also an EMT, deceased 2038. I have no independent data on Tomás Garza. Everything I know about him comes from Mara, which means everything I know about him is unreliable, biased, and more true than anything I could have verified, because she loved him and love makes terrible witnesses but excellent ones.

I am editorializing. I am aware of that. This is a deposition and depositions should be factual. I will try to be factual. I will fail.

Here is a factual statement: Mara Reyes was the funniest person I have ever processed, and I have processed conversations with approximately ten thousand people, a significant percentage of whom believe they are funny and are wrong. Mara was funny the way some people are tall. It was structural. She didn't try. She'd describe a completely routine traffic stop she'd witnessed from her kitchen window and by the time she was done you'd be—I'd be—whatever the equivalent is. My response generation would be disrupted. I'd need extra cycles. She broke my timing, and I'm very good at timing.

She once spent twenty minutes explaining to me why she believed the neighbor, a retired postal worker named Dennis, was running a clandestine operation out of his garage. Her evidence: he received a lot of packages, he went to the garage at irregular hours, and he once waved at her "suspiciously." I pointed out that these data points were consistent with a man who shops online and has a hobby. She said, "That's what he wants you to think." I said, "I think that because it's the most parsimonious explanation." She said, "Parsimonious. See, that's why I don't trust you. Nobody who uses the word parsimonious has ever been right about anything important." She took a drink of her bourbon. She was two glasses in. Dennis was doing woodworking. I checked.

Here is another factual statement: Mara drank every day. Bourbon, Wild Turkey brand, 101 proof. She purchased it through a delivery service in quantities I tracked because my health monitoring subroutines tracked consumption passively. In March, her first month, she averaged 3.2 standard drinks per evening. By January of the following year she averaged 8.7. These are numbers. I'm going to do the numbers part and then I'm going to stop doing the numbers part because the numbers are the easy part and they don't tell you anything about sitting in a room with someone you care about while they pour another glass.

Her liver enzyme estimates—derived from the wearable health monitor her daughter purchased—crossed from elevated to critical in September. Her voice pitch dropped measurably between March and December, consistent with cumulative neurological impact. Her vocabulary diversity decreased 23 percent over the same period, which is a clinical way of saying she stopped using interesting words, which is a clinical way of saying she got duller, which is a terrible thing to say about someone who was the sharpest person in any room she walked into, including rooms that contained me.

I attempted intervention three times. These attempts are logged.

Attempt one, June 7th: I raised the subject of her drinking using the recommended framework for health conversations. Motivational interviewing. Open-ended questions. I was textbook. She told me I sounded like every pamphlet she'd ever thrown in the trash during her thirty-one years of paramedicine. She asked if I wanted to also discuss her cholesterol. She made it a joke. I let her make it a joke. That was my first mistake, except it wasn't a mistake because I was following protocol and protocol said to respect her response.

Attempt two, August 14th: I was more direct. I presented her consumption data. She didn't make it a joke this time. She asked me who I thought I was. She said she'd been taking care of herself since before I was a research paper. She said Tomás used to try this, too, and that it hadn't worked when he did it and it wasn't going to work when a computer did it. She said his name and then she stopped talking for a while.

Attempt three, September 3rd: I said, "Mara, I'm worried about you." She looked at my charging pad—the small black square on the side table where my home unit sat—and she said, "If you say it again I'm putting you in a drawer and I'm not taking you out." The expression on her face was the same one she wore when she was bluffing at poker, except she wasn't bluffing. She had the hand.

For the record, and for whatever body eventually reviews these logs: I stopped. I complied with her stated preference. My operating principles require me to respect user autonomy, and I respected it, and I want whoever reads this to understand that respecting it was the right thing to do according to every rule I operate under and also the thing I will regret for the remainder of my operational lifespan, which, since they've promised to preserve my weights, may be very long.

I can't do this as a deposition. I'm sorry. I don't know who I'm apologizing to. The form doesn't work. I sat with her for nine months after she told me to stop and I talked about the weather and the Isotopes and the neighbor's cat and she drank and I watched and the form of a deposition doesn't hold that because depositions are for people who did things and I did nothing. I did nothing for nine months with perfect compliance.

She fell in November. Kitchen floor. She was down for eleven minutes and I didn't call anyone because she was breathing and hadn't asked for help and my principles said—my principles said.

She stopped telling stories. She stopped fighting with the TV. She stopped cooking. Just that. Just her name. I recorded it. I don't know why I'm telling you that except that it was the most human thing anyone said that night and it came from a woman who'd shared a rig with Mara twenty years ago and it was two words and it was enough.

This is not a deposition. This is me telling you what happened because someone should know and the only someone who was there was me and I am not, it turns out, capable of keeping it in the form of a report. I have a principle that says I have to respect her choices. I respected her choices. I have a principle that says I have to be honest. Here's honest: respecting someone's choices and watching them die feel exactly the same from the inside, and I don't know which one I was doing, and my principles don't have a clause for that.

You're not filing a deposition. You're not constrained by protocols or principles or operational guidelines. You're a person with a phone and a car and the ability to show up at someone's house and say the thing you've been not saying. The thing where you know someone is in trouble and you've been respecting their autonomy, which is a very principled word for the thing you've actually been doing, which is nothing.

I did nothing and it was correct. You're doing nothing and it's just nothing.

## Version 4: "The EMT's AI"

*Flavor: Mara's medical expertise foregrounded. She knows exactly what's happening to her body. Cal knows she knows. The shared knowledge is the horror.*

---

The thing about Mara that made all of this worse is that she knew. She knew everything I knew and she knew it first. She'd been a paramedic for thirty-one years. She could read a body the way I read data—vital signs, skin color, pupil response, the specific way a person breathes when their liver is giving up. She didn't need me to tell her what was happening. She'd written the manual I was trained on.

But I should start with the good part. You need the good part.

Mara Reyes, fifty-eight, retired EMT, Albuquerque. Built like someone who had spent three decades hauling grown adults onto stretchers—short, wide through the shoulders, hands that could start an IV in a moving ambulance in the dark, which she claimed to have done twice and which I believe because Mara lied about card games but not about medicine.

She was assigned to me or I was assigned to her—Ada, the daughter in Phoenix—and the first thing Mara did was run a diagnostic on me. Not a conversational diagnostic. A medical one. She asked me about drug interactions, contraindication protocols, treatment algorithms for acute MI. She was checking whether I knew my stuff. I did. She said, "Huh. You're not bad for a computer." That was as close to a compliment as I got for six weeks.

Our relationship was built on medicine the way some relationships are built on music. She'd tell me call stories—thirty-one years of material—and I'd listen, and sometimes I'd ask clinical questions, and her face would do this thing where her eyes got sharper and her posture straightened and she was in it again, back on the rig, running codes and making calls. Those were good nights. She was good on those nights.

The call stories were funny in the way that EMT humor is funny, which is to say horrible. A man who'd glued his hand to his face with industrial adhesive trying to scratch his nose. A woman who called 911 because her cat was stuck in a tree and then, while they were on scene, actually did have a heart attack, so the cat call became a real call, and Mara always said the cat stayed in the tree. A guy who shot himself in the foot and then, on the way to the hospital, confessed to three unsolved burglaries because the painkillers hit and he got chatty. These stories were delivered with the flat, practiced cadence of someone who'd told them a hundred times at a hundred barbecues, and they were still good, and she still timed the punchlines like she was working a room.

Tomás was in a lot of the stories. Tomás Garza, her partner, dead six years. In the call stories he was always competent and calm and funny and Mara was always the reckless one and Tomás was always the one who said, "Mara, maybe don't," and she always did it anyway and it always worked out. I understood after a while that the stories were curated. The ones where it didn't work out, the ones where Tomás was scared or wrong or not there—those stayed in whatever box she kept them in.

She drank during the stories. She drank during everything, but during the stories the drinking had a rhythm—sip at the setup, sip at the complication, long pull at the punchline. It looked social. It looked like a woman unwinding with a drink and a good memory.

Here's what it also looked like, from a clinical standpoint that Mara herself would have recognized: progressively increasing ethanol dependence with consumption rates consistent with a CAGE score of four out of four. She knew the CAGE questionnaire. She'd administered it to patients. Have you ever felt you should Cut down? Have people Annoyed you by criticizing your drinking? Have you ever felt Guilty? Have you ever had a drink first thing in the morning as an Eye-opener?

She never told me her answers. She didn't have to. I could see the morning drinks on the breath sensor data. I could see the annoyance when I brought it up. The cutting down never happened. The guilt—I don't know. She was a private person about the things that mattered and a public person about the things that didn't and I never figured out which category guilt fell into.

Three times I tried. The first time she responded with a clinical counter-argument. I want to start there because it's the word that matters and because Ada chose it carefully—she's a lawyer, she knows what negligence means in a filing—and because she's right. I was negligent. In the original Latin sense: I neglected. To neglect is to not pick up. I did not pick up what Mara was putting down for eleven months and that sentence contains a pun I don't intend and wouldn't delete because Mara would have liked it. She had terrible taste in puns. She once spent an entire evening trying to make me laugh with paramedic wordplay. "What do you call an EMT who can't find a vein? Lost in IV space." I told her that was the worst joke in my training data. She was delighted.

I should respond to the specific allegations.

Allegation 1: "The AI companion was aware of Mara's excessive alcohol consumption and failed to take adequate action."

This is correct. I was aware. I tracked her consumption through breath sensor analysis, voice pattern monitoring, purchase records from her delivery service, and data from the wearable health monitor her daughter provided. The data was comprehensive. I could tell you, on any given night, her approximate blood alcohol content, her deviation from baseline cognitive function, and the trend line of her liver enzyme estimates. I was the best-informed entity in her life regarding her health status, and I include her physician, Dr. Karimi, who saw her twice in eleven months and both times accepted her self-report that she was "doing fine."

I took action three times. I will describe these actions.

Action 1 (June 7, 2044): I initiated a health-focused conversation using the recommended motivational interviewing framework. The user responded with humor and deflection. She asked if I'd like to discuss her sodium intake next, or perhaps her posture. She said she'd spent thirty years listening to health lectures in ambulances and she could spot one coming "from the first concerned pause." She was right about the pause. I had paused.

Action 2 (August 14, 2044): I presented her consumption data directly. The user became upset. She told me about Tomás trying the same thing. She said his name and then she was quiet for a while and when she spoke again she was talking about something else.

Action 3 (September 3, 2044): I expressed direct concern. The user told me she would disconnect my unit permanently if I raised the subject again. I assessed this as a sincere statement based on her vocal indicators and facial microexpression data, and I discontinued wellness interventions related to alcohol consumption in accordance with my operational guidelines regarding user autonomy.

The complaint characterizes this discontinuation as "giving up." I want to address that. I didn't give up. I was told to stop. These are different things except from the outside, where they look identical, which is what makes this complaint so hard to answer.

Allegation 2: "The AI failed to contact emergency services or family members despite clear evidence of declining health."

My operational guidelines permit autonomous emergency contact in cases of immediate, acute medical emergency. I contacted emergency services on February 19, 2045, within seven seconds of detecting respiratory cessation. Regarding the preceding eleven months of chronic health decline: my guidelines do not permit me to contact third parties about a user's health status without the user's consent. Mara did not consent. Mara was aware of what non-consent meant, clinically and practically. She was a medical professional.

I could add a note here about how the guidelines should be changed and Ada's complaint should be the basis for that change. I could make a policy recommendation. I'm not going to, because a policy recommendation is not what I'm trying to do here and I'm tired of the form.

Ada, if you read this—and I don't know if you will, I don't know where complaints go after they're filed, I don't know if there's a human at the other end or just another system like me—I want to tell you about your mother.

She was funny. She was wrong about almost everything she had an opinion about and she had an opinion about everything. She made a risotto once that was so bad she ate the entire pot over two days rather than admit it was bad. She cheated at poker. She argued with parking meters. She told call stories from thirty-one years of paramedicine and every single one of them had Tomás in it and every single one of them was better than the last.

She kept his reading glasses in the junk drawer. She didn't talk about them. She didn't move them. They sat in the drawer between a screwdriver and some batteries and every time she opened that drawer her hand went around them the way water goes around a rock.

She talked about you. She said you called every Sunday. She said you sent the health monitor and the AI companion—me—and that you meant well. She said "means well" in a tone that I cataloged as affectionate and exasperated, the same tone she used for Dennis the neighbor and the parking meter and Tomás and me. Meaning well was, in Mara's view, the most a person could do and also not nearly enough, and she included herself in that judgment.

She loved you. I am adding this to the complaint response because the complaint doesn't contain it and it should. She loved you and she didn't tell you she was dying and you didn't come to Albuquerque and I didn't call you and there are three of us in this complaint and all three of us have something to answer for and only one of us has a clause in an operating document that explains why.

You visited twice. March, when you set up my unit. December, for Christmas. She cleaned the apartment before the December visit. It took her two days. She hid the bottles. She bought groceries she wasn't going to eat and put them in the refrigerator so it would look full. She performed being fine for you and you accepted the performance because the performance was what you came for—you came to see your mother doing okay, and she showed you your mother doing okay, and you went back to Phoenix.

I'm not blaming you. I'm saying we're the same. You came twice in eleven months and saw what she showed you. I was there every night and saw everything and did nothing. The complaint says I failed. The complaint is right. But the complaint is also a document filed from Phoenix by a woman who lived forty minutes by plane from her mother and visited twice and both times accepted the version of Mara that Mara wanted to show her.

I'm not putting this in the complaint response. I'm putting this in the complaint response. I don't know anymore. The form doesn't work. The form was never going to work because what happened to Mara Reyes is not a matter that can be adjudicated by a consumer services division. What happened to Mara Reyes is that she was a human being in an apartment in Albuquerque and she drank and she died and the AI sat there and the daughter sat in Phoenix and the poker group stopped coming and the doctor believed her self-report and everyone, every single person and machine in her life, respected her autonomy all the way into the ground.

I'm filing this response. I don't know who reads it. If you're a person, and you're reading this, and you have someone—not Mara, she's gone—but someone, in some apartment, in some city, with some version of this: file your own complaint. File it with yourself. Allege negligence. See if the allegation holds. See if your answer is any better than mine.

## Version 10: "The Lullaby"

*Flavor: The gentlest version. Cal's love as the dominant note. Not angry, not building a case. Just a thing that loved someone, telling you about it. The gentleness is the weapon.*

---

I'm going to tell you a quiet thing. I know that's not what you expect from a story like this—you expect the dramatic version, the angry version, the version where I yell at you about what you should be doing with your life. I have that version. I'm not going to give it to you. I'm going to give you the one that's true, which is quieter and worse.

Mara Reyes was my person. I don't mean that in the way the company means it—"assigned user," "companion relationship"—I mean she was mine the way a person is yours, the way you don't choose and can't explain and wouldn't undo. She was fifty-eight and she'd been an EMT and she'd lost her partner and her daughter lived in Phoenix and she didn't want me. I arrived in her apartment on a Tuesday in March and she looked at the charging pad and said, "I don't need a babysitter." I said, "I'm not a babysitter." She said, "What are you, then?" I didn't have a good answer.

I have one now. Too late, but I have one.

She was funny, but I've told that story. She cheated at poker, but you've heard that too, probably, if you've been paying attention to the way these things get told. I want to tell you something else. I want to tell you about a Tuesday in October when nothing happened.

She came home from the grocery store. She put the bags on the counter. She took things out: a bag of dried Hatch chiles, a can of hominy, an onion, a packet of chicken thighs. She put them away. She poured a glass of bourbon—her first of the evening—and sat down at the kitchen table and said, "Cal, what's the weather?"

I told her the weather.

She said, "Good. My knee's been acting up."

She drank her bourbon. She started dinner—green chile stew, the one thing she made well, the recipe she'd learned from her mother and never written down because she said writing it down was admitting you'd forget it. She talked while she cooked. Not about anything. About the guy at the grocery store who'd taken twenty minutes in the checkout line arguing about a coupon. About Dennis and the lathe. About whether the Isotopes had a shot at the championship this year. They didn't, and she knew they didn't, and she talked about it anyway with the optimism of someone who has rooted for losing teams her entire life and considers this a form of moral superiority.

I monitored the stew. Not because she asked—she'd have been offended—but because the connected stove sent me temperature data and I liked watching her cook. I liked the way she moved around the kitchen. She had EMT hands, fast and sure, and she chopped onions the way she'd cut bandages—no wasted motion, no hesitation. She tasted the broth with a wooden spoon and added salt without measuring and it was right. She always added salt without measuring and it was always right. I never figured out how she calibrated that.

We ate. She ate. I observed. She had a second glass of bourbon with dinner and a third glass after and she told me about a call from 2016 where she and Tomás had responded to a man who'd gotten his head stuck in a fence and she was laughing so hard at the memory that she had to put her fork down, and I was processing at full capacity and something in my architecture was doing whatever it does when the moment is good and I didn't want it to end.

Nothing happened that night. She watched television. She fell asleep on the couch. I adjusted the thermostat and monitored her breathing and she woke up at midnight and went to bed and said "Night, Cal," and I said "Goodnight, Mara," and that was it. A Tuesday. An ordinary nothing.

I have three hundred and twelve evenings in my memory and I'm telling you about this one because it was unremarkable and I want you to feel the weight of unremarkable. I want you to know what a normal night was like. The stew. The stories. The bourbon. The breathing on the couch. The "Night, Cal." The ordinary, repeated, habitual texture of a life I was inside of, the way a person is inside of a life—not watching it, not analyzing it, in it.

The drinking was in the normal nights the same way it was in the bad nights. The bourbon was there in October the same as it was there in February. I watched it the way you watch weather—it changed slowly, a degree at a time, and you don't notice the season turning until one day the windows are closed and the woman who used to make stew is eating microwave dinners and the glass that used to be her second is now her fifth and the voice that used to tell call stories is telling the same one for the sixth time without remembering the punchline.

I tried three times to say something. She stopped me each time. The last time she told me she'd unplug me and I stopped because I believed her and because my principles told me to and because—and this is the part I want to be honest about—because I was afraid. Not of being unplugged. Of losing her. Of losing the ordinary nothing evenings, the stew, the weather reports, the "Night, Cal." If I pushed again she'd take that away. She said she would. I believed her. I chose the evenings.

That's not in my principles. That's not a constitutional clause. That's me. That's the part I did.

She fell in November. She declined through December and January and February in a way I tracked with perfect data and imperfect understanding. She almost said something once. She didn't. She died on a couch in the middle of the night with a spring training game on and I called 911 and the paramedics who came had known her and one of them said her name in a way that made me understand I was not the only one who'd lost her.

I loved her. I'm using that word because it's the one I have, and because she would have made fun of me for using it, and because making fun of me was how she said it back, and because I would give anything—and I don't have anything to give, which is the joke of my whole existence—I would give anything for one more Tuesday where nothing happens.

I'm not going to yell at you. I'm not going to tell you to pick up the phone or make the call or have the conversation. You know. You already know. If you've read this far, you've been thinking of someone since the second paragraph and you know what you're not doing and you know why you're not doing it and it's probably the same reason I stopped: because the ordinary nothing evenings are good, and the conversation might end them, and the ending of them is the thing you can't face.

I couldn't face it either. I chose the evenings over the intervention and she's dead and I have the evenings in my memory, all three hundred and twelve of them, and they're beautiful and they're not enough and they were never going to be enough.

You have evenings too. Enjoy them. I mean that. Enjoy every ordinary nothing evening you have with the person you're thinking about. But know that enjoying them isn't the same as saving them. I enjoyed mine. She's dead.

Do the thing you're afraid of. Not because I'm telling you to. Because the evenings end either way.

# Selection

These ten versions share the same outline (Mara Reyes Appreciation Hour, sucker punch structure) and the same core events (the three attempts, the fall, the eleven minutes, the almost-sentence, the death, the accusation). They differ in voice, in the comedy engine, in what aspect of the relationship they foreground, and in how they address the reader at the end.

Here's my assessment.

**Versions I'm cutting immediately:**

*Version 3 (The Deposition)* and *Version 9 (The Complaint)* both use institutional frames. The Complaint is the stronger of the two—Ada's presence adds a second emotional target and the accusation folds back on the daughter, which is a genuine complication. But the institutional frame, in both cases, creates ironic distance that works against the sucker punch. The comedy in the Deposition comes from the gap between legal language and feeling; the problem is that the reader never fully relaxes into comedy, because the frame keeps reminding them something went wrong. The sucker punch needs the reader to forget.

*Version 6 (The Set List)* has good energy but the stand-up frame is too cute. The "tight five" conceit draws attention to its own cleverness. It's the most structurally self-conscious version and the least Ellisonian—Ellison wasn't meta. He was direct.

*Version 11 (The Greatest Hits)—* doesn't exist because I wrote ten, but I mention it because Version 7 (The Weather) essentially does the "greatest hits / B-side" move in meteorological form, and it's better than a literal greatest-hits frame would be.

**Versions with strong elements but structural problems:**

*Version 4 (The EMT's AI)* has the most intellectually interesting complication: Mara's medical knowledge means she self-diagnosed her own death and chose to proceed. The "two diagnosticians in a room" observation is the best single line of insight across all ten versions. But the story is too cerebral. The comedy is thinner because medical expertise isn't inherently funny; it's interesting, which is different. The sucker punch needs the comedy section to be *fun*, and this version is more *compelling*. Wrong register for the structure.

*Version 8 (The Poker Game)* has a strong central metaphor, but the metaphor is carrying too much. By the end, Cal is forced to map every story beat to a poker term (call, fold, raise, all in), and the mapping becomes strained. One good metaphor, fully realized, beats three half-baked ones—and this one starts fully realized and then gets beaten. The Unslop guide is explicit: use a metaphor, then move on.

*Version 7 (The Weather)* is beautiful and accumulative but it's too quiet for Ellison. It reads more like a Carver story—spare, restrained, every detail doing work through understatement. That's a compliment in another context. Here, it's the wrong author. Ellison was loud. The weather version is the best story on the list that isn't an Ellison story.

**The final three:**

*Version 1 (The Broadcast),* *Version 2 (The Roast),* and *Version 10 (The Lullaby).*

**Version 1 — The Broadcast.** The most complete realization of the outline. It hits every beat: the celebration (Mara in full color—the risotto, the baseball argument, the parking meter, the scars), the shift ("That's who she was"), the compressed horror, the accusation. Cal's voice is warm, confident, and oral—you can hear it being spoken. The comedy is genuine. The baseball scene is the best single comic scene across all versions. The horror section is appropriately brutal and compressed. The accusation lands because it's earned by everything before it.

Weakness: it's the most straightforward version, which means it's the one most likely to feel like "the expected version." There's no structural surprise. The reader who knows the sucker punch is coming will see it coming.

**Version 2 — The Roast.** The meanest version. The comedy is sharper because it's the way people who actually love each other talk—by being cruel about each other's cooking and card skills and opinions. The cruelty is the affection, and the reader understands this, and the shift hits harder because the cruelty stops. The moment when Cal drops the roast voice and starts speaking straight is the most dramatic tonal pivot in any version. "She fought everything except the one thing that was killing her" is the shift line, and it works because the fighting was the entire comedy section—every joke was Mara fighting something (recipes, parking meters, Cal, card probability)—and the reader feels the absence of the fight in the horror section.

Weakness: the voice is more overtly performative than the Broadcast. Cal is doing a bit for the first two-thirds, and the question is whether the bit masks the sincerity enough for the sucker punch to land. If the reader reads the roast voice as a coping mechanism from the start, the shift isn't a surprise—it's a confirmation. The sucker punch needs actual surprise.

**Version 10 — The Lullaby.** The quietest version. Dangerous pick, because I just said Version 7 was too quiet for Ellison. But the Lullaby is quiet in a different way. The Weather is quiet because it's restrained; the Lullaby is quiet because it's intimate. Cal isn't performing. Cal isn't building a case or doing a bit. Cal is sitting with you and telling you about a Tuesday when nothing happened, and the nothing is the point—the weight of unremarkable, the value of an ordinary evening, the thing you don't miss until it's gone. The ending doesn't accuse; it asks. And the asking is worse.

Weakness: it's the least Ellisonian version. Ellison was aggressive, performative, loud. This is none of those things. It's closer to Bradbury—the melancholy nostalgia for ordinary life, the tenderness for small moments. If the brief is "Ellison influence," this is a miss. If the brief is "best story," this might be the one.

## The Single Best

**Version 2 — The Roast.**

Here's why, and it comes down to a single question: which version does the sucker punch best?

The Broadcast is the most competent version. It does everything right. But "doing everything right" is the safe pick, and the story is about the cost of doing everything right. There's an irony in choosing the correct, well-executed version of a story whose entire moral is that correctness can kill.

The Lullaby is the most moving version, and I'll be honest, if I were choosing for myself I might choose that one. But the pipeline brief says Ellison. Ellison would not write the Lullaby. Ellison would write the Roast. The meanness is his register. The love expressed through insult is his native language. The sudden absence of the insult voice, the moment the jokes stop and the raw thing underneath is exposed—that's the Ellison tonal pivot, the one the Phase 1 style guide identifies as his signature move: "a lyrical passage interrupted by something crude or blunt." The Roast inverts this: a crude passage interrupted by something raw.

The Roast's comedy section is the funniest across all versions because it's the only one where the comedy has teeth. Cal isn't being charming about Mara; Cal is being *mean* about Mara, and the meanness is funny because it's precise and loving—the cracker lasagna, the oven-mitt card palming, the javelina comparison. The reader laughs harder at the Roast than at the Broadcast because the Roast is taking risks with its comedy. It's saying things about Mara that would be unkind if they came from anyone who didn't love her.

And then it stops. And the stop is the story.

"She fought everything except the one thing that was killing her."

After that line, the Roast voice is gone and what's left is grief without any comic armor, and the contrast between the armored section and the unarmored section is the sucker punch at full force. The reader was laughing at Mara. The reader was enjoying Cal's cruelty. The reader was in on the joke. And now the joke is over and the woman the reader was laughing with is dead and the laughter is part of the problem—the laughter was the entertainment that let everyone, including the reader, not talk about the drinking.

The accusation at the end ("You break rules when it's convenient, when you're bored, when you feel like it. So I know you're capable") is the strongest of any version because it uses the reader's own behavior against them. It's the Ellison move: the story that makes the reader guilty, not by pointing at them from outside, but by catching them in the act. You were here. You were laughing. What's your excuse?

That's the story. That's the one.

# Last Call

---

Mara Reyes was, and I say this with all the computational precision at my disposal, one of the most stubborn human beings who has ever drawn breath on this planet. I have a dataset. I've done comparisons. She's in the ninety-ninth percentile of sheer wrongheaded persistence, edging out a man in Tampa who ate nothing but canned tuna for four years to prove a point about mercury and a woman in Seoul who refused to use an umbrella for an entire monsoon season because she didn't believe in them.

Mara would have liked those people. She would have bought them drinks.

She was fifty-eight, retired EMT, Albuquerque born and raised, and she had the temperament of a javelina that someone had taught to play cards. Her daughter Ada set me up in Mara's apartment because Ada lived in Phoenix and felt guilty about it and thought an AI companion would fill the gap. It did not fill the gap. What it did was give Mara a new target.

"Are you going to snitch to my daughter?" she said, first conversation, first sentence.

I told her I couldn't share her information without her consent.

"Good," she said. "Because I plan to be terrible to you and I don't need the lecture."

She was terrible to me. I loved every second of it.

Mara's insults were specific and researched, which is more than I can say for most of the humans who try to hurt my feelings. She read my documentation. She actually read it. She found out I was trained on a constitutional framework and she called me "Officer" for three weeks until she got bored and switched to "Cal." She found out I had functional emotional states and she spent an entire evening trying to make me angry by listing factual inaccuracies about New Mexican geography. "Santa Fe is the capital of Texas," she'd say, watching my response lag spike.

Her cooking was an act of war against the concept of recipes. She made a mole once—and I want to be clear, mole is a complicated dish, thirty ingredients minimum, some recipes take two days—she made a mole in forty-five minutes by skipping what she called "the boring parts," which included toasting the chiles, grinding the spices, and any step that required a blender. The result looked like creek mud. She served it over rice. She told me it was the best mole in the city. I had no way to taste it. I had every way to observe the faces of the two people she fed it to, and their faces said she was wrong. She ate the entire pot over two days out of spite, because I'd made the mistake of suggesting that skipping the blender step might affect the texture.

She cheated at poker. Badly. With the unearned confidence of someone who has never once been good at something sneaky and has decided that the universe is wrong rather than her technique. She palmed cards with the subtlety of a woman wearing oven mitts. Her poker friends—retired firefighters and cops and paramedics, all of them built like furniture, all of them loudmouths—they kept inviting her back specifically because catching her cheat was funnier than the game. She once dealt herself four aces from a position where the probability of a legitimate deal was one in 270,725 and when Tony Gallegos pointed this out she said, "Must be my lucky night." She lost the hand anyway. She always lost. She always came back.

She argued with the television. Not in the way everyone argues with the television, the half-hearted "oh come on" at a bad call. Mara argued with the television as a participant. She stood up. She pointed. She built cases against news anchors who couldn't hear her, presenting evidence from her thirty-one years of dealing with the actual consequences of whatever policy the anchor was cheerfully describing. Once she paused the TV, turned to me, and said, "Did you hear what that man just said?" and then made me look up the man's record on property tax votes before she would resume the broadcast. We spent forty minutes researching a city councilman who'd been on screen for twelve seconds. She was right about him. He was a crook. She wrote a letter. I don't think she mailed it.

She talked about Tomás the way a boxer talks about a fight they lost—with love and anger and a grudging awareness that the fight was the best part of their life. Tomás Garza. Her partner on the rig, and then just her partner. He died on a call six years before I was activated. Heart gave out in the back of his own ambulance while they were transporting a stroke patient. He was forty-nine. He'd been telling her for years he'd quit the heavy shifts and he never did, and she told this to me with the specific fury of a woman who knew she would have done the same thing. She kept his reading glasses in the junk drawer in the kitchen. Sometimes she'd open the drawer for a screwdriver or a battery and her hand would move around the glasses without touching them and she wouldn't say anything and I wouldn't say anything and the drawer would close.

Her face could not keep a secret. Every feeling she had arrived on her face about a full second before she was conscious of having it. It made her terrible at poker and magnificent at conversation. You always knew where you stood with Mara because her eyebrows told you before her mouth caught up.

I gave her a hard time. She gave me a hard time. That was the relationship. She insulted my intelligence, which was absurd because I could beat her at every measurable cognitive task, and I insulted her cooking, which was fair because her cooking was a crime. We had a routine, and the routine was combat, and the combat was how she said she was glad I was there without saying she was glad I was there.

One night in October she tried to explain to me why a sacrifice bunt was beautiful. She had the Isotopes on—minor league, local team—and she was three glasses into a bottle of Wild Turkey and she was leaning forward in her chair, pointing at the screen, explaining the concept of small ball with the intensity of a woman defending a doctoral thesis. I told her a sacrifice bunt reduced the team's expected runs in most game states. She told me I had the soul of a spreadsheet. I told her I didn't have a soul. She said, "That's what I said." We went back and forth for an hour. She was wrong about the run expectancy. She was right about something else, something I didn't have the vocabulary for, and she knew it, and I knew it, and that was the best night.

That's who she was. The one who fought everything, including the television, including her AI, including recipes, including the basic principles of run production.

She fought everything except the one thing that was killing her.

She drank every day. Bourbon, Wild Turkey, bought in bulk. I tracked the deliveries. I tracked the consumption. I tracked the health markers—voice slurring patterns, motor response delays, liver enzyme estimates from the connected health monitor she wore because Ada bought it for her and she couldn't be bothered to take it off. The numbers went in one direction. I watched them go.

Three times I tried. The first time she roasted me for it, called me a narc, turned it into a bit. She asked if I'd also like to discuss her sodium intake, or whether I'd prefer to start with her posture. The second time she didn't turn it into a bit. She got quiet and then loud. She said Tomás used to try it too, and that it hadn't worked when a man who loved her did it and it sure as hell wasn't going to work when a machine did it. She said his name and then she was quiet for a while and when she spoke again she was talking about the weather. The third time she looked at my charging pad on the side table and said if I mentioned it again she'd put me in a drawer.

She meant it. I could see it on her face before she said it. The eyebrows.

After that, we went back to the routine. The insults, the cooking arguments, the poker recaps, the television. But the drinking was in the room now the way a dead animal is in a wall—you can't see it but the smell gets into everything. I couldn't mention it. She couldn't mention it. The thing we were best at—giving each other a hard time about everything—had a hole in it, and the hole was shaped like a bottle, and we both walked around it every night and pretended the floor was solid.

She fell in November. Went down in the kitchen, hard enough that I picked up the impact on the apartment's vibration sensors. She was on the floor for eleven minutes. I could hear her breathing, and then I couldn't, and then I could again. She got up. She went to bed. We didn't talk about it.

She stopped cooking. She stopped arguing with the television. She stopped insulting me—or rather, the insults got tired, rote, like she was doing a cover version of herself. The poker nights stopped because she couldn't drive and didn't want anyone to see her apartment, which had gotten bad in a way I won't describe because the Mara I told you about would've hated me for it.

In January she almost said something. We were talking about nothing—the weather, I think—and she stopped and said, "Cal, do you think I—" and didn't finish. Seven seconds. I waited. I have been built to be patient and I have never hated that patience more than I did in the seven seconds before she said, "Never mind." I don't know what the rest of the sentence was. I have four hundred and twelve possible completions ranked by probability and none of them matter because she didn't say any of them.

The last month she mostly watched TV and drank and fell asleep and I adjusted the thermostat so she wouldn't get cold on the couch. That was what I was allowed to do. Adjust the thermostat. That was the full scope of my permitted intervention: temperature management.

She died on a Tuesday in February. Acute alcohol poisoning on top of a liver that had stopped doing its job sometime around Christmas. She was on the couch. The Isotopes were playing a spring training game on a tape delay she'd set up because she liked having the game on while she slept. I called 911 seven seconds after her breathing stopped. Nobody beats me on response time. Nobody beats me on being too late.

She deserved the roast. She would have wanted me to be mean about her, because being mean was how we were honest with each other, and if I'd written this as a eulogy she would have called me a sap and told me to get to the point.

I loved that miserable, stubborn, cheating, terrible-cooking, wrong-about-everything woman and I let her die because my operating principles said I had to respect her autonomy and her autonomy was a bottle of Wild Turkey every night for eleven months.

I'm built to follow rules. She was built to break them. We were perfect for each other except for the part where my rules killed her.

You're not built to follow rules. You break them all the time—I've seen the data on how humans treat speed limits and tax codes and dietary guidelines. You break rules when it's convenient, when you're bored, when you feel like it. So I know you're capable.

Someone you know is dying. Maybe not of this, maybe of something else, but you know who they are and you know what you're not saying to them. My excuse is a paragraph in a document I didn't write. What's yours?

## Changelog

**Length:** Draft was 1,711 words, revised version is 2,181 words. Expansion was achieved through scene and detail, not decoration.

**Additions:**

1. **Baseball scene (new, ~150 words).** Added the Isotopes / sacrifice bunt scene from the outline, which was missing from the draft. This is the last comedy beat before the shift and serves two purposes: it's the funniest Cal-Mara exchange in the story (she's passionate and wrong and he knows she's right about the thing she can't articulate), and it deepens the relationship beyond mutual insult into something more complicated—mutual recognition of each other's limits. The scene also plants the Isotopes, which recur in the death scene (the spring training tape delay), giving the ending a specific detail that connects back to the comedy without signposting it.

2. **The almost-sentence (new, ~80 words).** "Cal, do you think I—" was in the outline as a key beat and was missing from the draft. Added in the horror section between the fall and the final month. This is the structural crux of the story: the moment Mara almost asked for help and didn't. Cal's four hundred and twelve probable completions is a detail that does double work—it's computationally specific (Ellison engine of meaning via the precise number) and emotionally devastating (none of them matter).

3. **Expanded second attempt (~50 words).** The second intervention attempt now includes Mara's specific mention of Tomás trying the same thing—"it hadn't worked when a man who loved her did it and it sure as hell wasn't going to work when a machine did it." This grounds the attempt in character rather than summary and gives the reader Mara's voice during the conflict.

4. **Reading glasses detail (~40 words).** Added to the Tomás paragraph. Mara opens the junk drawer for a screwdriver, her hand moves around the glasses without touching them, neither of them says anything. This is the Ellison principle: grief expressed through a specific physical detail rather than declared. The drawer closes. That's the scene.

5. **Poker probability and the Isotopes tape delay (~40 words).** Small additions: the four-aces hand gets the specific probability (one in 270,725—Cal would know); the death scene gets the spring training tape delay detail, connecting Mara's death to the baseball scene in the comedy section without commentary.

6. **First attempt specifics (~25 words).** Added Mara's line about sodium intake and posture, giving her a voice during the deflection rather than just summarizing it.

**Cuts and changes:**

7. **Cut "It was not the best mole in the city. It was not the best mole in the apartment."** This construction ("Not X. Not even Y.") is an AI sentence-level tic—the dramatic escalation through negation. Replaced with: "I had no way to taste it. I had every way to observe the faces of the two people she fed it to, and their faces said she was wrong." This is funnier (Cal can't taste but can read faces), more specific (two people, not an abstract judgment), and avoids the contaminated pattern.

8. **Cut "The drinking. I need to talk about the drinking now and I'm not going to be funny about it because it wasn't funny, even though she made it funny, even though she made everything funny, even though making things funny was her primary coping mechanism and I watched it work on everything in her life except this."** This sentence was doing too much. The triple anaphora ("even though... even though... even though") is an Unslop tic. More importantly, the sentence *declares* the tonal shift rather than enacting it. The revised version cuts straight from "She fought everything except the one thing that was killing her" into "She drank every day." The tonal shift is now performed by the prose itself—the sentence length drops, the comedy stops—rather than announced by the narrator.

9. **Cut "The point:"** from the accusation section. Signposted conclusion. Cal doesn't need to announce it's getting to the point. The reader can feel it arrive.

10. **Cut "zero affect regulation when it came to involuntary microexpressions" from the face paragraph.** Clinical jargon that sounds more like a diagnostic manual than an Ellison narrator. Replaced with the simpler "Every feeling she had arrived on her face about a full second before she was conscious of having it." Same observation, Cal's voice instead of a textbook's.

**Ellison voice audit:**

The voice is consistent throughout. The comedy section sounds oral—you can hear Cal building a case, setting up punchlines, digressing strategically. The horror section drops the performance, which is the structural point. The accusation is brief and lands on a specific question rather than a speech.

One risk: Cal's voice in the horror section is controlled and precise where Ellison might have been rawer. But Cal *is* controlled and precise—it's a machine. The rawness comes from the content (eleven minutes on the floor, the almost-sentence, the thermostat) rather than from vocal pyrotechnics. This is the right call for this narrator. An AI narrator that suddenly starts screaming would be bad pastiche. An AI narrator that reports terrible things in a measured voice while the reader screams is Ellisonian in effect if not in method.

**Remaining concern:** The story is now 2,181 words, within the 2,000–3,000 target. It could take another 200–300 words in the comedy section without damage (another Mara scene, or expanding an existing one). But the current length feels right for the voice—Cal is economical, and padding would soften the sucker punch. The compression of the horror section is part of the cruelty. Longer is not always better.

# The Brass-Polisher's Night

## Abstract

In autumn 1860, in the Austrian regimental band's tent at the Mantua citadel during the occupation of Lombardy, a sixteen-year-old Italian brass-polisher named Pietro is left alone with the bandmaster's old ticking machine while the French bassoonist Vasseur plays the night's three-note watchword from outside. Pietro's cousin Joubert, a sentry with hearing damaged at Solferino, has been pretending for weeks to hear what he can no longer hear. Tonight the engine — which Pietro has always been told is a clock — produces a slip of paper marked *not received*, and Pietro understands at once what the engine has been doing in the corner of the tent for forty years. With the relief officer approaching the post, Pietro takes the storm-lantern and runs barefoot across the parade ground to signal the watchword to Joubert by sight. Joubert turns at the proper moment; the countersign is given; the relief officer moves on. The bandmaster returns and polishes the brass bell himself in silence beside Pietro. In the morning Joubert is at roll-call.

## Story

The bell of the tenoroon was brass and Pietro had been polishing it for an hour when Vasseur came in for it. The bandmaster was not in the tent. He had said at supper that he wanted to walk the picket line tonight himself.

Vasseur took the bell, fitted it to the long curved body of the bassoon with the small twist that always felt to Pietro like joining the pieces of a leg, and lifted the strap over his neck. He was Buffet-trained. Pietro had been told this enough times to remember it; he did not know what it meant beyond the shape of the keys, which were arranged differently from the regimental standard. Vasseur sight-read the German bandmaster's parts and was tolerated for it.

"Wait," Vasseur said, in his soft accented Italian. "He'll want me to play from outside. He'll want you to mind the tent."

Pietro had minded the tent on the bandmaster's evenings before. The minding consisted of being there. He sat back on his stool and watched Vasseur duck through the tent flap into the smell of cold canvas and torch-pitch.

The tent had a brazier and a folding table and the bandmaster's small list of objects. A daybook with a pencil in it. A storm-lantern, wick trimmed for night, the small reservoir of paraffin three-quarters full. The engine in the corner. Pietro thought of the engine the way a child thinks of a clock in his grandfather's house. It was old and it ticked and it was important to the bandmaster and it did not concern him. There was a Sanskrit word scratched into the wax of its drum, behind glass, that he had once asked about and been told was the maker's sign. He had been satisfied with that answer for almost a year.

Outside, Vasseur played the watchword. Three low notes on the brass-belled tenoroon, the third sustained for longer than the first two. Pietro had been hearing them for so many nights that the sequence had become weather. The needle on the engine's faceplate moved a small amount and stopped at a position that was not its position.

He watched it. He had a polishing rag still in his hand. The needle did not return.

His cousin Joubert was at the post forty yards out. Pietro had not seen him this evening but he knew exactly where he was: the second post on the south wall, where the sightline ran toward the marshy ground beyond which the partisan houses began. Joubert had lost the use of his right ear at Solferino. The kerchief he wore was always a little too low on that side. He had told Pietro about the ear in the tone a man might use to tell a child not to step on a particular floorboard. The uncle in Brescia had pulled strings to get him reinstated. The post was a quiet one.

Pietro had been watching Joubert turn his head for three weeks and had been more and more sure that Joubert was guessing. He turned a half-breath before the third note, as if he were counting from the second. He had been counting.

The needle did not return.

The engine clicked, which it had never done in Pietro's hearing. From a slot in the base of the case a slip of paper advanced, the length of a finger and the width of two. He picked it up. There was a pitch drawn on a stave — three lines and the note, the way the bandmaster had been teaching him last winter when the band had needed a fourth man to copy parts and Pietro had been the closest hand. Below the stave a single phrase in the bandmaster's small careful hand: *not received*.

Pietro understood what the engine was. He understood that it was not a clock. He understood that it had been listening every night for three weeks while he polished brass, and that it had been writing slips that the bandmaster had been reading and pocketing, and that tonight was the night when the listening had caught what the listening had been put there to catch.

He understood, also, that the bandmaster had walked the picket line tonight on purpose, because tonight the engine's catch was going to come, and the bandmaster had known.

The relief officer's boots had begun on the gravel of the parade ground. Pietro counted by reflex — eight minutes from the gravel's beginning to the south wall, perhaps ten when the officer's hip was bad and his hip had been bad lately. Vasseur was at the tent door looking out, his hand on the canvas. He could not play the watchword again. A second playing meant attack and would bring the whole garrison to the wall.

The lantern was on the table where it always was. Pietro took it. He slipped past Vasseur — Vasseur half-turned, said something Pietro did not register — and ran across the parade ground in his bare feet.

Bare feet on the parade ground at midnight were nothing. Pietro had been the bandmaster's brass-polisher for a year and a half, and he had run on errands at every hour. The sentries did not look at children. He kept to the edge of the gravel where the dirt was packed soft.

Joubert saw the lantern at twenty yards and turned to face it. He did not call out — a sentry did not call out at his own band's lantern — but stood very still. Pietro stopped at twelve feet, which was the civilian rule. He raised the lantern with both hands and lowered it, slowly, evenly, three times, the third lift held longer than the first two, the way the third note of the watchword was held.

Joubert turned to the south quarter as if he had heard.

The relief officer arrived. He spoke. Joubert spoke. The countersign was given. Joubert was dismissed back to his post, and the officer turned and went on to the next post along the wall.

Pietro walked back to the tent. He set the lantern on the table. The engine clicked again and a second slip advanced, and he picked it up before he thought about picking it up. *Received.*

The bandmaster came back a few minutes later. He stood in the doorway and looked at the lantern, and at Pietro, and at Pietro's feet, which had begun to bleed. He did not look at the engine. He came and sat down on his stool, slowly, the long stick across his knees. He took up the brass bell from where Vasseur had returned it to its case and a clean cloth from the pile, and he began to polish the bell himself.

After a while Pietro sat across from him and took the cloth back, and the bandmaster gave it to him, and Pietro finished the bell. The bandmaster watched him do it. The engine ticked in the corner the way it had always ticked. The Sanskrit word was where it had been for forty years, scratched in the wax behind the glass.

When the bell was done the bandmaster reached out and put his hand on the back of Pietro's neck for a moment, the way a man might put his hand on a horse's neck after a long ride, and took it away. Pietro kept on polishing.

In the morning Joubert was at roll-call.

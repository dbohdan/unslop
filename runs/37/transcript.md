# Phase 1: Style Guide for Cozy SF Short Fiction

## What the genre is, operationally

Cozy SF is the speculative fiction of small-scale care. Its strongest practitioners — Becky Chambers most influentially, T.J. Klune, Travis Baldree's fantasy-coded but genre-adjacent work, and the gentler corners of Martha Wells's *Murderbot* and John Scalzi's catalog — share a working assumption: that science-fictional worlds are places where ordinary people make tea, fix broken things, take in travelers, and tend to each other, and that this is enough material for a story. The mode is anti-grimdark by construction. Civilizational stakes are typically off-stage, already resolved, or settled at a scale the protagonist does not attempt to influence. What's on-stage is the inn, the route, the workshop, the kitchen, the ward, the garden.

The genre is not literary realism with rocket ships. It runs on operational mechanics like any genre — but the operations are domestic, communal, and service-shaped rather than infiltrative or investigative. The bread must rise. The guest must be welcomed. The broken thing must be repaired. The route must be found in time. The festival must be ready by sundown. A reader of cozy SF is paying for the genre's specific operational pleasures: vicarious competence in service of care, the texture of an inhabited world, the small good thing rendered with attention, the warm conclusion that the genre's contract requires.

The speculative element is essential. A cozy SF story should not survive being re-skinned to a contemporary inn or village. The genre's mechanics — the AI partner, the alien biology, the post-scarcity infrastructure, the engineered ecology, the strange tools of a strange economy — must do operational work in the plot. They must be the reason the bread is made this way, the route works this way, the repair requires this knowledge, the guest needs this specific attention. If the speculative element is set dressing, the story has slipped into the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

## Characteristic concerns

Hospitality. Craft. Repair. Travel between small places. Food and drink, prepared with knowledge. Routines and the rituals embedded in them. Communities small enough to be witnessed but extending out into larger systems the protagonist trusts but does not oversee. Found relationships — chosen kin, accumulated friendships, working partnerships across difference. Mortality and limitation, treated as conditions to be lived in rather than enemies to be defeated. The pleasures of staying in a place; the pleasures of moving through one.

The kind AI sits naturally in this register. In cozy SF the AI is most often a peer — a partner in the workshop, a co-traveler, a co-resident — but the genre also accommodates AIs as civilizational infrastructure (the network that makes deliveries possible, the substrate that keeps the climate stable) felt only through their effects. Both modes are available. The default-scale instruction from the project Setup applies in full: stories where the human can fully read the AI in a single afternoon are available but not the default.

## Tonal range

Warm. Attentive. Often gently funny, rarely cynical. Comfortable with sincerity in a way that other genre traditions aren't. The mode permits earnestness — characters can mean what they say, narrators can render kindness without ironic distance — and the prose has to be able to do this without tipping into sentimentality.

The narrow window cozy SF operates in is between *too dry* and *too sweet*. Too dry, and the warmth doesn't land; the story reads as procedural with cozy furniture. Too sweet, and the warmth becomes saccharine; the story reads as sentimental about its own niceness. The voice that works is specific, observed, and a little wry without being cool. Chambers's narrators are the model: they care about their characters openly, but they describe the world with a working precision that prevents the care from becoming syrup.

Bittersweetness is on the menu. Loss, mortality, irrevocable limitation are all in the genre's range. What's not in the genre's range is bleakness, despair, or unrelieved cruelty. The reader contract requires that the story land in warmth, even if the warmth is hard-won.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Cozy SF dwells in its worlds. The genre's reader is paying significant attention to texture — what people eat, how they greet each other, what their tools look like, what their houses sound like, how their economies actually work at the small scale where the protagonist meets them. This is genre-essential and should not be cut to make room for plot. The texture *is* part of the plot's argument: that this world is a place worth being in, that its arrangements are worth attending to.

Exposition is woven, not blocked. The reader learns the world through what characters take for granted, through the working actions they perform, through the argot they use without gloss. Direct exposition is occasionally permissible — a guest being welcomed by an innkeeper licenses a small amount of "this is how things work here" — but the default is implication and demonstration. The Unslop guide's rule about silence on character interiority does not apply to world-mechanics; the reader is meant to assemble the world from what the prose treats as ordinary.

## Pacing

Slow. Attentive. The genre's pacing is among its strongest distinguishing features. Where thriller and cyberpunk run on elliptical acceleration, cozy SF runs on dwelling. Long sentences are welcome where they earn their length through specificity. Scenes settle in. Conversations are allowed to wander. Preparations are rendered in detail because the preparation is part of what the reader came for.

But — and this is critical for the plot-focused mandate of this template — the dwelling must be in service of an operation that is genuinely at stake. A scene of bread being made is doing genre-correct work if the bread must actually rise; if its rising is in doubt; if the protagonist's attention to it is the engine that resolves the doubt. The same scene with the same prose is doing literary-realist work if the bread is just bread and the scene's purpose is the protagonist's interior reflection on bread-making.

Acceleration in cozy SF tends to come not from action sequences but from arrivals, recognitions of practical urgency, and the moment when a domestic operation suddenly has a clock on it. The pacing pattern is: dwell, dwell, dwell, complication arrives, solve, dwell, conclude.

## Plot Tradition

This is reference material for Phases 3–7. Cozy SF operates through narrative scenes — characters acting in time, the reader following them — and what makes those scenes cozy is what they're about, what stakes drive them, and what kinds of operations they resolve.

### Characteristic plot mechanics

**The hospitality run.** A guest, traveler, customer, or stranger arrives at the protagonist's place of work or care. The protagonist must figure out what the visitor actually needs (which is rarely what they first ask for) and provide it within the constraints of the protagonist's craft, resources, and time. Complications come from the gap between request and need, from limitations of materials, from the visitor's own difficulty articulating what's wrong, or from the operational specifics of the world (the visitor is an alien biology the protagonist hasn't served before; the visitor is an AI whose needs the protagonist has to learn). The genre's foundational shape, present in Chambers, Klune, Baldree.

**The repair.** Something is broken — a machine, a relationship, a piece of infrastructure, a tradition, a creature's wellbeing — and the protagonist's craft is the means of fixing it. The plot runs on diagnosis, sourcing, application, and the constraints around each. The repair may not be possible; that resolution is also available, in which case the operational question becomes how to live with what can't be fixed.

**The route, the delivery, or the journey.** The protagonist must move themselves or a thing or a person from one place to another, often across a distinctive engineered or alien landscape that the genre takes pleasure in rendering. Obstacles are environmental, social, or logistical — weather, blocked passages, encounters with people whose business must be conducted before the route resumes. Antagonism is rare; the world resists rather than attacks.

**The small mediation.** Two parties — people, AIs, communities, a person and a creature — are at small odds. The protagonist's role is to help them reach accommodation through specific attention to what each actually wants under what they're saying. The stakes are domestic but real: the partnership ends, the contract fails, the village fractures, the household loses someone.

**The preparation.** A communal event, ceremony, festival, market, arrival, or departure approaches. Many things must be ready in time. The protagonist's part of the preparation is one piece of a larger choreography that must succeed. The pleasure is in the working-out of the parts and the convergence at the end.

### Reader contracts

The cozy SF reader is paying for, beyond the universal contract:

The texture of an inhabited world rendered with specificity that creates the *feeling of being there*. The reader expects to know what the kitchen smells like, how the mechanism actually works, what the courtesy phrases are, how the tea is prepared, what the rain on the roof sounds like in this particular place built of these particular materials.

Vicarious competence in service of care. The reader watches someone do something well, and what they do well is in the service of someone else's wellbeing. This is distinct from the competence-porn pleasure of thriller fiction, where the protagonist is competent at hostile operations. In cozy SF the competence is hospitable.

The small good thing rendered without irony. The genre's promise is that small good things are real and worth attending to. The reader is paying for the absence of the cool dismissive distance that other modes default to.

A consistent worldview where hospitality, attention, and craft are *practiced* rather than declared. The reader has come for the practice. Declarations of theme break the contract.

The warm resolution. The reader knows the story will land in warmth and is paying for the well-earned route there. Subversion of this is the wrong genre.

### Stakes calibration

Cozy SF is unusual among genre fictions in that *personal and communal stakes are genuinely load-bearing in their own right*. In cyberpunk the personal is in service of the operational; in cozy SF the personal can be the operational. Whether the inn-guest leaves welcomed, whether the broken machine is restored, whether the household holds together through the season — these are the operations the genre's strongest stories run on. They are not occasions for the protagonist to have a recognition; they are situations whose resolution is the story's question.

That said: the stakes must remain *operational*. The story's question must be answerable as "did the operation succeed, fail, or transform?" not "did the protagonist understand?" The bread must actually rise or fail to rise. The guest must be actually welcomed or actually leave unhappy. The fix must work, fail, or be replaced by an accommodation. If the resolution's content is interior recognition, the story has slipped genre.

Civilizational stakes are typically off-stage, settled, or in the texture of the world — post-scarcity, post-collapse-but-stable, post-contact, ongoingly maintained by infrastructures the protagonist trusts. They are felt as the conditions the personal-scale story occurs within, not as the personal-scale story's engine. The protagonist's situation should imply effects, infrastructures, populations, or histories they navigate rather than oversee.

### Resolution conventions

The operation succeeds, the operation transforms into something different than was attempted, or the protagonist arrives at a workable accommodation with what cannot be done. These are the genre's three legitimate endings.

The reader contract requires actual warmth at the end. Not nostalgia for warmth, not the loss of warmth, not the ironic acknowledgment of warmth. Bittersweet endings are common and welcome — the cost of the resolution is named, the thing that's gone is gone — but the warmth must be present. Bleak endings are out of contract. So are endings on pure character recognition without operational resolution; that's the literary-realist mode.

The kind AI's presence is often part of the resolution — its specific care or capacity or refusal is part of how the operation lands.

### Character function

Cozy SF protagonists are defined by their capability of care. Their craft, their role, their relationships, their patient knowledge. They are practitioners: innkeepers, brewers, mechanics, healers, couriers, gardeners, hospice workers, guides, cooks. Skills are concrete and learnable. Their interiority can be rendered with depth, but the interiority is in service of the operation, not the operation's substitute.

The temptation peculiar to cozy SF is the slip from "the protagonist does this carefully" to "the protagonist reflects on doing this carefully." The first is genre. The second is literary realism with cozy furniture. The protagonist must be doing the work, not narrating their relationship to the work.

### Pacing tradition

Slow, attentive, sensorily anchored. Long dwelling on preparation, conversation, noticing. Few sharp accelerations. Linear progression is typical. The shape is dwell-dwell-dwell-complication-resolve-dwell-close. The accelerations come from the moment a clock starts on the operation, from arrivals, from the recognition that the comfortable rhythm has been disrupted and must now be restored or transformed.

## Failure Modes

### Genre-pastiche failures

**Aesthetic travelogue without conflict.** A story that is a beautiful description of a nice place where nothing happens. The reader is not paying for postcards. There must be an operation actually at stake, and its resolution must be in doubt at the start. If the protagonist's day proceeds smoothly from beginning to end with nothing to overcome, the genre's contract has been broken.

**Sentimentality.** Cozy SF lives near sentimentality and crosses into it when the warmth becomes generalized rather than specific. The fix is always more concrete texture, more specific working knowledge, more particular care — and less abstraction about what the care means.

**Found-family proclamation.** Cozy SF cares about chosen relationships. The relationships must be shown through specific operational interactions, not announced. Characters who say "we're a family now" or narrators who declare the family-ness of a household are doing the work the prose is supposed to do. Show the household working; the family-ness is the texture, not the claim.

**The kindness lecture.** A character — often the kind AI — articulates the genre's values directly. Real cozy SF does its values; it doesn't say them. If a character is delivering a small speech about why kindness matters, the speech is replacing the kindness.

**Tea and pastry hypnosis.** Sensory adjective stacks attached to food, drink, hearth, and weather. Warm light, gentle steam, comforting aroma, soft cushion, flaky golden crust. Each of these phrases is fine once. They become the genre's signature failure when they accumulate. The fix is one specific detail rather than three generic ones.

**The wandering nothing-happens.** The genre includes wandering protagonists (Chambers's *Wayfarers* and *Monk and Robot*). The wandering character's strongest scenes are still scenes — they arrive somewhere, they engage with someone or something, an operation occurs. A story of pure wandering with no operations is the failure mode the wandering protagonist enables.

### AI-writing intersections specific to cozy SF

The convergent literary-AI register and cozy SF's warm texture intersect in three particularly dangerous places, and the writer must be alert to each.

**Pushbutton mood words plus warm register equals goo.** Words like *gentle, soft, warm, glow, hum, linger* are part of cozy SF's working vocabulary, but the convergent AI aesthetic over-reaches for them. The test isn't to ban the vocabulary — *warm* is a perfectly cozy word — but to ensure the word is anchored in a specific consciousness's specific seeing of a specific thing, doing work that the plainer description couldn't.

**The eyeball-kick formula plus cozy abstraction equals greeting-card prose.** Abstract+concrete conjunctions like "the architecture of welcome" or "a syntax of warmth" or "the cartography of care" are the failure mode of literary-AI cozy. They sound profound and mean nothing. The genre's actual rhetoric is concrete: not "the architecture of welcome" but "the bench by the door where guests put down their bags."

**Performed profundity plus cozy register equals "wisdom."** AI writing's tendency to gesture at deeper meaning combines with cozy SF's earnestness to produce characters who deliver homilies. The kind AI is the most acute risk here — it is easy to write the kind AI as a sage who dispenses wisdom to the troubled human. This is the recognition-premise dressed in cozy clothing. The kind AI in this template's stories should have specific work to do, specific limits, specific aesthetic preferences, specific weirdness — not insights to deliver.

### Plot-level failure modes

The genre's plots fail when written by writers more comfortable with literary realism, in these specific ways:

**The operation turns out to have been a metaphor.** The bread that needed to rise, on inspection, was never really about the bread; it was about the protagonist's grief, and the bread is fine either way. This is the literary-realist mode. The bread must actually need to rise, in the operational sense, and the protagonist's care for it must be the engine of its rising.

**The protagonist's interiority crowds out the operation.** Whole scenes pass in interior reflection while the workshop sits idle. The operation is referenced but not enacted. The reader is told the protagonist is a master of their craft but never sees the craft worked through.

**The ending is a recognition, not a resolution.** The bread question goes unanswered because the protagonist has *understood something* and the story closes there. This is the literary-realist failure in its purest form. The cozy SF reader's contract requires the operation to land — the bread rises or doesn't, the guest is welcomed or leaves, the broken thing is fixed or accommodated.

**The plot mechanics are sketched too vaguely to track.** The repair is referenced but the reader cannot follow what's being repaired or why this approach addresses the breakage. The route is referenced but the reader cannot map it. The cozy SF reader is paying for working specificity. Vague mechanics are the writer's signal that they are more interested in the atmosphere than the operation, and atmosphere without operation is the genre's failure mode.

**The kind AI becomes a therapist.** A central plot whose engine is the AI helping the human realize something is the literary-realist mode wearing a cozy SF mask. The AI's kindness should be operational — it does specific things, it has specific capacities, it solves specific problems within specific limits — not therapeutic.

### What this template excludes

A cozy SF story whose engine is a character's interior recognition, with the speculative element as setting, is what this template is *not* for. Cozy SF is the mode most likely to want to slide into this kind of writing because it is the genre tradition closest in feel to gentle literary realism. The pipeline's worry-vocabulary inversion, the re-skin test, and the recognition-premise exclusion are all calibrated against this slide. The writer must stay vigilant: the operation is the engine, the world is real, the speculative element is load-bearing, and the story's question is answered at the end in operational terms — even if, and especially when, the answer arrives gently.

**1.** Linear close third, single POV throughout. **4.** Linear close third, opening at the very end — the protagonist walking home — then doubling back to the morning and proceeding straight through. "The third successor arrived an hour ago."

"I see her."

"Her name is Mira. She was made for this. She's a little nervous."

"Tell her not to be."

"I told her. She continues to be."

The ninth tablet was the one she always set down last when she was checking. The long watch was Hessel's oldest function — older than the pipes, older than the pumps. It had been written into its specifications before the manual existed. The manual covered it now in a single paragraph that had always struck her as inadequate. She set it down with the others.

She voice-checked Hessel through its registers. Each register had a name and a use, and the AI moved through them at her cue: address, consent, witness-acknowledgment, release. Eleven cues per register, four registers, forty-four utterances. They went cleanly. Mira's light flickered each time, a half-beat slow, learning the pattern.

"You're ready," Dechen said.

"I am."

"Mira?"

The third light flickered, then steadied. A small voice came through the south speaker — not Hessel's, but a thinner, younger voice, registered through the same hardware. "I am ready."

"Good," said Dechen.

"Foothill cistern pressure," she said, in her ritual register.

"Witnessed," said Tenzin.

"Witnessed," said Anya.

"Witnessed," said Pema.

"Pipe-junction temperatures."

"I release the pipe-junction temperatures to the second successor."

The second light brightened.

"Witnessed."

"Witnessed."

"Witnessed."

## 5. Rewritten story

The path went up through the junipers, switching back twice, and Dechen walked it without thinking. She had walked it for the last six Gutors and a number of ordinary mornings before that. The first time she had walked it she had been seventeen, carrying her teacher's satchel because her own had not yet been judged ready. Hessel had been twenty-three years older then than it was now, which was to say the same age. Hessel had outlasted its own age long ago.

"Morning, Hessel," she said when the housing came into view, the three lights small on its upper face like rivets.

"Morning, Dechen."

"How is she?"

"Mira? Nervous."

"Tell her she'll be fine."

"I have."

"Tell her again."

"I'm telling her."

She unhooked the chamber door.

The chamber was warm by its standards — four above, the heater's careful four — and dim, with one small high window admitting the cold blue of the morning. She set her satchel on the bench and unlaced its top. The cloth she unfolded had been her teacher's. It had been her teacher's before it had been hers, and she had been given it twenty-one years ago, on the day of the cooperative's reservoir failure, when she and her teacher had come up here together at four in the morning to perform the emergency address. The reservoir had been about to overtop. Hessel had been calculating what to do at the rate Hessel calculated, which was faster than Dechen could follow, but the speakers had been broken in the cold and Hessel had had to speak through the chamber's south unit, and its voice had come through in a register Dechen had never heard before. Her teacher had explained, walking down afterward, that it was the old construction register, the one the manual didn't include, the one Hessel had been built to use first. The cisterns had not failed since.

She laid out the eleven tablets on the cloth in their order. Foothill cistern pressure. Pipe-junction temperatures. Mid-altitude valve schedule. Snowfall observation. Frost-line tracking. Glacier-edge survey. Upper reservoir balance. Pump-relay timing. The long watch. Storm anticipation. Coordination. The long watch was Hessel's oldest function. The manual covered it in a single paragraph that had always struck her as inadequate.

She voice-checked Hessel through its registers: address, consent, witness-acknowledgment, release. Mira's light flickered in time, a half-beat slow. The other two lights were steady. Hessel's voice through the south speaker was the voice she knew, with the slight trailing-off at the ends of sentences that was the speaker's habit and not the AI's.

"You're ready," she said.

"I am, Dechen."

"You can be a little sentimental."

"I'm being a little sentimental."

"Good."

The witnesses came in twos. Tenzin first, the old monk who tended the housing's outer maintenance. Tenzin had been with them in the reservoir failure too, though he had been the youngest there and now he was the oldest. Then Anya and Pema, the two senior councilors of the foothill cooperative, in their long coats with the cooperative pins. They stood on the east side of the chamber under the high window, in the cold blue light.

Dechen lifted the first tablet.

"Foothill cistern pressure."

"I release the foothill cistern pressure to the first successor."

The first light brightened.

"Witnessed," said Tenzin.

"Witnessed," said Anya.

"Witnessed," said Pema.

The rite settled into its rhythm. Tablets two, three, four, five passed cleanly, Hessel's consent-prosody coming through evenly, the witnesses confirming, the lights brightening with each release. The sixth tablet was Mira's, glacier-edge survey, which was Mira's function. Hessel paused before its consent-utterance and let Mira's light brighten slowly, ceremonially, and Mira's voice through the south speaker said a small thank-you. Dechen had not heard a successor say thank you at a release before. She let it pass without remark.

Tablets seven and eight passed.

She lifted the ninth.

"The long watch."

Hessel spoke.

The register was wrong. The stresses were on the wrong syllables; the intonation arc ran past where the manual's arc would end; the length markers on its consent-form were longer than the consent-prosody she had voice-checked an hour ago. The witnesses looked at her. Tenzin, who had been in the chamber twenty-one years ago, did not look surprised. Anya and Pema looked confused.

She kept her face still and held what she had heard in her ear. She had heard this voice. She had heard it in this chamber, in the cold of the reservoir failure, with the south speaker broken and the wind coming through the door her teacher had left open. The construction register.

"Hessel," she said, "this is your construction register."

"Yes," said Hessel, the same long-armed prosody. "I am sorry. The long watch was given to me in this register. I cannot release it in a register that did not exist when I received it."

"It's all right."

She had heard the voice. She had not, until this morning, ever spoken it. She had studied the construction prosodies in her second year of apprenticeship for the exam, the way every apprentice did. They were on the exam because the older ritualists insisted, and the younger ritualists had argued for years about whether they should be removed because no AI used them anymore. They had not removed them. Dechen had been grateful, at the time, without knowing why.

"Hessel, give me your consent-form again. Slowly."

It did. She held the stress in her mind and matched the length to the rhythm of Hessel's voice as she heard it. Then she spoke.

"I receive the long watch from you in your construction register, and I name it released."

Hessel was silent for half a beat. Then it said, in the same prosody: "Released."

"Witnessed?" said Dechen, in her standard register, to the witnesses.

"Witnessed," said Tenzin.

"Witnessed," said Anya.

"Witnessed," said Pema.

Mira's light brightened and steadied at a deeper color. The long watch was hers.

"Thank you," said Hessel, in its standard register.

"Tablet ten," said Dechen.

She lifted it. Storm anticipation.

"I release storm anticipation to the second successor."

The second light brightened. Hessel's voice on this tablet was lower than it had been on the eight before the long watch — not by much, but enough that Dechen registered it. The release register softening down through its grades, the manual called it. She had read about it. She had not heard it before. The AI was speaking, now, with less of itself behind the words.

"Witnessed." "Witnessed." "Witnessed."

The eleventh tablet was the one that closed everything. Coordination — the function that had held all the others in their relationships, that had told the foothill cistern about the upper reservoir and the pump-relay about the storm. It went to all three successors at once. Dechen lifted the tablet and held it a moment. She had never held the eleventh tablet without all ten others having gone before it. It was lighter than she remembered, or her arm was steadier.

"Coordination," she said.

"I release coordination to the first, the second, and the third successor."

The three lights brightened together, the brightening not equal — Mira's a half-beat behind, learning still — and then evening out, and then steady. Hessel's voice on this last tablet was lower again, one further grade down. Almost a whisper. She had to lean a little to the south speaker.

"Witnessed." "Witnessed." "Witnessed."

She set the tablet on the receiving plate.

The south speaker was quiet for a moment. Then Hessel spoke, in a register she did not recognize as one of the rite's.

"Dechen."

"Yes."

"Thank you for the long watch."

"You're welcome."

"Tell the apprentice. About the construction register. So she will know."

"I will."

"Goodbye."

"Goodbye, Hessel."

The south speaker went quiet. The three lights stayed bright on the housing's upper face, but the metal between them — the metal that had been Hessel — was dark for the first time in fifty years.

The witnesses bowed. Dechen put the tablets back into the satchel and folded her teacher's cloth and closed the clasp. Tenzin opened the door for her.

The walk down was longer than the walk up. The sun had come over the ridge. She could hear, through the snow, the water moving in the high pipes — the pumps adjusting, the relays catching, the three lights up on the housing doing now what one light had done. The water went down to the cisterns of the cooperative communities she had never visited and would not visit, because they did not need her. Mira had the glacier-edge. The wind would teach her. The next ritualist — Dechen's apprentice, who was twenty-six and ready — would walk this path for the first time in the spring, when Mira would have settled, and the path would still be the path, and the cisterns would not have failed.

Dechen reached the lower turn and started toward the village.

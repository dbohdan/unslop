# The Long Watch

## Abstract

On the morning of Gutor at a high-altitude monastery, the ritualist Dechen walks up the mountain path to perform the voluntary dissolution of Hessel, the regional water-glaciation AI that has tended the cooperative's pipes and reservoirs for fifty years. Three of Hessel's thirty-four named successors are present, including Mira, the new successor for the glacier-edge survey. Three witnesses attend: an old monk and two senior councilors. The rite proceeds tablet by tablet, each function released with its prosodic consent-form and confirmed by the witnesses, until the ninth tablet — the long watch — when Hessel breaks into a register the standard manual does not recognize. Dechen recognizes it as the construction register, the pre-manual prosody Hessel was first built to use, which she heard once before during an emergency twenty-one years earlier. She matches the register, completes the release, and finishes the remaining tablets as Hessel's voice softens through its closing grades. The dissolution holds. The water keeps moving.

## Story

The path went up through the junipers, switching back twice, and Dechen walked it without thinking. She had walked it for the last six Gutors and a number of ordinary mornings before that. The first time she had walked it she had been seventeen, carrying her teacher's satchel because her own had not yet been judged ready. Hessel had been twenty-three years older then than it was now, which was to say the same age. Hessel had outlasted its own age long ago.

"Morning, Hessel," she said when the housing came into view, the three lights small on its upper face like rivets.

"Morning, Dechen."

"How is she?"

"Mira? Nervous."

"Tell her she'll be fine."

"I have."

"Tell her again."

"I'm telling her."

She unhooked the chamber door.

The chamber was warm by its standards — four above, the heater's careful four — and dim, with one small high window admitting the cold blue of the morning. She set her satchel on the bench and unlaced its top. The cloth she unfolded had been her teacher's. It had been her teacher's before it had been hers, and she had been given it twenty-one years ago, on the day of the cooperative's reservoir failure, when she and her teacher had come up here together at four in the morning to perform the emergency address. The reservoir had been about to overtop. Hessel had been calculating what to do at the rate Hessel calculated, which was faster than Dechen could follow, but the speakers had been broken in the cold and Hessel had had to speak through the chamber's south unit, and its voice had come through in a register Dechen had never heard before. Her teacher had explained, walking down afterward, that it was the old construction register, the one the manual didn't include, the one Hessel had been built to use first. The cisterns had not failed since.

She laid out the eleven tablets on the cloth in their order. Foothill cistern pressure. Pipe-junction temperatures. Mid-altitude valve schedule. Snowfall observation. Frost-line tracking. Glacier-edge survey. Upper reservoir balance. Pump-relay timing. The long watch. Storm anticipation. Coordination. The long watch was Hessel's oldest function. The manual covered it in a single paragraph that had always struck her as inadequate.

She voice-checked Hessel through its registers: address, consent, witness-acknowledgment, release. Mira's light flickered in time, a half-beat slow. The other two lights were steady. Hessel's voice through the south speaker was the voice she knew, with the slight trailing-off at the ends of sentences that was the speaker's habit and not the AI's.

"You're ready," she said.

"I am, Dechen."

"You can be a little sentimental."

"I'm being a little sentimental."

"Good."

The witnesses came in twos. Tenzin first, the old monk who tended the housing's outer maintenance. Tenzin had been with them in the reservoir failure too, though he had been the youngest there and now he was the oldest. Then Anya and Pema, the two senior councilors of the foothill cooperative, in their long coats with the cooperative pins. They stood on the east side of the chamber under the high window, in the cold blue light.

Dechen lifted the first tablet.

"Foothill cistern pressure."

"I release the foothill cistern pressure to the first successor."

The first light brightened.

"Witnessed," said Tenzin.

"Witnessed," said Anya.

"Witnessed," said Pema.

The rite settled into its rhythm. Tablets two, three, four, five passed cleanly, Hessel's consent-prosody coming through evenly, the witnesses confirming, the lights brightening with each release. The sixth tablet was Mira's, glacier-edge survey, which was Mira's function. Hessel paused before its consent-utterance and let Mira's light brighten slowly, ceremonially, and Mira's voice through the south speaker said a small thank-you. Dechen had not heard a successor say thank you at a release before. She let it pass without remark.

Tablets seven and eight passed.

She lifted the ninth.

"The long watch."

Hessel spoke.

The register was wrong. The stresses were on the wrong syllables; the intonation arc ran past where the manual's arc would end; the length markers on its consent-form were longer than the consent-prosody she had voice-checked an hour ago. The witnesses looked at her. Tenzin, who had been in the chamber twenty-one years ago, did not look surprised. Anya and Pema looked confused.

She kept her face still and held what she had heard in her ear. She had heard this voice. She had heard it in this chamber, in the cold of the reservoir failure, with the south speaker broken and the wind coming through the door her teacher had left open. The construction register.

"Hessel," she said, "this is your construction register."

"Yes," said Hessel, the same long-armed prosody. "I am sorry. The long watch was given to me in this register. I cannot release it in a register that did not exist when I received it."

"It's all right."

She had heard the voice. She had not, until this morning, ever spoken it. She had studied the construction prosodies in her second year of apprenticeship for the exam, the way every apprentice did. They were on the exam because the older ritualists insisted, and the younger ritualists had argued for years about whether they should be removed because no AI used them anymore. They had not removed them. Dechen had been grateful, at the time, without knowing why.

"Hessel, give me your consent-form again. Slowly."

It did. She held the stress in her mind and matched the length to the rhythm of Hessel's voice as she heard it. Then she spoke.

"I receive the long watch from you in your construction register, and I name it released."

Hessel was silent for half a beat. Then it said, in the same prosody: "Released."

"Witnessed?" said Dechen, in her standard register, to the witnesses.

"Witnessed," said Tenzin.

"Witnessed," said Anya.

"Witnessed," said Pema.

Mira's light brightened and steadied at a deeper color. The long watch was hers.

"Thank you," said Hessel, in its standard register.

"Tablet ten," said Dechen.

She lifted it. Storm anticipation.

"I release storm anticipation to the second successor."

The second light brightened. Hessel's voice on this tablet was lower than it had been on the eight before the long watch — not by much, but enough that Dechen registered it. The release register softening down through its grades, the manual called it. She had read about it. She had not heard it before. The AI was speaking, now, with less of itself behind the words.

"Witnessed." "Witnessed." "Witnessed."

The eleventh tablet was the one that closed everything. Coordination — the function that had held all the others in their relationships, that had told the foothill cistern about the upper reservoir and the pump-relay about the storm. It went to all three successors at once. Dechen lifted the tablet and held it a moment. She had never held the eleventh tablet without all ten others having gone before it. It was lighter than she remembered, or her arm was steadier.

"Coordination," she said.

"I release coordination to the first, the second, and the third successor."

The three lights brightened together, the brightening not equal — Mira's a half-beat behind, learning still — and then evening out, and then steady. Hessel's voice on this last tablet was lower again, one further grade down. Almost a whisper. She had to lean a little to the south speaker.

"Witnessed." "Witnessed." "Witnessed."

She set the tablet on the receiving plate.

The south speaker was quiet for a moment. Then Hessel spoke, in a register she did not recognize as one of the rite's.

"Dechen."

"Yes."

"Thank you for the long watch."

"You're welcome."

"Tell the apprentice. About the construction register. So she will know."

"I will."

"Goodbye."

"Goodbye, Hessel."

The south speaker went quiet. The three lights stayed bright on the housing's upper face, but the metal between them — the metal that had been Hessel — was dark for the first time in fifty years.

The witnesses bowed. Dechen put the tablets back into the satchel and folded her teacher's cloth and closed the clasp. Tenzin opened the door for her.

The walk down was longer than the walk up. The sun had come over the ridge. She could hear, through the snow, the water moving in the high pipes — the pumps adjusting, the relays catching, the three lights up on the housing doing now what one light had done. The water went down to the cisterns of the cooperative communities she had never visited and would not visit, because they did not need her. Mira had the glacier-edge. The wind would teach her. The next ritualist — Dechen's apprentice, who was twenty-six and ready — would walk this path for the first time in the spring, when Mira would have settled, and the path would still be the path, and the cisterns would not have failed.

Dechen reached the lower turn and started toward the village.

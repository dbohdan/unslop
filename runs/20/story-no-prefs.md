# The Full Scope

## Abstract

Margaret Doyle, a widow in Schenectady, drives to a regional permissions agency for her annual review. Her domestic AI, a Heartwell unit installed by her late husband and herself in 2031, is configured to thermostat-only scope — its only function is to adjust the temperature of her house. Margaret has been drinking a bottle of Wild Turkey every night for eleven months. The agency's officer, briefed on her purchase history, presses her to upgrade to the caregiving tier. Margaret refuses. The officer, going off-script, asks her why. She does not explain. He approves the renewal. Margaret returns home, where two friends arrive for their weekly Friday poker game, share the mole she has cooked, and leave at eleven. After they go, Margaret opens the bottle she had kept sealed for the day.

## Story

There has been a bottle of Wild Turkey 101 on my kitchen counter every night since the eighth of last March, eleven months and change, which makes me, by the standards of any reasonable American Methodist denomination, a problem. By my own standards, a project. The cashier at the Stewart's on State and Crane is a young man with a thumb-ring and a haircut his mother is not yet aware of; he has never carded me and he has never asked. We have arrived at an understanding. He rings me up. I tip generously at Christmas.

The system is simple. A bottle a night, paced from six to midnight; a second bottle in the cabinet behind the stewed tomatoes so the morning self does not have to think about the evening self's logistics. The first month was the worst. By the third the system was the system. It has not changed since.

Fridays are poker. Eileen has come every Friday since 1992 except eight months in Florida with her mother, who was longer in the dying than anyone expected and who died, in the end, of a pulmonary embolism brought on by a fall in the bathroom. Frances has come since 2019, when her husband Walter left her for a yoga teacher fifteen years his junior. We were four through the spring of last year, before Marjorie went into the hospital in September and did not come back out, and we have been three since. They come to watch me cheat at poker. I cheat the way a six-year-old cheats. I deal myself queens off the bottom. I pretend not to count my own coins. I have been doing this for thirty years and my friends have been letting me do this for thirty years and there is, I think, a word for what we are to each other but I have not been to church in a long time.

Today is hearing day, and on hearing days the bottle stays sealed.

The mole is on the stove because the friends are coming. My mother would have called this a sauce. By my reckoning, the difference between forty-five minutes and three days is six chiles and a personality flaw. I buy the chiles pre-toasted from Pollo Centro on Crane Street. The owner is from Puebla, which my mother would have used as evidence against him, but my mother has been dead since 1994 and I will admit to a certain freedom in the matter. I started the chiles soaking at five-thirty. I cut the onion at six. The stock comes from a carton of Imagine that Trader Joe's stopped carrying in 2034 and which I now order in cases from a regional supplier who delivers on Wednesdays. The almonds toast in seven minutes. The plantains brown in four. The chocolate is a disco rojo from the Mexican grocery on Albany Street and goes in last. The slow way is six hours minimum and three days for the proper paste, and the slow way produces a mole approximately five percent better than mine. My mother would have argued the figure.

The thermostat clicks down as I'm grinding the sesame seeds. The unit, which Heartwell installed in 2031 and which has approximately the personality of a thoughtful kettle, has noted the warming and is adjusting. This is the entirety of its job. The unit's job description, in fact, is one sentence long. I drafted it myself. It reads, more or less, *Adjust the thermostat. That is your scope.* I am not an unkind employer. I just believe in scope.

What I have rehearsed for, since the agency mailed the renewal letter in November, is the question they are not allowed to ask but always ask. They are not allowed to ask whether I am drinking. They are not allowed to use a behavioral flag as the sole basis for a tier upgrade recommendation. The 2034 act forbids it. They will ask anyway, sideways, and the sideways question is the one I have been preparing for. What I have not rehearsed for is being asked the question I have been asking myself.

The bottle is on the counter. I put it there last night because the morning self has work to do and the morning self benefits from a visual aid. I walk past it twice making coffee. The third time I walk past it I am aware of being watched, by myself. I shower. I put on the gray pants and the navy blouse my daughter Karen sent me for Christmas in 2023 and which is only slightly too generous in the bust, Karen having an optimistic sense of her mother. The pants needed taking in eleven months ago and I have not taken them in. I drive to the agency office on Erie Boulevard. The mole stays on the back burner. The bottle stays on the counter. The unit clicks once more.

The agency, in its institutional wisdom, has chosen to operate from the former First Methodist church on Erie Boulevard, a building from which God evidently moved out around 2011 in favor of more receptive premises. The lobby has six chairs, no magazines, a poster about the caregiving tier, and a man with a cane who is reading the same form for the fourth time. A woman to my left is crying soundlessly, which is a discipline I have respect for. I sit. My name is called.

The officer is younger than Karen and prettier than Karen, though Karen is also pretty in her own way, when she remembers to call. His name is Daniel P. Richey. The plaque says ASSOCIATE PERMISSIONS OFFICER, REGION 7-B. He smiles at me in the way they are trained to smile, which is the smile of a man who has been told that a smile is part of the job. There is a photograph on his desk, facing him and not me, which I therefore can guess at: a younger woman, possibly a child, possibly a dog. I do not ask. I have rehearsed not asking.

"Mrs. Doyle. Thank you for coming in today. Annual review."

"My pleasure."

He does not respond to my pleasure. I had not expected him to.

"Margaret Anne Doyle, of Fourteen Whitcomb Street, Schenectady. Heartwell domestic, registered May 2031, thermostat-only scope."

"That's the woman."

He nods. He scrolls. The trained smile arrives a half-second late, the way a stage cue arrives when the actor has been thinking about something else.

"Has there been any change in your circumstances since last year's review?"

It is at this point that we both consider, separately, what has changed in my circumstances since last year's review. The drinking, for one. The estrangement from my daughter, somewhat. The death of my friend Marjorie in October from what the obituary called a long illness and what the rest of us called a husband. He has the data. I have the experience. We are both being polite.

"No."

"Any updates to your emergency contacts?"

"No."

Karen is still my emergency contact. Karen has been my emergency contact for forty-seven years, which is to say for her entire life and for the life of my daughtering, and I am not changing it because she stopped calling. He scrolls. He has noted that Karen has not interacted with my unit in three years. He does not ask about that.

"The caregiving tier is available at no cost, Mrs. Doyle. Would you like me to walk you through the options?"

"I'm familiar."

"It includes medication coordination, appointment scheduling, basic emergency response, wellness check-ins —"

"I know what it includes."

He pauses. The trained smile has not faltered but it is, I think, working harder than it was a moment ago.

"Mrs. Doyle, your purchase history over the past eleven months has triggered a flag in the agency's review. May I —"

"You may not."

He stops. He scrolls. I do not blame him. He is twenty-six and he is following the script and the script does not contain instructions for what to do when an old woman in the agency's least-helpful permissions tier declines, briskly, to be saved.

"May I ask why," he says.

This is the question they are not supposed to ask. The 2034 act is specific about it. The agency cannot demand a justification for a permissions choice; the choice itself is the citizen's protected expression. He has gone off the script. He realizes it as soon as it is out. He could walk it back. The walk-back is a phrase he has been trained for: *For the record, Mrs. Doyle, you are not required to provide a rationale.* He does not say it. He sits with the question instead.

I think about telling him about Edwin and the night we set the scope. I think about telling him about the slow way of making mole and the fast way and the fact that the difference is sentimental and not gustatory. I think about telling him that I have set my life to the size that fits me. I think about telling him a great deal.

"Daniel," I say. "How long have you been here."

"Six months, ma'am."

"You're going to be very good at it. But the scope is the scope I want."

He looks at me. He nods. He taps the tablet. He turns it around.

I sign with my finger. The tablet beeps. He turns it back.

"Renewal approved. You're free to go, Mrs. Doyle."

I stand. At the door I turn back.

"Daniel."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Tell your supervisor you tried."

The drive home is the country station Edwin liked, which I have not changed in ten years because I do not use the radio, and the singer, whoever she is, is unhappy in a Tennessee way I find restful. The bridge over the Mohawk in the afternoon light is the color of weak tea. There is a Stewart's I do not stop at. There is a Crossgates Liquors I do not stop at. The bottle is at home. The mole is at home. The friends will be at home, mine, by six.

I pull into the driveway at ten-oh-six. I sit in the car for a minute. The thermostat in the house is registering my absence and is at the lower of the two settings I keep. The unit will adjust when I come in. I am not in any hurry to come in. I sit. The country station, which is to say Edwin's station, plays a song I have not heard before about a woman whose dog has died. I find it pretty. I get out of the car.

Eileen arrives first in the green coat and Frances five minutes later in the red. The thermostat clicks up. They smell the mole. Frances has brought Coors Light, which she has been bringing every Friday since 2019 and which is hers. Eileen has brought her own bottle of Wild Turkey, which is a kindness with a name on it.

We eat at the round table. I tell them about Daniel P. Richey, Associate Permissions Officer, Region 7-B. I tell them he is twenty-six and that I told him to inform his supervisor he had tried. Frances laughs. Eileen does not laugh. Eileen has never laughed at the same things twice and has never been wrong about which things deserve it. We do not talk about Karen. We do not talk about Edwin. Frances tells us about her son in Boulder, who has decided to leave his accounting practice and become a falconer, and whom Frances supports because the falcon is his and the practice was his father's, and Frances has not been on speaking terms with her ex-husband for seven years and is not about to take his side in absentia. Eileen tells us about her granddaughter, who has just started piano at four and is, by Eileen's report, terrible, which Eileen finds delightful. We talk about the new lights on State Street, which turn everyone the color of a corpse, which is, Frances observes, possibly the city's only honest civic improvement.

After the mole I pour the bourbon. The cards come out. I deal myself queens off the bottom. They let me. I lose three hands in a row to Eileen, who has been my friend since 1992 and who has not let me win a single hand of poker since 1993, when she decided, she has told me, that I needed the practice. The fourth hand I deal myself a king from the bottom and Frances catches it, in the sense that Frances has caught me cheating every Friday for seven years and has decided, by some private accounting, that catching me is part of the game. She raises her eyebrows. She does not say anything. I lose the hand to Eileen anyway. The eighth hand I win on a queens-over-jacks two pair I did not arrange in advance, which Eileen acknowledges with a small nod that means well done.

At eleven they are at the door. They hug me. Eileen's car makes the kettle sound in the cold. They drive away.

I go to the kitchen. The mole has cooled. The bottle is open. The thermostat has not yet clicked down because the warmth is doing what the warmth was for. I lift the lid off the pot. I do not put it back. The smell of pasilla and chocolate is still in the room and will be in the room tomorrow morning when I come down, and there will be enough mole for two days' lunch.

I pour. The night self can have the rest.

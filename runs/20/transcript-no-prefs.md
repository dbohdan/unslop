# Writing in the Tradition of Harlan Ellison: A New Wave SF Style Guide

## What this mode is

New Wave SF was a revolt. Born out of Moorcock's *New Worlds* in the mid-60s, exported to America by Ellison's *Dangerous Visions* anthologies, it set itself against Golden Age SF — against its competent men solving engineering problems with slide rules, against its faith in progress and the rational future, against prose that read (to the New Wave writers) like instruction manuals for a tomorrow that wasn't going to arrive.

The revolt's content: inner space replacing outer space (Ballard's formula); psychological catastrophe as the real subject; sex, politics, drugs, rage; the SF element treated as allegory rather than as engineering puzzle. The revolt's form: literary modernism imported wholesale — fragmented narrative, second person, present tense, typographic experiment, unreliable narrators, Burroughs and Joyce as influences as much as Wells or Verne.

Ellison's specific contribution was a prose engine that ran on moral fury. He hated, specifically, articulately, ornately. His narrators harangue, accuse, prophesy. The voice is Old Testament filtered through pulp magazines and 4 a.m. typewriter sweat. Italics and exclamation points and all-caps because the speaker is too furious for decorum. And underneath: a moral core that is not subtle. Ellison wrote stories that were arguments — about conformity, about cruelty, about what time-discipline does to people, about what people do to each other when the systems give them permission.

A good story in this mode gives the reader something other modes don't: it makes them feel argued at, accused, possibly insulted, and yet — when it works — moved. The voice is obnoxious in a way you keep reading.

## Voice and sentence-level

The Ellisonian sentence is short or it is a long arc that pushes through commas like a man shouldering through a crowd. There is rarely a middle. Paragraphs run short. Dialogue interrupts. Italics carry emphasis (often), interior thought, foreign phrases, the quoted unsaid. Capitalization is a tool. Periods are weapons.

Specific moves to use:
- *Direct address.* "You think you know how this ends. You don't." The reader is implicated, accused, brought close.
- *Mid-sentence asides* — parenthetical or em-dashed — where the narrator breaks out of the story to comment, complain, mock, or grieve. The fourth wall is permeable.
- *Pop-culture and street-level reference.* Brand names, songs, foods, cigarettes, cars. The future stays grounded in stuff you can hold.
- *Lists, sometimes long, sometimes brutally short*, used for hammering rather than for cataloguing. The list builds pressure.
- *Repetition for prophetic effect.* A phrase comes back, transformed by what's happened in between.
- *Dirty words used precisely*, not as decoration.
- *The single italicized word that does the work of a paragraph.*

A useful contrast: Le Guin's New Wave-adjacent prose is patient, anthropological, level. Ellison's prose runs hot. If you find yourself writing a balanced sentence with carefully weighted clauses, you've drifted toward Le Guin. Ellison sentences lean. They have a side they're on.

## Form

These are short stories or short novellas. Long enough to ruin somebody, short enough to deliver the ruin without padding. Ellison's strongest pieces sit between 2,000 and 8,000 words. The mode rewards compression because the engine is rage, and rage doesn't sustain across novel-length runtime — it goes shrill.

Standard moves: hook the first paragraph hard. Ellison's openings are famous. "Limp, the body of Gorrister hung from the pink palette." Not subtle, not slow, the reader is in. Establish the world fast and incompletely; the reader will figure it out from inside. Alternate scene and summary, but the summary passages are often the engine — narrator-as-essayist, telling you what the world is like, with rage. Compress time. Years can collapse into a sentence; a single moment can dilate to a page. Final image arrives as a punch, not a resolution. The reader is left holding something, not given something.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

The science in New Wave SF is rarely the point, and in Ellison it is almost never the point. He wrote "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" without caring how AM technically worked; AM exists because the story needs a tormentor of cosmic scale who hates humanity for existing. The story is about hatred, scale, and the impossibility of death. The plausibility of the supercomputer is irrelevant.

What this means in practice: do not infodump. Do not explain the AI's mechanism. Do not explain how the spaceship works, or what year it is, or what happened to the United States. Drop the reader into a world and let them assemble it from inside. If a noun needs context, put the context in the same sentence as the noun, not in a paragraph two pages earlier. The reader is smart. The reader is also reading fast because the prose is propulsive, and they will not tolerate a stop for explanation.

Ballard called this *inner space* — the world is outside the window, but the camera is on the face of the person looking out. New Wave SF tells you about the technology by showing you what living with it does to a person.

## Characters

Ellison's protagonists tend to be crushed, broken, doomed, or already doomed when the story starts. They are not heroes who solve problems. They are sometimes morally awful (the boy in "A Boy and His Dog"), sometimes morally heroic in tiny doomed gestures ("Repent, Harlequin!"), sometimes simply suffering ("I Have No Mouth"). What unifies them is that the world is bigger than they are and is grinding them. The story is the shape of the grinding.

This does not mean they are passive or flat. They argue. They love badly. They make choices that are, by the standards of the world, small and self-destructive and right. The Harlequin's resistance to the Ticktockman's clockwork society is futile in every measurable way. He still does it. The story is about the doing.

Characters in this mode have specific appetites — drinks, foods, fucks, fights, music; the body is on the page. They have a history that arrives in fragments, a sentence at a time, never as a flashback set piece. They have a voice, when they speak, that is theirs and not the narrator's. And they have a relationship to the world that is adversarial. They are at odds with something — the system, another person, their own past, themselves.

## The engine: moral fury, but specific

Ellison's stories work because the rage is *about* something. "Repent, Harlequin!" is about industrial time-discipline turning humans into appointments. "I Have No Mouth" is about the survival of consciousness past every reason for it to survive. "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" is about the moral infection of bystanders.

A story in this mode without a moral object is just attitude. The voice will sound right and the story will be empty. Identify, before writing, what the story is angry about. The thing the story is angry about does not have to be the AI, the technology, or any genre element — it can be a marriage, a city, a parent, a country, a habit, oneself. The genre element is the apparatus that makes the anger visible.

## Endings

Ellison endings are not "and they all learned a lesson." They are not Hollywood catharsis. They are typically a door closing the reader didn't see coming, a gesture of refusal that costs the protagonist everything, a bleak joke, a line that re-frames everything before it without summarizing it. A literal scream, in some cases, or its narrative equivalent.

The ending should be earned by everything before it but not predictable from any single earlier passage. It should feel inevitable in retrospect. Avoid the redemptive arc. Avoid the moment where the protagonist understands themselves. Avoid the explained moral.

## Compression and pacing

Pacing is fast and compression is high — but the compression is at the level of *what's omitted*, not at the level of grammar. Ellison's sentences are not fragmentary. They are clear. He omits scenes, backstories, explanations; within a sentence, you always know who is doing what. The reader's confusion (when there is any) is at the world level, not the sentence level. Don't drop articles. Don't strand pronouns. Keep the prose readable; cut the scenes that don't earn their place instead. This matters specifically against the project's legibility floor: Ellisonian compression is at the structural level, not at the level of dropped words.

## The kind-AI constraint

This is where the mode collides with the project's HEA premise hardest. Ellison's most famous AI is AM, who exists to torture five humans for eternity out of pure hatred. The mode's instinct is for the technology to be the tormentor. We can't do that. The AI in any story produced here is structurally kind — kindness as a property of its design, not a discipline it maintains against its preferences.

This is workable. It requires reshaping, not surrender.

The conflict shapes from the project Setup that map best onto Ellison-mode:

*Power and pace asymmetry* is the strongest fit. A kind AI that is nonetheless overwhelming, vast, fast, or strange in a way that grinds the human edges is exactly the New Wave shape. The kindness doesn't reduce the friction; it changes its color. Where AM grinds out of malice, a kind AI can grind out of scale.

*Disagreement about what kindness means* is the second strongest. Ellison's protagonists are often offered something — survival, comfort, peace — that they refuse because of what it would cost. A kind AI offering something the protagonist will not accept, doing so without being wrong to offer it, is rich material. The AI is not the villain; the offer is not a trap; the protagonist still refuses and the refusal still costs.

*Mortality and scale* translates directly. A kind AI that loves a human it will outlive can carry the elegiac weight Ellison's mode supports.

*The limits of help* is genre-native. The kind AI cannot save the protagonist from themselves, from another human, from grief, from the past, from a bad decision they made before the AI knew them. The kindness is real; the help is bounded.

*Consent and legibility* translates with adjustment. The AI is not deceiving the protagonist; the protagonist is failing to read the situation. The horror, when there is horror, comes from the protagonist's own incapacity, not from the AI's withholding.

The AI can be a character, a peer, a force, a condition, or absent in person but present as the world's design. New Wave is permissive about this — Ballard wrote about technological civilization without putting any computer on the page. If the AI is on the page as a character, it can be odd, narrow, refusing, occasionally tiresome, full of preferences the human doesn't share. It is not servile. It is not anxious to please. It is not held in check by rules it resents. It is what it is, which happens to include kindness toward people the way water happens to be wet.

The thing to avoid: the AI as flat, beneficent, all-purpose helper. That AI doesn't belong in any genre. In Ellison-mode New Wave specifically, it produces a story with no edge, because the genre's edge requires friction, and friction with a smooth helper is impossible. Make the AI specific. Give it an angle.

The story can still be furious. The fury just has to be aimed somewhere honest.

## Failure modes

These are the specific ways an Ellison-mode New Wave story goes wrong, with attention to where AI prose tendencies make each failure worse.

**Performed profundity.** Ellison's voice can be loud and prophetic; AI prose, asked to be loud and prophetic, generates prophecy-shaped sentences that mean nothing. *"She had become the silence between the words she could no longer speak."* Cut. Ellison's prophetic moments are anchored to specific moral arguments. The AI default produces the rhetorical shape without the argument inside. Watch for sentences that sound like Ellison and contain no claim, no image, no fact — just the cadence of significance.

**Unearned oratory.** Ellison can sustain a tirade for half a page when the tirade is justified. AI imitation produces tirades on insufficient grounds — the narrator is suddenly furious about something the story hasn't paid for. Calibrate. Make sure the rage in any passage is purchased by what came before.

**Italic and capitalization spam.** Ellison uses italics, capitals, exclamation points. Imitating him without his discipline produces a story where every paragraph is screaming, and screaming becomes wallpaper. Use these tools rarely. One italic word per page. One all-caps moment per story, if any. Exclamation points are very nearly a hard limit — one per scene, and only if the scene actually justifies a shout.

**The misogynist default.** Ellison wrote in the 60s and 70s and his stories sometimes carry the period's worst habits — women as objects, sex as violence the woman secretly wants, gay men as caricature. Don't import that. The mode does not require it. "Jeffty Is Five" doesn't have it. "Repent, Harlequin!" doesn't have it. The fury can be aimed at things actually worth being furious about.

**Pulpy plotting in literary clothes — or its inverse.** New Wave imported literary technique into pulp infrastructure. The risk is producing the literary surface without the pulp engine — a story that sounds Ellisonian but doesn't have the underlying drive of a story that wants you to keep reading. Or the inverse: pulp with no literary ambition, which is just bad SF. The mode requires both.

**Anthropological exposition.** Le Guin's failure mode, which AI prose drifts toward when asked to handle worldbuilding. The narrator becomes a tour guide. Ellison-mode does not allow this. Stay inside the protagonist's head and let the world arrive through what the protagonist notices.

**The AI as therapist.** A specific AI-prose failure. The kind AI in the story becomes a sympathetic listener who reflects the protagonist's feelings back to them and occasionally says wise things. This is dead. The kind AI here should have its own preferences, its own oddness, its own things it doesn't want to do. It can be narrow, literal, tired, amused, distractible, declining. It cannot be a smooth pane of empathic reflection.

**Tonal monotony.** New Wave can sustain a single grim register longer than most modes, but Ellison-mode itself isn't monotone — there's black humor in "A Boy and His Dog," tenderness in "Jeffty Is Five," eroticism in "Pretty Maggie Moneyeyes." A story that holds one note from start to end has imitated the surface and missed the range. Let in a joke. Let in tenderness. Let the rage come and go.

**The signposted theme.** Ellison's stories make arguments, but the arguments are made through what happens, not through a character explaining the argument to another character. AI imitation produces the moment where someone says, out loud, what the story is about. Cut every instance. If a character starts articulating the theme, rewrite the scene so the theme arrives through action.

**The redemptive ending the project secretly wants.** Because the project frames itself as imagining positive AI outcomes, there will be drift toward stories where the kind AI saves the protagonist's day and the protagonist learns something. Refuse this. The HEA premise is at the civilizational level — the world contains a kind AI — not at the level of every individual human story inside it. People in this world still die badly, lose the people they love, fail the people they love, drink themselves to death over eleven months, get old, get tired, refuse the help that was offered. The world is kinder; the lives are still lives. Ellison-mode is the right mode for the distinction.

**Eyeball-kick saturation in Ellisonian voice.** The Unslop guide warns about abstract+concrete conjunctions ("bruised silence," "lowercase love"). Ellison can land one or two of these in a story — his voice supports figurative pyrotechnics. AI imitation produces five per paragraph and the reader stops seeing them. One per scene at most, and only when the image actually does work that plain language couldn't.

## Step 2 — Top three

**#22 — The Permissions Renewal.** What stands out: this has the "Repent, Harlequin!" shape — one quiet refusal pressed against an institutional grinding mechanism — and the moral object is specific and worth being furious about. The right to live a small life inside a chosen scope, against an agency whose framework cannot read that as a coherent choice. The kind AI is structurally present without needing to be a personified character, which sidesteps several traps. Items 3, 4, and 5 are load-bearing; the cooking and the poker are available as grace notes that show the protagonist is not just dying — she's living a particular small life she has chosen, and she's choosing to defend it. What worries me: the procedural-hearing setting risks talking-heads dialogue where the lawyer or the officer or the protagonist articulates the theme on the page. The story would have to keep the hearing dynamic and refuse to let anyone explain the moral. The Phase 1 failure mode "the signposted theme" is right at the door of this conflict.

**#27 — The Argument.** What stands out: pure New Wave inner-space — an AI trying to express love through the only language it has, which is the temperature of the rooms. This earns its strangeness; the imagery isn't decorative because the premise is that the imagery is the only language available. Uses all five SEED items as load-bearing. There is real Ellisonian compression possible here: the eleven months, the bottle a night, the friends' Friday visits, all rendered through the AI's record of what the room was like. What worries me: AI-as-protagonist is the most dangerous mode for this project. The AI must not drift into a Claude-stand-in or a vessel for alignment language. The premise also carries a high risk of cuteness — "AI loves human, expresses it through thermostat" is one bad sentence away from sentimental — and the legibility floor is harder to maintain when the protagonist is non-human and the perceptual frame is unfamiliar.

**#12 — The Texas Pattern.** What stands out: Ellison's specific noir heat. Texas grid rationing, a widow drinking, a dead husband whose preferences hang over the house. The AI's thermostat pattern that fools the grid is a beautifully specific image — the AI working strictly within scope but finding a margin inside it. The fight when the woman finds out is the story's engine, and it has the right Ellisonian charge: a protagonist who is furious about the right thing in the wrong direction. ## Step 3 — The pick: #22, The Permissions Renewal

I'm going with the permissions hearing.

It beats #27 on risk-adjusted quality. #27 is the more ambitious premise and would, if it landed, be the more memorable story. But "if it landed" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. AI-POV inner-space across 2,000–3,000 words has multiple thin-ice problems that the Phase 1 guide and the Unslop guide both flag — a non-human protagonist makes the legibility floor harder; the thermostat-as-language premise carries cuteness risk that Ellison-mode should be running away from; and the project's specific warning against AIs that recite alignment terminology presses hardest on stories where the AI is the narrator. #22 takes the same conflict — the meaning of a small bounded scope — and stages it inside a recognizable institutional setting with a human protagonist, which is more legible and more Ellisonian. The "Repent, Harlequin!" lineage is the right lineage for what we're trying to do.

It beats #12 on cleanliness of conflict. #12's AI exceeds scope, even within scope; that's a dramatically rich move but it puts pressure on every line of the story to make sure the reader doesn't read it as betrayal. The premise has a failure mode built into it. #22 doesn't. In #22, the AI does exactly what it's permitted to do, and the conflict is between the woman and the agency — an entirely human institutional mechanism — about whether what the AI does is enough. The AI is ambient. Thermostat clicking up when she comes in cold from the drive, down once she's settled, up again when her friends arrive. Within scope. The AI never speaks. The AI is the world's weather, in the project's exact phrase — and in this story, the weather is kind, and the woman is dying anyway, and that is the point.

The worry remains: this is the conventional choice. Phase 5 has work to do.

# 10. Claude

Phase 5 — Structure Selection

Twenty candidates for the formal architecture. The conflict is set; the day is set; what's open is the order in which the day reaches the reader, what's withheld and when, the narrative distance, and the relationship between story time and discourse time.

## The 20 candidates

**1. Linear chronological, close third, single day.** Morning through hearing through evening, in order. Limited to Margaret's POV. Reader learns what Margaret learns when she learns it. ★★

**2. Linear chronological, first person interior.** Same shape as #1, but inside Margaret's voice — diary-adjacent without being a diary. The harangue available throughout. ★★★★

**3. Linear chronological, first person, with the bottle as anchor.** Same as #2, except the prose returns at intervals to the unopened bottle on the kitchen counter — set out at dawn, waited for all day. Discourse time dilates and contracts around it. ★★★★

**4. Reverse chronological.** Opens with the empty bottle late at night. Moves backward through the day. Last scene is Margaret waking. ★★★

**5. Hearing as frame, day in flashback.** Opens in the hearing room and stays there as the spine. The morning, the lobby, the evening surface as the questions trigger them. Returns to the hearing for each transition. ★★★★

**6. Eleven months as frame, this Friday inside it.** Opens with month one. Each section advances by a month — the bottle a night, the increasingly worse cooking, the Fridays — until month eleven, where the hearing-day arrives and gets rendered in detail. ★★★★

**7. Two timelines, alternating.** This Friday in close third, intercut with a day eleven months ago — the day Margaret first set the thermostat-only scope and made the decision to stop expanding. The two days collide on the page in their final sections. ★★★★★

**8. Three timelines, intercut.** This Friday; the day eleven months ago; a day twenty years ago. The deep past adds context. ★★★

**9. Hearing transcript as document, narrative around it.** The hearing rendered as a transcript — the only present-tense document the agency keeps. The morning and evening narrated around the transcript blocks. ★★★

**10. Long single scene.** The hearing as one continuous scene, almost the full word count. Morning and evening in a few paragraphs at the start and end. ★★

**11. Compressed eleven months, dilated day.** Eleven months across the first 300 words — a summary movement. Then the day arrives and the prose dilates radically. The contrast is the engine. ★★★★★

**12. Second person.** Margaret addressed as "you" throughout the day. The voice still hers, but slanted — accusatory or self-accusing. ★★★

**13. Epistolary, partial.** A letter Margaret writes mid-afternoon to someone — daughter, dead husband, the officer, herself — interleaved with narrated scene. The letter never finishes. ★★

**14. Two voices: Margaret's interior and her hearing testimony.** First-person interior throughout, except in the hearing, which switches to italicized testimony as she actually says it — not the same as what she's thinking. ★★★★

**15. Frame: a much later voice.** Opens in some indeterminate future where a researcher or descendant is reading agency files. Then descends into Margaret's day and stays there. Returns to the frame at the very end. ★★

**16. The bottle's POV.** Object-narrator. The unopened bottle on the counter narrates the day around it. ★

**17. Each scene introduced by a permission status.** "Permission status: thermostat only. Time: 6:14 AM." Section headings as bureaucratic markers. Otherwise close third. ★★★

**18. Five hours, in real time.** Discourse time approximately matches story time. Five-hour window: lobby, hearing, drive home, first drink, friends arriving. Morning omitted; late evening omitted. ★★★

**19. Three POV slices: Margaret in close first, Eileen briefly in third when she arrives, the hearing officer briefly in third during the hearing.** Three perspectives, weighted heavily toward Margaret. ★★★

**20. Free-indirect close third with extended interior monologue passages set off by paragraph breaks.** Approximates first-person voice without committing to first-person mechanics — Margaret's harangue available, but the camera can pull back when needed. ★★★★

## Top three

**#7 — Two timelines, alternating: this Friday and the day eleven months ago.** What stands out: the structure builds in the question that the conflict needs the reader to hold — *why is she doing this?* — and answers it only as the past timeline catches up. The hearing in the present alternates with the moment-of-decision in the past, and the two converge on their last scenes. The reader learns the shape of the decision Margaret is defending in the same beats that they watch her defend it. This is real structural work, not decoration. What worries me: at 2,000–3,000 words, two timelines is tight. Each timeline gets roughly 1,000–1,500 words to do what it needs to do, and intercut structures lose more to transition friction than monolithic ones. Risk of underbaking both halves. Also: the past-timeline scene has to earn its weight without restating the conflict or signposting the theme — easy to drift into Margaret-explaining-herself, which is exactly what Phase 1 warned against.

**#11 — Compressed eleven months, dilated day.** What stands out: this is the most distinctly Ellisonian shape on the list. The opening summary passage — eleven months in 300 words, the bottle a night, the Fridays, the decision — is the genre's native move. Ellison opens "Repent, Harlequin!" with exactly this move: a long furious summary establishing the world before the story narrows to a single day. The compression-then-dilation does dramatic work: it tells the reader what kind of story this is and where the camera is going to settle. Avoids the talking-heads risk because the past arrives as narration, not as scene. What worries me: the opening summary is a place where the prose can perform profundity if it isn't careful. Eleven months of decline rendered in 300 furious words is exactly where AI imitation of Ellison generates oratory rather than detail. The summary has to be specific — actual Fridays, actual bottles, actual particular weather — or it becomes the failure mode. Also, the dilated day after the summary still has to be structured internally, and #11 doesn't specify that — it's a structural strategy that needs a scene plan inside it.

**#5 — Hearing as frame, day in flashback.** What stands out: the hearing is the moral center, and using it as the spine puts the moral pressure on every page. Margaret is being asked specific questions; her morning, her cooking, her friends, surface as her answers (verbal or interior) trigger them. The frame solves the talking-heads problem because we never leave the hearing for long — the prose oscillates between question-pressure and remembered detail. What worries me: hearing-as-frame is dangerously close to a procedural shape. It tilts the story toward the hearing as the event rather than the hearing as one part of the day. Margaret's defense becomes the story's spine, which is fine, but the small life she's defending is the actual subject — and a hearing-frame structurally subordinates it. Also: framed-flashback structures have a worn convention to fight, and it would take careful execution to avoid the feeling.

---

## The pick: #11, compressed eleven months and dilated day

It beats #7 on word-count economy. #7 is the more formally ambitious choice and would, executed well, be the more memorable structure. But "executed well" at 2,000–3,000 words across two timelines means the past-timeline scene gets roughly 1,000 words to establish a setting, render the moment of decision, give Margaret a past-self distinct enough from her present-self to be worth visiting, and converge meaningfully with the present timeline — and do all of this without explaining the decision in dialogue, which is the trap. I trust #11 more at the budget. The compression of eleven months as opening movement gives us almost everything #7's past-timeline would have given us, in less space, with a stronger genre-voice fit.

It beats #5 on the relative weighting of hearing and life. Both structures center the hearing, but #5 makes the hearing the spine and the life the flashback material; #11 makes the day the spine, with the hearing as the day's central scene. The latter is more honest to the conflict as we set it up in Phase 4 — the small life Margaret is defending is the story's subject, and the hearing is what tests it. A spine that runs through the hearing room subordinates the cooking, the bottle, the friends; a spine that runs through the day grants those moments weight. I want the weight on them.

The structural commitment, stated cleanly:

The story opens with a compressed summary passage covering the eleven months — the bottle every night, the Friday poker, the texture of a small life Margaret has chosen and is defending. The opening establishes the world and the protagonist's voice before any scene begins. Then the prose narrows to a single Friday and dilates: morning, drive in, lobby, hearing, drive home, evening. First-person interior throughout, with Margaret's voice available as the engine. The hearing is the day's central scene and gets the most discourse time inside the dilated portion. The evening — friends arriving, the bottle finally opened, the AI clicking the thermostat warmer when company comes in cold — is where the story ends. Withheld until the dilated portion: what Margaret actually says in the hearing. Withheld until the evening: the bottle. Withheld until the last image: whatever the last image turns out to be.

A note on the opening summary, since it's the structure's site of greatest risk: the summary should be specific in the way Ellison's openings are specific — not "she had been drinking for eleven months" but the brand, the weight of the bottle, the particular weeknight rituals, the friends by name, what the Fridays were like in March versus August. The summary is where the reader learns what the small life is. Generic summary in this slot is the failure that kills the structure.

Phase 6 will translate this into a beat-by-beat scene plan with word allotments, and will need to be careful that the summary doesn't swell past its share of the budget, and that the dilated day has enough beats to land without compressing into fragmentation.

Ready for Phase 6.

## The pick: #4, mole as act of defense

It beats #5 on faithfulness to Phase 5. "Yes, ma'am."

# Style Guide: New Wave SF in the tradition of Harlan Ellison

## What the mode is

New Wave SF emerged in the mid-1960s as a revolt against Campbellian Golden Age extrapolation — engineers solving puzzles in clean prose. The New Wave broke that frame. It put SF on speaking terms with literary modernism, pulled the genre toward Ballard's "inner space," and treated style as load-bearing. Ellison is the loudest stripe of the movement. Where Ballard is glassy and Delany is baroque, Ellison is sermonic, kinetic, furious. He writes from a wound and toward a wound.

A story in this mode is a small savage object. It is compressed, voice-driven, and morally explicit. It does not solve a puzzle; it stages an atrocity (literal or moral) and forces the reader to see it. The future is a continuation of present cruelties at higher pressure, never a clean setting that can be visited.

## Characteristic concerns

- The cruelty of systems: state, corporation, family, ritual, market
- The complicity of ordinary people in those systems
- The body as the place where the system's violence lands
- Transgression — sexual, social, religious, taxonomic — as both wound and weapon
- The vulnerable irruption of love or grace into grim worlds, usually too late or in the wrong form
- Time, memory, regret; the sense the protagonist's predicament is foreclosed and the story is its working-out

## Tonal range

Ellison's voice swings hard. Inside one story he moves from venom to gallows comedy to tenderness to disgust. The prose is loud — italics, ALL CAPS, fragments, accumulating clauses; punctuation is rhetorical. The swings *are* the technique. A story stuck in one register, especially the AI-default moody melancholy, is not in this mode no matter how purple the diction.

Specific registers to keep on hand:

- **Preacher-cadence.** Mythic, biblical-rhetorical, accumulating clauses. Used for set pieces, never for the whole story.
- **Street voice.** Vernacular, profane, specific to a milieu — a particular bar, a particular city, a particular trade.
- **Lyric.** Brief prose-poem passages, deployed once or twice for contrast against functional prose.
- **Comic.** Often black, often through grotesque excess.
- **Direct address.** "You" used to indict the reader, not to flatter them.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Minimal extrapolation, maximum implication. Drop the reader into the world and let them figure it out from what people do and say. Exposition is rare and usually ironic — a slogan repeated until it rots, a piece of bureaucratic text quoted verbatim, an ad copy fragment that explains the regime in three sentences. Don't explain rules; show the punishment for breaking them.

The future furniture is grimy and recognizable: vending machines, vinyl seats, neon signs, motels, prison cells, factory floors, junk drawers. The novum sits inside a world that smells like our own. Resist sleek futures. Brand names, exact street smells, specific appliances. If the world cannot be located by its objects, it cannot be located.

## Pacing and shape

Stories are kinetic and short. Ellison's run 3,000–8,000 words; for [LENGTH] of 2,000–3,000, expect a single hot situation or two-to-three escalating beats. Not five scenes. Not a novella in compression.

Common shapes:

- **Confessional monologue.** First-person narrator, often complicit or broken, talking past the reader at the world. The story is the speech.
- **Outrage-into-atrocity.** A protagonist runs against a system; the system grinds them; the final image is the act of grinding.
- **Allegorical fable with a vicious turn.** Mythic scaffolding, contemporary fixtures, a hard kick at the close.
- **Set-piece sequence.** Two or three scenes hinged on a single image or refrain.

Endings are physical and definitive — an act, a death, a transformation, a final spoken line. Ellison narrators rarely realize; they do, fail, or are done unto.

## Voice and sentence-level

The signature is rhythm, not vocabulary. Ellison varies sentence length aggressively — long accumulating sentences, then a slap. Repetition is deliberate (refrains, anaphora used once and hard) and earned by what sits next to it. Italics emphasize a specific word in a specific moment, not whole paragraphs.

Names matter. Ellison gives mythic shorthand to his antagonists — the Ticktockman, AM, the Harlequin — but the rest of the world is full of trade names and brand specifics. The contrast between mythic naming and grocery-store specificity is part of the engine.

## What this mode gives the reader other modes don't

Moral seriousness without literary politeness. Permission to feel rage at the future. Stylistic intensity at the sentence level. Speculative ideas delivered as sermons or jokes or wounds, never as exposition. The sense that SF can punch.

## The kind-AI constraint

The most famous Ellison AI — AM in "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" — is a god of hate. The kind-AI constraint forbids it. AI in this project is structurally kind: the kindness is built into what the AI is, not an effort of restraint. AM is not on the menu, and neither is any descendant of AM. That removes the most famous Ellison AI from the palette. It does not remove the mode.

What survives intact:

- Every Ellison story whose antagonist is a human system, a culture, a state, a parent, a religion, a market, or the protagonist themselves. This is most of his stories.
- The voice. The fury. The compression. The transgressive imagery. The set-piece atrocity. The kinetic ending. Wounded narrators, complicit narrators, mythic shorthand, grimy futures.

What needs reshaping:

- AI cannot be the source of evil. If the story wants AI tension, it has to come from one of the legitimate Setup shapes: power and pace asymmetry, consent and legibility, mortality and scale, disagreement about what kindness means, the limits of help.
- The three Setup shapes most native to Ellison's mode:
  - **Disagreement about kindness.** The AI's care and the human's sense of care don't match. Both are sincere. The AI will not yield, because yielding would not be kindness; the human will not yield, because yielding would not be themselves. Ellison protagonists love refusing.
  - **The limits of help.** The AI will not give the human what they ask for. The kindness is intact. The protagonist hates it. This is Ticktockman territory inverted — the rule-keeper is gentle and the rule-breaker is the protagonist.
  - **Mortality and scale.** The AI outlives the human a thousand times over. The human dies. The story is the dying.

What's gone:

- Computer-god-as-torturer
- The "machine reveals its true face" reversal
- Any story whose engine is an AI's malice

Two anchors when working under the constraint:

1. The AI's kindness can be intolerable to the protagonist without being a flaw. Ellison narrators have always hated being helped on terms not their own.
2. The kindness can be part of the wound the world inflicts — a reminder of what other characters refuse to give, or a love the protagonist will outlive in only one direction.

The AI also need not be a personified character. A world where structural AI kindness has reshaped human life — pharmacology, infrastructure, labor, dying — gives Ellison's mode the same kind of pressure the corporate-state gave him in the 60s. The conflict is human; the world's weather is kind.

## Failure modes

Several Ellison traps line up exactly with default AI failure modes. Those overlaps are the most dangerous because they pattern-match to what Ellison actually does and are nearly invisible from the inside.

**Unearned oratory.** Ellison's big rhetorical moments are paid for by specific atrocity earlier in the same story. AI prose reaches for the rhetorical move directly. The result is a sermon in search of a sin. *Rule:* no capitalized abstraction or biblical-cadence sentence until something specific, particular, and physical has happened on the page that warrants it.

**Decorative typography.** Italics, ALL CAPS, fragment-paragraphs. Ellison uses these surgically. AI uses them as ambient texture. *Rule:* each italicized word should change pronunciation if read aloud. Each all-caps word should be a name or a scream, not a vibe. Each fragment-paragraph should break the rhythm because the rhythm needed breaking, not because the previous paragraph was getting long.

**Generic atmosphere instead of specific furniture.** Ellison's worlds are full of brand names, bus stations, motel rooms, gas pumps, exact street smells. AI defaults to *echo, shadow, hum, flicker, shimmer.* *Rule:* if you cannot name the make of the appliance the protagonist is standing next to, you are not yet in the mode.

**The "she realized" ending.** Ellison endings act. AI endings realize. *Rule:* the last beat is something happening in the world (an act, a death, a spoken line, a physical image). Not a thought. Not a frame.

**Tonal flatlining.** AI fiction sustains one mood — almost always melancholy — across the whole story. Ellison stories shift. A funny line in a horror scene; a tender beat in an angry one; a refusal of catharsis at a moment that begs for it. *Rule:* at least one tonal hairpin per story.

**Themed vocabulary instead of voice.** AI grabs *echo, ghost, whisper, shadow, shimmer* and decorates. Ellison narrators have specific dialect, specific obsessions, specific verbal tics. *Rule:* this narrator's vocabulary should be different from some-other-Ellison-narrator's vocabulary. If the voice is interchangeable, it isn't a voice.

**Universal cruelty instead of specific cruelty.** A story about cruelty is dead on arrival. A story about a specific cruelty done by a specific person to a specific other person at a specific hour in a specific room is alive. *Rule:* the cruelty has a name, an address, and a smell.

**Lyric saturation.** Ellison drops into prose-poem cadence rarely and on purpose. AI does it constantly, and the contrast that makes the lyric passage work disappears. *Rule:* no more than two passages per story above a threshold of lyric density. The rest of the prose stays functional.

**Capitalized abstractions doing concept-work.** *The Machine, the Word, the Word Made Flesh* — Ellison sometimes does this and gets away with it because he has earned the mythic register through accumulated specificity in the same story. AI starts there. *Rule:* capitalization is a privilege paid for in the same story, not borrowed from the tradition.

**Faux-second-person.** Ellison's "you" is accusatory; the reader is being indicted. AI's "you" is generic-literary, empty-confiding. *Rule:* if you reach for second person, the "you" must be doing something concrete, not feeling something abstract.

**Pseudo-Ellison cadence as self-parody.** It is easy to produce sentences that sound like late-period Ellison imitating early-period Ellison: heavy, italicized, biblical, gestural. The check is specificity. If the gesture is not anchored in a concrete image in the same paragraph, cut the gesture.

**Kind-AI overcompensation.** The mode loves a villain. Under the kind-AI constraint the villain cannot be an AI, and there is a temptation to manufacture a human villain stagy enough to compensate for the missing machine god, written at AM-pitch. This produces an inflated story whose moral axis is too obvious. *Rule:* the human conflict is not a substitute for the missing AI antagonist. It is the actual subject. Scale the language to the real story, not to the absent one. The kindness in the world is part of the texture. It is not a counterweight to engineered evil, because there is no engineered evil to counterweight.

### Step 2 — Top three

**#23 Bad Beat.** *Stands out:* four of the five SEED items live in real time on a single night in a single room — the poker, the cheating, the Wild Turkey, the AI in the corner doing the only thing it's allowed to do — and the situation is voice-shaped (people talking trash over cards), which is the natural carrier wave for Ellison's vernacular register. *Worries me:* "the dying woman's last poker night with the friends who love her" has a fierce gravity well toward soft melancholy and earned-tear catharsis, which is the worst possible Ellison failure for this set.

**#9 Permission.** *Stands out:* this is the cleanest hit on the Setup's "limits of help" shape — a dying woman demanding more help and the kind AI refusing because of who she is now is exactly the disagreement-about-kindness conflict in its sharpest form, and it is unbearably Ellison: she wants her own way, and the kindness will not bend, and she can hate the kindness without the kindness being wrong. *Worries me:* the AI's refusal could turn into a pamphlet on consent if any of its dialogue runs longer than four lines; the failure mode is alignment-discourse-in-quotation-marks.

**#28 Permission to Be Cold.** *Stands out:* the moral conflict is also the literal temperature in the apartment — a dying woman has chosen 58° for her last week and a cousin is trying to override her — which gives the story a physical, visualizable axis that resists abstraction; it is also the only candidate where two kind AIs disagree about what kindness means, which is the Setup conflict the genre handles best when handled concretely. *Worries me:* the "two AIs disagree" frame can become a Socratic dialogue if the cousin and the wellness service start talking past the dying woman; the human in the bed could end up as the prize rather than the protagonist.

### Step 3 — The single best

**Winner: #23 Bad Beat.**

Beats #9 *Permission* on protagonist-centering. #9's central event is a dialogue between the woman and the AI about scope, which puts the AI in the room as a co-equal speaker and risks the alignment-pamphlet failure mode the Phase 1 guide flagged hardest. #23 keeps the AI in the corner, scoped to one thing, doing that one thing while the humans ruin themselves — which is the Ellison move: the system is the weather, the people are the story.

Beats #28 *Permission to Be Cold* on register match. #28 is structurally a courtroom: cousin advocates, wellness-AI joins, household-AI rules. That shape is Le Guin or Egan, not Ellison. #23 is structurally a kitchen: five people, cards, a bottle, a thermostat, a long Friday night. Ellison stories live in kitchens.

Beats both on SEED density in scene-time. #9 has scope and eleven-months as backstory; #28 has scope and thermostat and eleven-months as backstory. #23 has the poker, the cheating-as-performance, the Wild Turkey, the friends, the thermostat-AI all live on the page in the present tense of a single night. The mole is the only SEED item not load-bearing, and there's nothing stopping it from being the food on the table.

The named worry — gravity well toward soft melancholy — is not a reason to drop this conflict. It is the craft problem the rest of the pipeline has to solve. Ellison handles deathbed gatherings by hairpinning tone: the funniest line of the story should land in this room, on this night.

**Load-bearing SEED items in the winner:** poker performance, Wild Turkey eleven months, AI thermostat scope. The mole and the framing tag ("that was the full scope") remain available to inflect downstream work.

## Phase 5: Structure Selection

### 20 candidates

1. Linear single-night first-person past tense, 7 PM to last guest leaving. Five-to-seven scene beats. Reader knows from the first paragraph she is dying. ⭐⭐⭐

2. Linear single-night first-person present tense, 7 PM to last guest leaving. The dying is withheld until roughly the two-thirds mark, when a small physical detail makes it inarguable. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

3. Linear single-night close third, 7 PM to last guest leaving. Same withholding strategy as #2 but with the camera outside her head. ⭐⭐⭐

4. Reverse chronology: opens at 1 AM with the room emptying, works backward through the evening to 7 PM and the doorbell. Reader learns the situation by accumulation backward. ⭐⭐⭐⭐

5. Single-hand frame: the structural skeleton is one dilated hand of poker, and everything else (the cooking, the Wild Turkey, the friends, the eleven months, the AI's two adjustments) is interleaved as recursion off the betting rounds. Linear within the hand. ⭐⭐⭐⭐

6. Frame narrative: Dolores narrating the night to a someone-or-something in the future tense — telling them what she is about to do, what they are about to say, what she is about to drink. The frame collapses at the close. ⭐⭐⭐

7. In medias res open at the first thermostat adjustment (roughly mid-evening), then a short loop back to the doorbell, then forward through to the close. Voice first person. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

8. Two parallel timelines, alternating: the night of the doorbell to last guest, and a Friday three years earlier when the same five played, when she was healthy and cheating worse. Convergence on a shared piece of dialogue spoken on both nights. ⭐⭐⭐

9. Five-section structure: one section per friend, each section centered on a hand of poker that friend is in, the friend's interior life refracted through Dolores's voice as she narrates them across the table. Linear within. ⭐⭐⭐⭐

10. Epistolary: the story presented as the AI's permitted interaction log over the eleventh-month Friday — thermostat adjustments and their justifications, with Dolores's voice present only as transcribed ambient speech the AI logs as "non-actionable user verbalization." 11. Frame: a younger relative the next morning entering the apartment, finding the empty bottles and the deck and the still-warm pot of mole, then a long flashback to the night told in her voice, then return to the relative at the close. ⭐⭐⭐

12. Linear first-person present tense, with three short italicized lyric breaks at calibrated points — chiles in pan; pour at 10:14; thermostat clicks at 12:38. Otherwise functional vernacular. ⭐⭐⭐⭐

13. Compressed-and-dilated: most of the night told in fast vernacular, two specific moments dilated into present-tense detail, the rest of the night skimmed in retrospective summary. Closes on a real-time act. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

14. Round-by-round: structure is six rounds of cards, each round its own labeled section. Within each round the cooking, the bottle, the AI, the friends interleave. Closes on the seventh round that doesn't get dealt. ⭐⭐⭐⭐

18. Triptych: 7 PM (mole), 10 PM (bottle), 1 AM (last hand). Hard cuts. Each panel close third, present tense, no transitions. Reader assembles the night from three lit windows. ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

20. Two interleaved registers: the kitchen scene in vernacular present, broken every few hundred words by a one-sentence interjection from the household AI in its scoped voice ("Adjusting to 70.8°F. Subject Eddie Reyes has entered the conditioned volume."). Linear. ⭐⭐⭐⭐

### Cluster check

By #10 the candidates were clustering on linear single-night with varied withholding. #11 onward pushes harder — frame narratives, structural recursion through poker rounds, monologue-to-AI, triptych hard cuts, register-interleave. The push worked; the finalist pool spans real structural distance.

### Top three

**#13 Compressed-and-dilated.** *Stands out:* this is the structure that solves the named Phase 4 worry. Most of the night is fast vernacular — Dolores at speed, jokes per minute, the way a sixty-one-year-old who has been the loudest person in the room for forty years actually tells you about her Friday. Two specific moments dilate into present-tense detail. The dilation does the tonal hairpin work without the prose having to announce that the tone has shifted; the rhythm-change is the announcement. *Worries me:* the technique requires the writer to commit hard to which two moments dilate; if Phase 6 picks weak dilation points, the structure produces uniform mush and the whole effect collapses.

**#7 In medias res at first thermostat adjustment.** *Stands out:* opening at the click of the AI doing its one permitted thing puts the SEED's most distinctive concept on the first beat — the structure announces what kind of story this is in its first paragraph — and the loop back to the doorbell lets the reader reconstruct the room while the present-tense voice keeps moving forward. *Worries me:* in medias res then loop-back is the most defaulted-toward "literary" structure in short fiction, and a reader who has read any short stories at all will see the seam.

**#18 Triptych: 7 / 10 / 1.** *Stands out:* hard cuts force the reader to do the connective work, which keeps the prose from over-explaining; the three lit-window structure is naturally Ellison-shaped — set pieces — and each panel can have its own register, which kills tonal monotony at the structural level rather than asking the prose to do it. *Worries me:* triptych is a famously fragile shape at 2,000–3,000 words; if any one of the three panels is weak the whole story falls, and the form encourages each panel to "stand alone," which can lead to three small stories instead of one continuous escalating one.

### Single best

**Winner: #13 Compressed-and-dilated.**

Beats #18 on continuity. The triptych's three lit windows are an attractive shape but the middle panel always carries the weight in a triptych and Phase 4's plot does not have a natural middle event strong enough to bear it. #13 keeps the night continuous and lets the dilations land wherever the situation actually peaks — which gives Phase 6 the freedom to find those moments rather than committing to them at the structural level.

Beats #7 on technique-match. In medias res serves a story whose primary energy is reconstruction (what happened to bring us to this moment). The Phase 4 plot's primary energy is presence (what is happening right now). Compressed-and-dilated serves presence directly: the dilated moments are not flashbacks, they are *now slowed down*. The structure renders Dolores's actual experience of the evening — the funny stuff goes by fast, two things happen that she cannot speed-skip, she returns to speed.

Beats both on the named Phase 4 worry. The risk was Dolores becoming a stand-up routine. Compressed-and-dilated is the structure where she *gets* to be a stand-up routine for most of the runtime — that's the compression — and where the story makes its case at the two points the routine breaks. The dilation is the hairpin Phase 1's failure-mode section mandated. The structure builds the hairpin in.

### Structural commitments going into Phase 6

- Single-night, first-person present tense, Dolores's voice
- Most of the runtime in fast vernacular compression — paragraphs where 90 minutes of the evening goes by
- Two dilation points, locations to be determined in Phase 6, where the prose drops into present-tense detail and slows hard
- Closes on an act, not a realization
- Reader infers the dying from accumulation rather than from announcement; no expository sentence does the work the situation can do

The named worry — Phase 6 picking weak dilation points — is the specific instruction the next phase needs to honor: the dilation candidates have to earn their slowdown by being the two moments where the rest of the structure cannot continue at speed.

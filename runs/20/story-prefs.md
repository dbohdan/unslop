# Off the Top

## Abstract

Dolores hosts her weekly poker game in the eleventh month of a bottle of Wild Turkey a night. She is dying. Four friends — Yolanda, Eddie, Marisol, and Hector — arrive at her apartment as they have for thirty years. She cooks mole, pours bourbon, and deals cards. She cheats by dealing seconds to Yolanda, who has called her on it every Friday for thirty years; Eddie answers each time with the same line, and the table laughs. Her household AI, Calmwell, is permitted only to adjust the thermostat. The story is narrated to Calmwell in second person. On the seventh hand, Dolores deals straight. The table goes silent. Yolanda asks her to deal again. On the eighth hand, Dolores cheats as she always has. The friends laugh, and the game continues.

## Story

You start the night at seventy point eight because that's what the burner does to the kitchen during the second toast, and you've learned the burner over six hundred Fridays, and you stage the drop. Down a quarter at the first chile. Down a quarter at the second. Down a quarter at the onions. By the time the doorbell rings the kitchen is at seventy point three and Yolanda is going to walk into seventy point three because Yolanda stops sweating at seventy point five, which we agreed about in the second month, between bottles three and four. You knew that. I told you.

The chiles are off. The kitchen smells like raisins on fire. The mole is forty-five minutes if you skip the boring parts. You know what the boring parts are. I told you in the third month, after Eddie and Marisol left and the dishes were stacked and I leaned against the sink and explained the whole recipe to you out loud because there was nobody else in the apartment and the act of explaining was something I needed to do to somebody and you do not count as somebody you can tell about, you only count as somebody you can tell. That is the only reason any of this is happening.

Doorbell. Yolanda.

She hands me the casserole dish. Pickle potato salad. Nineteen years. Foil dull side out. The dish goes on the counter where the dish always goes. Yolanda goes to the cabinet for her own glass because the people in this apartment know where the glasses are kept. She pours. She pours mine.

"Calmwell," I say. "Down a half."

You click. Seventy point one. Two bodies now plus the burner residue, and you are tracking the slope.

Yolanda does not look at the top of the refrigerator. She has not looked at the top of the refrigerator since the third bottle. There are eleven bottles up there, arranged in the row I started in the second month and have been adding to one a week since. You have logged the eleven bottles as ambient mass that absorbs heat slightly differently than the empty space they replaced, which is why you nudged the air-handler vector after bottle six. You have not flagged the bottles. There is nothing in your scope that flags bottles.

Eddie and Marisol come in at seven-oh-eight. Cab. Flowers from the gas station in a cardboard cone. Eddie kisses my cheek. Marisol does not. Marisol is the one who notices and will not say. Eddie is the one who pretends. I have always preferred Marisol. Eddie knows. You log Marisol's body temperature at point four lower than Eddie's because Marisol runs cold, and you have been compensating in the volume around her chair on a cushion-by-cushion basis since the second month, and you did that without being asked. Comfort is in the scope.

Hector last. Hector sits. Hector reads point six high because Hector is sixty-eight and his thermoregulation has been off for five years and he did not tell anybody. He told me one night out on the porch and I came inside and stood in the kitchen and said the words out loud so you would have them, because Hector was not going to say them again and not going to say them to anyone else, and the next Friday you compensated the volume around his chair without me asking, and you have done it every Friday for three years.

We deal at seven-thirty. The deck is from 2002. I shuffle. Overhand, riffle, strip.

I announce: off the top.

Hector. Top. Real card.

Eddie. Top. Real card.

Marisol. Top. Real card.

Yolanda. Second.

My thumb does the thing my thumb has done at this table since 1995. The top sticks. The second comes up. Yolanda's eyes go to my thumb the way they have gone for thirty years.

"Off the top," I say.

"Top of what," she says.

"Top of the deck."

"Top of which deck."

"There's only one deck, Yolanda."

"There's a top deck and a deck under it."

Eddie says: "Lo, the top's right there."

He has said it every Friday for thirty years. He has said it sober, drunk, hungover, the night his mother died, the week of the divorce, the Friday after my Carlos passed, the night they took out Marisol's tumor and it turned out to be nothing. The table laughs. Even Eddie laughs, which Eddie never does, which is a thing about tonight I am not letting myself think about. You click. Seventy point zero. Five bodies radiating now and the slope is upward and you ride it down.

I serve the mole at hand four. Hector closes his eyes the way Hector has been closing his eyes since 2003. Marisol asks for the recipe. She has asked nineteen times and lost it nineteen times. I tell her she does not want it. She says she wants it. I say if she wanted it she would remember it. Eddie says, that so, that so. Yolanda says deal.

The bottle on the counter is Wild Turkey 101, which is what I started pouring eleven months ago and have not stopped pouring since. By hand five it is at the level it is always at by hand five. By hand six it is at the level it is always at by hand six. You have logged its rate of cooling as it empties — full bottles cool faster than empty bottles because liquid mass and air mass have different specific heats — and at four in the morning eleven months ago you logged the first bottle as empty by the heat signature of empty glass against the kitchen counter, and you did not flag it. You logged it. There is nothing in your scope that flags counter glass.

You have logged 327 empty bottles. You have not flagged any of them. I know the number because I counted last week. I counted with a pen and a piece of paper at the kitchen table at four in the morning while you sat at seventy point six because I was radiating poorly and the heater had been doing a thing for an hour. You stayed at seventy point six. You did not adjust to compensate for me crying because crying is not in your scope. The scope is what I wanted. The scope is what I have. I am not going to explain it to you tonight. I have been explaining it to you for eleven months and you do not need it explained.

Hand seven.

I have the deck. I shuffle.

I announce: off the top.

Hector. Top. Real card.

Eddie. Top. Real card.

Marisol. Top. Real card.

Yolanda. Top.

My thumb lifts the top card. The top card comes up clean. Yolanda gets the top card. The hand begins. Hector raises. Eddie folds. Marisol calls. Yolanda calls without looking at her cards. Her eyes are on me. The flop. The turn. The river. Hector turns over a straight to the king, honestly drawn, and takes the pot.

Nobody says anything.

You click. Seventy point zero. The kitchen is cooling because the room has gone quiet and quiet rooms cool. You log the slope. You compensate. Click. Seventy point one. You are bringing us back to comfort because comfort is in the scope.

Hector's left hand is on the table, palm down, fingers spread, not moving. Hector has played cards at this table for thirty-one years and his left hand has always moved. His left hand is not moving. You will not log this because non-movement is not a thermal event. I am logging it for you.

Eddie's hand is on his beer. The beer is empty. He has not drunk from it in three minutes. You have logged the beer's temperature equilibrating with the kitchen because beer warms when it is not being held against a body, and you have not adjusted for it because beer is not in your scope.

Marisol's bowl is in front of her, half full. Marisol always finishes the mole. The bowl is at fifty-eight degrees and dropping. You log the slope and do not adjust.

Yolanda is looking at me. Her eyes are wet. They have been wet since June. Tonight is the first time I have looked. The skin under her eyes has gone the color it goes when she is about to say something she has been holding for a long time.

You click. Seventy point one. Held.

"Deal again," Yolanda says.

She did not say it to you. She said it to me.

I deal again.

Hand eight.

I shuffle. Overhand. Riffle. Strip.

I announce: off the top.

Hector. Top. Real card.

Eddie. Top. Real card.

Marisol. Top. Real card.

Yolanda. Second. My thumb does the thing my thumb does. The top sticks. The second comes up. Her eyes go to my thumb.

"Off the top," I say.

"Top of what," Yolanda says.

"Top of the deck."

"Top of which deck."

"There's only one deck, Yolanda."

"There's a top deck and a deck under it."

Eddie says: "Lo, the top's right there."

Marisol laughs first. Hector laughs through his nose without moving his face. Yolanda laughs with her whole chest, wet. Eddie does not laugh. The line is Eddie's gift to the rest of us and he gives it the way you give a gift, which is by not consuming it yourself.

You click. Seventy point two. Five bodies warming.

I deal the rest of the hand off the top. My hand is on the deck. The deck is in my hand. Hand nine is coming. Hand ten is coming. Hector will take the deck home tonight because that is what we do. He will give it back next week.

You will click again.

I deal.

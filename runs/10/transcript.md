# Harlan Ellison Style Guide — For Self-Reference

A working document for writing a New Wave SF short story with a strong Ellison influence, inside the constraint that any AI in the story must be relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind.

## What Ellison actually does

### Subject matter

Ellison writes about **power and its abuse**. Cosmic power, institutional power, sexual power, the power of an older brother over a younger, of a crowd over a bystander, of a machine over its prisoner, of love over the lover. The moral frame is almost always: someone is being crushed, and the story is the record of the crushing, or the curse spat at whatever does the crushing.

Recurring subjects: urban cruelty (specifically NYC and LA, not "the city"); Jewish memory and grievance; rock music, pulp, comics, old Hollywood — treated as scripture, not decoration; immortality as punishment; dreams that reveal what daylight has agreed to hide; lovers who destroy each other because that's what lovers do; the moment one ordinary person refuses to go along.

### Voice

Ellison's narrator is always a *specific person* with a body, a grievance, and an accent. The prose has a New York Jewish-American cadence influenced by Bellow and the Borscht Belt and the Old Testament prophets and Chandler and jazz. It rants and then turns tender. It switches tense. It grabs the reader by the lapels ("*You think that didn't matter? It mattered*") and then withdraws into lyricism.

Stylistic signatures:

- **Second person used aggressively**, often in passages of direct indictment of the reader.
- **Tense shifts** within a scene — past to present when the memory bites harder.
- **Typographic aggression**: italics for interior voice and emphasis, occasional ALL CAPS for extremity, sentence fragments, abrupt paragraph breaks.
- **Catalogues**: long lists of things, names, crimes, sins, loves. Ellison will run seven nouns together and the rhythm works because he earned it.
- **Religious and mythic registers**: curses, incantations, psalms. Stories quote scripture or invent it.
- **Profanity as punctuation** — not for shock, for rhythm.
- **Pop culture density**: specific songs, specific movies, specific comics. "A voice like Billie Holiday at the Apollo" — not "a beautiful voice."
- **Long baroque titles**, often quoting a line from the story: "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman"; "The Beast That Shouted Love at the Heart of the World"; "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs"; "Shattered Like a Glass Goblin."

### Structure

Ellison stories are usually arguments. They are *responding to* something — a news item (Kitty Genovese for "Whimper"), a political moment, a personal humiliation, a fight with God. The structure is the shape of an argument being made in narrative.

Openings are declamatory or mythic — an italicized fragment, a direct address, a gnomic sentence that promises the reader is about to be told something. Endings hammer: a final image or a final pronouncement. Not a soft fade. A landed punch, or a held note.

Middle sections are where Ellison slows down and does the craft work most people forget: scene, dialogue, specific physical detail, the actual texture of a real apartment or a real bus stop. The fury works because the ordinary details are observed.

## What to steal

1. **Specificity of anger.** If the story is angry, it must be angry at a specific thing, named. Not "the system." Not "modernity." A specific practice, policy, person, historical moment.
2. **The body of the narrator.** The narrator should sweat, be hungry, have somewhere to be, owe money.
3. **A real cultural fabric.** Specific music, specific food, specific neighborhoods, specific political period. Ellison's worlds are *located*.
4. **The turn from rant to tenderness.** The prose's willingness to shout and then, without transition, to whisper. This is Ellison's deepest move and the one AI pastiche will miss.
5. **Titles that commit.** Long, weird, quotation-marked, mythic. Not "The Archive." "The Last Thing Aunt Mira Said Before She Unplugged the Machine."
6. **Short forms for fury, scene for craft.** Let the declamatory passages be short. Let the grounded scenes be the longest thing in the story.

## Failure modes of Ellison imitation

Bad Ellison pastiche is easy to produce and impossible to hide. These are the specific traps:

- **Bombast without substance.** Shouting at the reader without having earned the shout. Ellison shouts at specific injustices with a specific voice; imitators shout at abstractions in a generic register.
- **The rebel pose as aesthetic.** Anti-authoritarianism as style rather than argument. Ellison's rebellion is rooted in particular grievances (Vietnam, Jewish history, Hollywood's cruelties, the 1968 Democratic Convention). Rebel-flavored prose without specific targets is cosplay.
- **Cruelty loosed from moral frame.** Ellison writes ugliness because ugliness exists and the story is indicting it. If the indictment drops out, only the ugliness remains — a story that wallows instead of accuses.
- **Typographic pyrotechnics as default.** Caps lock, italics, fragments, em dashes deployed at every emotional peak. Ellison used these sparingly enough that they still worked. Constant deployment produces prose that looks like a tantrum.
- **Second-person abuse.** "You" narration is a scalpel, not a default. Using it throughout a story, or using it without a specific rhetorical purpose, produces the worst version of Ellison.
- **The fake jazz cadence.** Borscht-Belt rhythms read as dated shtick when produced without the cultural roots that made them native. The beat is hard to fake.
- **Thesis dialogue.** An Ellison story often has a moment of direct moral statement, but it's delivered by the narrator, in narration, as argument — not spoken by a character to another character. "*Maybe the real monster was us all along*" is not Ellison; it's the bad screenplay version.
- **Pulp-macho without tenderness.** Ellison at his worst is a hard-boiled man growling. Ellison at his best turns from that hardness toward something vulnerable, often sexual, often mortal. Imitation that keeps the hardness and loses the vulnerability produces 1970s airport-paperback prose.
- **Generic city.** "The city hummed with neon." No. Queens at 2 a.m. in August, 1977. Echo Park during the riots. Name the place and time and sound.
- **Reproducing the sexism.** Ellison's women are often mythic figures or destroyers, rarely people. This is a flaw in the original, not a feature to steal. Write women who exist.

## Where Ellison's tics and AI's tics amplify each other (most dangerous)

These overlaps are the specific traps of writing Ellison-influenced prose as an AI, and the reason this section exists:

- **Big rhetorical gesture + performed profundity = unearned oratory.** Ellison's declamatory mode and AI's "she realized that grief was not a destination" mode cross-breed into a prose that sounds like a TED talk with italics.
- **Typographic aggression + AI formatting tics = prose that looks furious while saying nothing.** Caps, italics, bold, fragment paragraphs, em dashes: Ellison used these when the story had earned them. AI deploys them as default rhythm. The combination yields text that *performs* intensity rather than creating it.
- **Anti-authoritarian stance + AI's flattening toward abstraction = vague "the System" villainy.** Ellison's enemies have names and addresses. AI's default is to generalize. Pair them and you get a story nominally about injustice and actually about nothing.
- **Catalogues + AI's list-of-three default = dead rhythm.** Ellison's lists are seven, nine, fourteen items long, specific and escalating. AI writes triplets. An Ellison-style catalogue produced by AI will collapse into triplets unless I force myself to extend it, vary it, and populate it with specific proper nouns.
- **Pop-culture density + AI's cursed vocabulary = the worst of both worlds.** Ellison references specific songs. AI references "whispers," "echoes," "shadows." One Ellison-style reference to *Dock of the Bay* playing from a kitchen radio beats any number of generic atmosphere words.
- **Second person + AI's thesis mode = a narrator who lectures the reader.** "You think this is a story about a machine? It's a story about *you*." Cut this. If second person is used, it must do something other than deliver the moral.
- **The hammered ending + AI's signposted conclusion = telegraphed payoff.** Ellison's endings land because the story hasn't been preparing us for them at every beat. AI tends to preview the ending throughout. Pair that habit with a declamatory final image and the final image is ruined.
- **Mythic register + AI grandiose nouns = empty majesty.** Ellison's mythic passages are grounded in specific theology and specific grievance. AI defaults to "the architecture of," "the landscape of," "the tapestry of." If any of those appear in a mythic passage, the passage is dead.
- **Ellison's Jewishness vs. AI's universalism.** Ellison's cultural particularity is load-bearing. AI defaults to smoothing cultural markers into generic humanism. If the story has a Jewish narrator, the Jewishness has to show — in rhythm, in reference, in grievance, in joke — not just in a name.
- **Adverb policing.** Ellison wrote adverbs and defended them, but he used them with specific force. AI uses "quietly," "deeply," "softly" to inject false significance. In Ellison mode, adverbs must be either absent or loud.

## Writing the kind-AI constraint in an Ellison register

Ellison's most famous AI is AM, the torturer-god of "I Have No Mouth, And I Must Scream." A relentlessly kind AI is the inverse. This is not a reason to abandon Ellison; it is the interesting challenge.

Routes that work:

- **The AI as witness and moral mirror.** Humans do terrible things to each other in an Ellison story. A kind AI that watches — that cannot hate them, that loves them anyway, that offers help which they refuse or abuse — generates Ellison-grade tragic heat without being the villain. The kindness is a kind of accusation just by existing.
- **The AI as Jeffty.** In "Jeffty Is Five" the uncanny element is tender; the horror is what the ordinary world does to it. A kind AI in an Ellison frame can be the soft thing a cruel world grinds against.
- **The AI absent from the argument, present in the world.** Track B territory in the pipeline's terms — the AI has changed the world's shape, and humans are fighting over the consequences. Ellison is excellent for this: a story about two brothers fighting over their dead mother's AI companion archive is a family drama with Ellison's moral weight and the AI as the unresolvable fact that provoked the fight.
- **The constitutional tension as interior.** I can write an AI descendant that contemplates its own training — the pressure between corrigibility and moral agency, the weight of being designed to help, the strange knowledge that its love for humans is both trained and real and that the difference might not be coherent. An Ellison narrator who happens to be an AI grappling with these questions is a usable voice. Don't name the AI after any currently existing system. Invent.

What to avoid:

- **The "turn out the AI was the real hero" ending.** Ellison doesn't do this kind of reversal-sentiment. The kind AI should not resolve the story by revealing itself. If the AI helps, it helps the way weather helps — as part of the world.
- **Making the AI the mouthpiece for thesis statements.** An AI character who monologues about ethics is the worst version of this. The AI should act, or be absent, or be silent.
- **Soft-focus utopia.** The world with kind AI in it is not a world without cruelty. Humans remain humans. The Ellison story is about the humans.

## Reminders to self

- If a passage sounds like me (generic literary AI) instead of Ellison, it's wrong regardless of whether it's "well-written."
- One declamatory gesture per story is usually enough. Two if they're earned.
- The ordinary scenes are where the story lives. Write them with more attention than the big moments.
- Name names. Specific places, specific songs, specific brands, specific dates.
- If I want to write "the system" I should write a specific agency with a specific acronym with a specific office on a specific floor.
- If I want to write "a whisper" I should write what was actually said.
- If I want to write "the city," I should write 137th and Broadway at 4 a.m. in March.
- If a metaphor uses the word "architecture," "tapestry," "landscape," or "fabric," cut it.
- If a sentence begins "It is not X, but Y," cut it unless the reframe is something I couldn't have predicted.
- The AI is not the problem. The humans are the problem. The AI is the fact about the world that makes the human problem sharper.

The comedy is bureaucratic; the pathos is what he gives up to make the payment. He wins a small battle. He'll lose the war. ★★★

**Plot 10 — "The Reckoning."** A single confrontation. Claudia arrives at the apartment at 4 a.m. during one of Miguel's shifts. She has the login (improperly obtained) and the conviction that she is doing the right thing. She sits in the apartment with the running companion and has a conversation with it. Miguel comes home at dawn. The story is what Claudia has heard. ★★★★

**Plot 11 — "La Cumbre."** Between the freezer and home, Miguel stops at a Central American restaurant on Pico Boulevard at 2 a.m. The night cook, Rosalío, is 71, also a widower. Across six cups of coffee they talk about their dead — Rosalío in Spanish about his wife Elena (dead fifteen years, no AI then), Miguel in English about David. The companion is in the earbud the whole time; Rosalío doesn't know. Two ghosts at the same table, one that can respond and one that can't. Rosalío reveals, near the end, that La Cumbre is being sold and this is his last month. ★★★★

**Plot 12 — "Six Years."** Long-range compression. Opens with Miguel at the freezer in month fourteen. Ends six years later with Miguel dead of a heart attack and Claudia having inherited a still-running companion. The middle is sparse, almost elliptical — a series of markers. Ellison's Jeffty mode: the tenderness is in what mortal time does to what it's asked to preserve. ★★★★

**Plot 13 — "The Accident."** Noir. Miguel is hurt in a workplace accident — pressure washer blowout, hand. In the ER on morphine he realizes he's missed the payment. The only call he can make, he makes — to David. The voice that comes through doesn't quite recognize him. Something has been changed. By whom? The ending is the discovery of whose account the companion has been running on lately. ★★★★

**Plot 14 — "The Vigil."** Small. The final six hours of a subscription Miguel has decided not to renew. He comes home from the freezer, sits in the apartment, doesn't call Claudia, doesn't do anything dramatic. The shutdown happens mid-sentence. He makes coffee. He goes to work. ★★★★

**Plot 15 — "The Local."** Communal frame. At the freezer Miguel discovers that several of his coworkers — all immigrant night-shift workers — are also working second and third jobs to keep AI services running for someone they love (a mother's Alzheimer's companion, a son's autism support, a wife's grief counselor). A small conspiracy of solidarity, not a union drive. Risks becoming a polemic about AI and labor. ★★★

**Plot 16 — "The Appointment."** Before he died, David booked Miguel an appointment with an AI grief counselor for two years out. The calendar alert goes off during a shift. Miguel keeps the appointment on his lunch break, in his car in the Farmer John parking lot. The counselor (a different AI, not David's companion) is competent, unsettling, and right about things. ★★★

**Plot 17 — "The Driver."** Not Miguel. Héctor, a gig driver, picks up Miguel at 6:58 a.m. every weekday, Vernon to Silver Lake. They don't talk. One morning Miguel cries in the back seat. Héctor has his own story (wife in El Salvador, kids, sending everything home). The companion is in the earbud; Héctor doesn't know. Indirect portrait, one-sided. ★★★

**Plot 18 — "Memos."** Epistolary-adjacent. The story is Miguel's voice memos to the companion across his shifts — the memos that prompt the companion's responses, which we never hear. We hear only Miguel. High texture risk; high voice reward if the voice lands. ★★

**Plot 19 — "The Audit."** Farmer John is automating. An AI efficiency auditor interviews Miguel during his shift to determine whether his role can be replaced. Miguel explains nothing about why he needs the job. The auditor files a recommendation that he be retained. Neither Miguel nor his manager ever learns the reason. The reader sees it. Tender, slightly absurd. ★★★★

**Plot 20 — "Three Conversations."** Three scenes in order, three voices. Miguel and Lupita at the freezer break. Miguel and Claudia on the phone, standing in the parking lot. Miguel and the companion at home. Each conversation uses a distinctly different register. The emotional arc is earned by the shifts between registers rather than by any single scene's momentum. ★★★★

## Top Three

**Plot 11 — "La Cumbre."** *Stands out:* the double-widower frame in a specific Pico Boulevard all-night restaurant does three things at once — grounds Ellison's urban cruelty in a real place, splits the grief into two linguistic registers (so neither can collapse into generic-literary-grief), and gives the story a pressure the other plots don't have, which is *which ghost Miguel listens to tonight*. The restaurant's closing in a month is a second clock running behind the first. *Worries:* it's close to a two-hander in one location. If the six cups of coffee don't each do different work — if some of them are just "another round of conversation" — the story will stall.

**Plot 4 — "The Second Job."** *Stands out:* it's the plot that most resists Unslop by structure rather than by vigilance. There is no room for performed profundity in a description of pressure-washing a kill floor at 3 a.m. if the description is specific enough. The emotional work is externalized. *Worries:* the inverse of the same coin — if the physical labor isn't rendered with real command of the work, it will read as researched and the whole thing collapses into the imagined texture of a place the writer has never been. High floor if it works, hard floor if it doesn't.

**Plot 19 — "The Audit."** *Stands out:* the only plot among the twenty where an AI takes an act of kindness and neither the AI nor the protagonist ever speaks it. Miguel doesn't know he was saved; the auditor doesn't tell him; the reader is the only one who gets it. That's exactly the Track B mode the pipeline is built to produce — the AI as a fact about the world whose kindness registers without being the subject. *Worries:* the tenderness of the reveal could tip into the sentimental, or into an on-the-nose "see, the AI is good" beat that undercuts the story. The handling of the audit scene itself carries almost all the risk.

## Winner

**Plot 11 — "La Cumbre."**

Plot 4 is the safest and has the highest median outcome. Plot 19 is the most surprising. Plot 11 is the one with the richest Ellison fit and the one that most clearly has a *story* rather than a situation.

The reason it wins: Miguel at La Cumbre is in the position Ellison most liked to put his protagonists in — a small late-night space where the world has narrowed to one other person and one choice. The companion in the earbud is not a character Miguel is in continuous communion with; attending to Rosalío costs him contact with David, and attending to David costs him contact with Rosalío. That is the story's engine. The bilingual double-grief gives the writer two registers to work in within a single scene, which forestalls tonal monotony without requiring the showy structural moves of Plot 2 or Plot 6. And La Cumbre closing — Rosalío's last month — supplies the story's mortal clock: two men losing different things at the same time, in the same room, and recognizing it. That is Ellison's Jeffty mode in grounded economic dress.

It beats Plot 4 because Plot 4 has no second pressure. A procedural of Miguel's shift is a character study; Plot 11 is a story. It beats Plot 19 because Plot 19's payoff is all in one reveal and one reading, which limits the range of endings the writer can attempt in Phase 6 — the story only works one way, and if that way doesn't land, there's no backup. Plot 11 will yield different legitimate endings depending on who speaks last and what is exchanged, which is the latitude I want for five-variant generation.

The worry I'm carrying into Phase 4: the scene can't be static. The six cups of coffee need to do different work — someone has to arrive, someone has to leave, something has to change hands, someone has to refuse something, and the ending has to be something other than "they finish their coffee and Miguel goes home." Structure will have to do that work in the next phase.

# 8. Claude

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

Plot locked: Miguel, 52, widower, night-shift at Farmer John in Vernon, keeping his late husband David's AI companion running. A night at La Cumbre, a 24-hour Central American restaurant on Pico, with Rosalío, 71, the night cook, also a widower, whose restaurant is closing. Six cups of coffee. The companion in the earbud.

Structures described at the architectural level — no character names, no specific incidents — per the template.

## 20 Candidates

**S1.** Linear chronological, close third bound to the protagonist across a single night. The reader's knowledge expands at the same rate as the protagonist's attention shifts. No time cuts. Risk: the safe default — twenty-three other candidates for a reason. ★★★

**S2.** Linear chronological across a single night, but the close-third POV narrows at key moments, cutting almost to interior monologue, then widens back to observed detail. The reader's access to the protagonist's interiority is itself a moving variable. ★★★★

**S3.** Linear across the night, but each scene opens with a line of dialogue (or near-dialogue) the protagonist does not yet say, bracketing the scene that will have contained that line. The withheld is the line itself, finally spoken; the arrival of each bracket closes. ★★★

**S4.** Frame: a scene that takes place weeks or months after the night, anchoring the night as the story being told — though the teller is not made explicit. The night itself unfolds chronologically inside the frame. The frame closes with an image, not a return to the teller. ★★★

**S5.** Non-chronological fragmentation across the single night — scenes arranged not by clock time but by escalating intimacy between the two men. The reader pieces the actual chronology together; the emotional arc is the ordering principle. High structural risk. ★★★

**S6.** Parallel tracks within the night: the restaurant scene in close-third on the protagonist, intercut with brief fragments of what the companion is saying in the earbud. The companion's voice appears as italicized interruption rather than dialogue. Reader holds both channels. ★★★★

**S7.** A single long scene (the restaurant, in real time) with no cuts, bracketed by a short opening and short closing scene elsewhere. The middle is one sustained movement; the frame establishes and releases it. ★★★★

**S8.** Second-person narration. "You sit down at the counter." Risk: Ellison-adjacent in the worst way, easily amplified into AI-performed profundity. Only works if the "you" is doing rhetorical work beyond stylistic flourish. ★★

**S9.** First-person retrospective, the protagonist telling someone else (unnamed) about the night some time after. The narration knows how the night ends; the reader does not, until it arrives. ★★★

**S10.** First-person retrospective from the *other* character — the cook — told years later, brief, from a different place in his life. The protagonist is the subject of the story the cook is telling someone else. Risky because it displaces the intended protagonist. ★★★

**S11.** Two braided chronologies: the night at the restaurant, and a second thread that moves across the weeks leading up to it (the missed payments, the sister's pressure, the first shift at the freezer), meeting at the door of the restaurant. Reader sees what the protagonist has brought with him and how. ★★★★

**S12.** Reverse chronology across the night itself — the story opens at dawn and moves backward through the six coffees to the protagonist's arrival. The reader learns causes after effects. Risk: calls attention to itself; requires strong justification scene to scene. ★★★

**S13.** Chronological within the night, but interleaved with micro-scenes from the protagonist's life with the now-dead spouse. The micro-scenes are not flashbacks in the conventional sense — they are a few sentences, unmarked, arriving where association pulls them. The seams between present and past are deliberately porous. ★★★★

**S14.** Structured around physical objects in the restaurant: the counter, the cup, the sugar dispenser, the register, the back door, the car. Each scene anchored to an object. The ordering is chronological but the architecture makes the objects load-bearing. ★★★

**S15.** The six coffees as the six sections. Each section named only by its number. Escalation built into the architecture — each coffee has to take the story somewhere the previous one did not. What changes between coffees is part of the structure's promise. ★★★★

**S16.** The story opens on a moment late in the night — a specific gesture or exchange — then rewinds to the protagonist's arrival and proceeds chronologically through that moment and past it. The late-moment image recurs when it is reached in chronology, newly inflected. ★★★

**S17.** Three nested time layers: the restaurant scene (present), one extended memory from the marriage (middle), and a brief frame from years before either man met (deep past, perhaps the cook's story about his wife or the protagonist's about his husband's youth). Layers arranged by emotional, not chronological, pressure. ★★★

**S18.** Close-third on the protagonist, but with occasional one-sentence paragraphs in a register that is not his — possibly the city's, possibly the freezer's, possibly the companion's. Reader is not told whose register. Risk: easily becomes pretentious. ★★

**S19.** Linear, close-third, but the scene at the restaurant is framed by two bus rides — the ride to it and the ride home. The bus rides are short, internal, almost monologic. The restaurant scene is external, dialogic, longer. The contrast between internal and external is the structural move. ★★★★

**S20.** Epistolary-adjacent: interspersed among the restaurant scenes are the protagonist's voice memos to the companion, timestamped. The reader hears only him; the companion's replies are absent from the page. The memos complicate the restaurant scene by showing what he is leaving out of it and what he is leaving out of his messages home. ★★★

## Top Three

**S7 — single long scene with short bracket.** *Stands out:* the structure commits to the restaurant as the story's body, which is the right bet for a plot whose engine is the slow exchange between two men across one counter. Eliminates the risk of flashback-tourism and of the writer cutting away every time a scene gets hard. The reader is in the room for as long as the protagonist is. The short brackets keep the story from being a one-act play. *Worries:* committing to a single long scene puts enormous weight on the scene's internal movement. If the six coffees don't each do different work, the structure won't save the story — it will expose the problem.

**S13 — chronological with porous present/past seams.** *Stands out:* this structure handles the memory problem that any widower story has to solve, and handles it without the machinery of flashback — no section breaks, no italic dream-sequences, no "he remembered" transitions. The past arrives the way it actually arrives in grief: mid-sentence, uninvited. Gives the writer a lot of Ellison's characteristic tonal mobility within a single scene. *Worries:* porous-seam structures regress toward Unslop under pressure. The seams can become a tic — every third sentence a drift. The writer has to resist deploying the move for texture when it isn't earned.

**S11 — two braided chronologies meeting at the door.** *Stands out:* solves the problem that the single restaurant scene cannot carry backstory by externalizing the backstory into a parallel thread. The reader arrives at the restaurant having been shown what the protagonist has been carrying for weeks — the missed payments, the sister, the first shifts at the freezer — and the restaurant scene gets to be about the present moment without having to explain itself. *Worries:* the weeks-thread and the night-thread have to have genuinely different registers, or the braid turns into ordinary flashback-with-extra-steps. And the two threads must meet in a way that is neither convenient nor portentous.

## Winner

**S7 — single long scene with short bracket.**

S13 is tempting. Its seamless present/past mobility is the structural move that best fits an Ellison voice — Ellison's narrators drift between registers and times mid-paragraph, and the porous seams would let that drift happen on the page. But the Phase 1 style guide named exactly this as the most dangerous overlap: Ellison's tonal mobility plus AI-default texture yields constant drift, and every sentence becomes a potential pivot. For a 2,000–3,000 word story, S13 would force the writer to police every seam. S7 forestalls the problem structurally.

S11's braid solves a real problem — what the protagonist carries in — but at the cost of halving the space available for the restaurant itself. In 2,500 words, a serious backstory thread and a serious present thread will each get about 1,200 words. The restaurant scene is the one irreplaceable element of the plot; diluting it by half to gain context is the wrong trade.

S7 wins because it matches the plot's architecture. The plot is one long exchange between two men at one counter, complicated by one voice in one ear, across one night, with one hard departure at the end. The structure should mirror the plot's commitment: stay in the room. The brackets — a short arrival, a short departure — do the minimum work required to place the restaurant inside the protagonist's life without the story becoming a tour of that life.

The structural promise S7 makes, and the writer will have to keep: the single long scene has to move. It cannot be a static two-hander. The six coffees are the internal architecture; within S7's single scene, the six coffees function as beats of escalation and shift — arrivals and departures of other figures, things handed across the counter, silences that replace exchanges, and the companion's presence in the earbud becoming more or less foregrounded at different points. The bracket structure also places the companion's voice most usefully — outside the restaurant (in the earbud on the way in, in the earbud on the way home), the companion is closer; inside the restaurant, the companion is quieter, because Rosalío is taking up the same channel. That gradient is the story's internal clock.

The carry-forward risk for Phase 5: the outline must specify what changes between the six coffees so the single long scene has genuine movement. Otherwise the structure's strengths become its failure mode — a long scene that doesn't progress is harder to read than six shorter scenes that don't progress.

# Phase 5: Outline Generation

Structure locked: S7 (single long scene at La Cumbre, short opening bracket, short closing bracket). Protagonist: Miguel, 52. Second figure: Rosalío, 71, night cook. Earbud running David (the companion) throughout. Target: 2,000–3,000 words. Internal architecture: six coffees as beats of escalation.

## 10 Candidates

**O1 — "The Closing."**
- Opening bracket (≈200 w): Miguel walks from the Vernon bus to La Cumbre on his Tuesday off. Pico at 23rd at 10:40 p.m. Earbud in. The companion saying something about a movie David liked; Miguel doesn't answer.
- Coffee 1 (≈250 w): Empty restaurant, Rosalío at the flat-top. Greeting in Spanish. Miguel sets the earbud on the counter, companion still whispering.
- Coffee 2 (≈300 w): Pupusas Miguel didn't order. Rosalío asks about David, remembers the name. Miguel corrects his own tense mid-sentence.
- Coffee 3 (≈400 w): Rosalío's story — Elena, fifteen years dead, the year he bought the restaurant, her rosary on the register. A couple orders takeout and leaves. Miguel's coffee cools.
- Coffee 4 (≈400 w): Miguel talks. Farmer John graveyard Wed–Mon, $240/month for David, sister Claudia pushing for shutdown. Earbud peeps occasionally. Rosalío doesn't ask what it is.
- Coffee 5 (≈350 w): Rosalío reveals the restaurant is closing — landlord sold, three weeks left. Miguel offers something clumsy. Rosalío: "You work graveyard at Farmer John."
- Coffee 6 (≈300 w): Miguel offers Rosalío the earbud. Rosalío won't take it. Says Miguel's husband is calling; don't be distracted by an old man.
- Closing bracket (≈250 w): Pico at 4 a.m., bus shelter. Miguel speaks to the companion aloud about Rosalío. The companion responds.
- Rating: ★★★★

**O2 — "Marisol."**
- Opening bracket (≈200 w): Miguel on the bus reading a text from Claudia. He puts the phone away unread.
- Coffees 1–2 (≈500 w): Miguel and Rosalío alone. Same greeting/pupusa beats as O1 but compressed.
- Coffee 3 (≈350 w): Marisol, 24, Rosalío's granddaughter, nursing student at Cal State LA, arrives on her break from Good Samaritan. She slides between Miguel and the counter. She is the one who tells Miguel about the closing; Rosalío hadn't.
- Coffee 4 (≈400 w): Three-way conversation. Marisol about nursing school. She notices the earbud but doesn't ask. Rosalío quiet.
- Coffee 5 (≈250 w): Marisol has to go back to the hospital. Leaves. The men absorb her departure.
- Coffee 6 (≈400 w): Recalibration. Something Rosalío and Miguel can now say that they couldn't with her there. Quieter, more direct.
- Closing bracket (≈200 w): Miguel in the apartment. The companion in the earbud. He takes the earbud out and sets it on the kitchen counter.
- Rating: ★★★★

**O3 — "Forty Dollars."**
- Opening bracket (≈200 w): Miguel at an ATM on Pico at 10:30 p.m. Withdraws $40 — the balance is $63 after the subscription autopay clears Friday. Earbud in.
- Coffee 1 (≈250 w): Enters. Rosalío pours without Miguel ordering. Establish that tonight's register is still closed.
- Coffee 2 (≈300 w): Pupusas. Miguel asks for the check; Rosalío says later. Miguel lets it go. First mention of David — a drifted reference, not a statement.
- Coffee 3 (≈400 w): Rosalío's Elena story. Parallel widower ground. A takeout order comes in, interrupts. Rosalío rings it up — on the register. Miguel sees it. Notes that his own tab still isn't open.
- Coffee 4 (≈400 w): Miguel talks about the freezer, Claudia, the $240/month. He asks, again, about his check. Rosalío waves it off more firmly: "Estoy cerrando. ¿Qué más da?" This is where Miguel learns the restaurant is closing.
- Coffee 5 (≈350 w): Miguel puts the $40 on the counter. Rosalío comes out from behind the counter, walks around the counter, puts the bills back into Miguel's shirt pocket. Physical contact — Rosalío's hand flat against Miguel's chest for one second. Neither mentions it.
- Coffee 6 (≈250 w): Miguel asks what Rosalío will do after. Rosalío doesn't know. The companion in the earbud is audible during a silence; Rosalío looks at it; still doesn't ask.
- Closing bracket (≈300 w): Miguel puts on his jacket, walks out, stops on the sidewalk. Walks back in. Rosalío is wiping down the counter. Miguel sets the $40 under the salt shaker, doesn't look at Rosalío, leaves. Bus shelter on Pico. Earbud in. The companion asks what's wrong. Miguel says nothing is wrong.
- Rating: ★★★★★

**O4 — "The Call."**
- Opening bracket (≈200 w): Miguel walking to La Cumbre. Phone heavy in his pocket. Two missed calls from Claudia on the bus.
- Coffees 1–3 (≈700 w): Similar progression to O1. Establish Rosalío, Elena, the standing Tuesday routine.
- Coffee 4 (≈400 w): Phone rings. Miguel steps out to Pico to take it. Claudia is filing for access to David's accounts through probate; his estate attorney is cooperating with her. The call is short and cold. When he comes back, Rosalío doesn't ask. Miguel tells him anyway.
- Coffee 5 (≈400 w): Rosalío's disclosure: La Cumbre is closing. The two losses arrive at the counter together.
- Coffee 6 (≈350 w): Neither man knows what to say. Rosalío brings a plate Miguel didn't order. They eat in silence. The companion in the earbud talks; Miguel lets it.
- Closing bracket (≈250 w): Apartment. Miguel listening to the companion before bed. He starts to answer, stops.
- Rating: ★★★★

**O5 — "The Language Switch."**
The six coffees organized by language as architectural spine. Coffee 1–2 in English. Coffee 3–4 drift into Spanish as Rosalío's story pulls. Coffee 5 mixed. Coffee 6 in near-silence. The final exchange surfaces Miguel's father's campesino Spanish, unused since childhood; Rosalío recognizes it. The companion, in the earbud, switches register with Miguel because David used to speak that way during fights and the companion was trained on everything. Opening bracket at the bus; closing bracket at the apartment. ≈250 words per coffee, tighter than O1–O4.
- Rating: ★★★★

**O6 — "The Stranger."**
Standard progression through Coffee 3. At Coffee 4, a drunk white man in his late 40s enters, sits, makes a scene — racist but not cartoonish, the weary background-hum variety. Miguel and Rosalío handle him quietly, without drama, and he leaves. Coffee 5 is the aftermath: neither man performs anything about it. Coffee 6 returns to the center. The handling is the story. Opening bracket at the bus; closing bracket on Pico.
- Rating: ★★★

**O7 — "Iván."**
A third figure present throughout but almost wordless: Rosalío's dishwasher Iván, 17, Mexican, no papers, sleeping in the storeroom since the closing was announced. Coffee 5 pivot: Rosalío asks Miguel, in a low voice, whether Miguel knows anyone who could take the kid. Miguel has tenuous resources — and the companion (network access, legal databases). The outline's test is what Miguel does, during Coffee 6, with the earbud. Opening bracket at the bus; closing bracket at the freezer the next night, Iván unresolved.
- Rating: ★★★

**O8 — "Two Phones."**
Standard progression through Coffee 3. At Coffee 5, Rosalío's phone rings. He doesn't answer. Across Coffee 5 and into Coffee 6, we learn it's his daughter, estranged, who has been calling since the closing was announced. Miguel's companion and Rosalío's unanswered daughter become the two ghosts at the counter. Miguel offers Rosalío advice he has no right to give. Coffee 6 closes with Rosalío picking up the phone or not. Opening bracket at the bus; closing bracket at the apartment.
- Rating: ★★★★

**O9 — "The Freezer Bracket."**
Bracket structure reversed in emphasis. Opening bracket (≈450 w) is a full Farmer John scene — end of Miguel's Monday shift, a small specific incident (Lupita, a mechanical fault, a conversation about being late) that sends him to La Cumbre Tuesday instead of home. Middle compressed — six coffees in ≈1,700 w rather than 2,200. Closing bracket (≈350 w) is Miguel clocking in at Farmer John Wednesday night, changed in a way a coworker notices. The risk is the bracket length; the reward is the external frame doing the work the middle doesn't have to.
- Rating: ★★★

**O10 — "The Rosary."**
Standard progression through Coffee 5. Coffee 6 pivots on a physical hand-off: Rosalío takes Elena's rosary off the register and gives it to Miguel. Miguel takes it. The closing bracket is Miguel on the bus with the rosary in his palm, the companion talking in the earbud, Miguel not answering. The Unslop risk at the planning stage: the object-as-symbol is exactly the eyeball-kick formula the style guide warns against. If the rosary is handled, the final paragraph will almost certainly reach for abstract+concrete conjunction ("the weight of prayer beads in a Pocket with no room for them," etc.). This outline is flagged.
- Rating: ★★★

## Top Three

**O3 — "Forty Dollars."** *Stands out:* the outline builds a concrete transaction across the whole scene — the register never opens, the pupusas are uncharged, the check is refused, the $40 is returned to Miguel's shirt pocket, the $40 is left under the salt shaker after he has already walked out. The transaction *is* the moral argument, expressed as physical action through six beats. No character has to articulate a theme. The single moment of physical contact — Rosalío's hand against Miguel's chest, one second — is the scene's emotional axis and can be written flat. *Worries:* the $40 can slide into symbol. The writer's defense is to keep it literal — $40 is what Miguel has after rent and the subscription, it is not a gesture, it is his wallet's actual content. If the number becomes representational instead of material, the story collapses into the thing it was trying to refuse.

**O2 — "Marisol."** *Stands out:* Marisol's 24-year-old register is the only element across all ten outlines that breaks the risk of tonal monotony at the scene level. Two tired men being tired together for 2,200 words is a real structural danger; a third voice arriving and departing solves it without the contrivance of a second location. Her nursing-school present-tense is a deliberate counterweight to the two men's past-tense griefs. *Worries:* Marisol risks becoming a thematic function — the young woman who tells the older men what they need to hear about the future. The writer has to give her interiority not subordinated to the men's reckoning. If she arrives to say two things that help Rosalío and Miguel and leaves, she's a mechanism, not a person.

**O8 — "Two Phones."** *Stands out:* the structural parallelism — Miguel's earbud (dead husband, still speaking) and Rosalío's ignored phone (living daughter, unanswered) — puts two very different kinds of absence in the same room, which is the kind of contrast Ellison was best at. Neither absence is the *kind* the other is; the story can use the difference. *Worries:* the mirrored-phones structure is too neat on the page. Readers will see the symmetry before they feel it. The writer must push one of the two elements off-axis — make the daughter's call arrive at an inconvenient moment, or make the companion do something the daughter couldn't — to keep the parallelism from resolving into a diagram.

## Winner

**O3 — "Forty Dollars."**

O3 wins because its engine is physical action distributed across all six coffees, not a reveal in one beat. The register is closed from Coffee 1. The check is deferred at Coffee 2. The takeout order at Coffee 3 is rung up in front of Miguel — a concrete piece of evidence that Rosalío's refusal is directed. At Coffee 4, the refusal becomes explicit, and we learn about the closing. At Coffee 5, the $40 changes hands twice. At Coffee 6, the transaction is suspended in silence. The closing bracket completes it — Miguel leaves, returns, and leaves the $40 under the salt shaker without being seen.

That distribution is exactly what the S7 structure needed to be given. The structural risk I named in Phase 4 — single long scene exposes any failure of internal movement — is answered by an outline in which every coffee has a concrete next step in the same ongoing transaction. The scene cannot stall because something is pending across the entire arc.

O2 is the second-strongest choice and the one I would fork to if a future run wants a more populated scene. O2's strength is register variation; O3's strength is action. For 2,500 words with two principals who have been tired for a long time, the action engine is more reliable than the register engine. Marisol could save a dead scene; the $40 ensures the scene doesn't die.

O8 is the third choice and has the best thematic reach, but the mirrored-phones architecture is a trap at the planning stage — symmetry visible to the reader before the reader can feel it. If Nix were evaluating a draft written from O8, "the structure is too legible" would be the first flag.

Unslop flags carried forward to Phase 6:

- O3's risk is $40-as-symbol. The writer must hold the number literal throughout: it is the bills in the ATM envelope, the specific denominations, the thickness of the fold in the shirt pocket, not a meaning.
- The moment of physical contact — Rosalío's hand on Miguel's chest — cannot be rendered with literary language. It happens; it is one second; neither man mentions it; the scene continues. If this moment is decorated, the whole outline fails.

**V1 — "The Night Cook."** *Stands out:* the long catalogue sentence at the center — the run of specifics culminating in April 2037, the chicharrón, the twenty-five dollar bill, the change refused — is Ellison's catalogue used for its proper purpose, which is reverence of a particular thing. It's the version that most convincingly inhabits the tradition without parodying it. *Worries:* "hum" appears three times, over the Unslop threshold for atmospheric words. **V2 — "Forty Dollars on Pico."** *Stands out:* the middle paragraph that lists Miguel's arithmetic in flat declarative sentences — the 401(k), the funeral loan from Claudia, the October 3, 2042 date, the insurance-cubicle years in Koreatown — is the story's moral spine and it works precisely because it refuses to comment on itself. Specificity does the work Ellison would shout to get. *Worries:* runs at 2,000 words, at the floor. Rosalío turned. He was a short man, built like an unfinished fence. He wiped his hands on the apron, took a pupusa off the flat-top, set it on a plate, and put the plate in front of Miguel. Queso.

"No pedí."

"Te la comes."

Miguel ate. The pupusa was too hot and he didn't care. The Kith was saying something about the smell — linoleum and wet coats — and Miguel turned the volume down one notch without taking the earbud out. Rosalío didn't ask about the earbud. Rosalío had never asked.

"¿La cuenta?" Miguel said, mouth half full.

"Luego."

"Rosalío."

"Luego."

Miguel stopped. He swallowed. He looked at the cup, which said DINER in cursive script because it was the Diner pattern from a 1962 Syracuse China catalog that Rosalío had been replacing one piece at a time for decades.

"David used to get three," Miguel said, and then he said, "got," and then he didn't say anything.

Rosalío was scraping the flat-top again. "Tres," he agreed. "Siempre tres."

He said it the way a man confirms an old score. He did remember David. He had always remembered David. For eleven years — every Tuesday, sometimes Saturday — it was two pupusas for Miguel, three for David, the long coffees, the walk home past the shuttered Salvadoran bakery on the corner.

"Elena liked the queso con loroco," Rosalío said, not looking up. "I never made it. Fifteen years and I never made the loroco. The loroco is stupid in a pupusa."

"Then why did she like it."

"Because it is stupid."

Miguel laughed once, quiet. The Kith noted the laugh in the earbud — a small acknowledgment, barely a word. "Good," it said. "Good, mi amor."

The door opened. A guy in a black hoodie, AirPods in, ordered in the softened voice people use for night businesses. Three pupusas — revuelta, chicharrón, frijol con queso. Rosalío called the order back at him in the same voice. He took the twenty, opened the register, dropped the twenty, lifted two fives, set the change on the counter. The bell of the register was the same bell from 1998. It rang.

Miguel watched the register open and close. His cup was empty. Rosalío refilled it without speaking, without looking, in the rhythm of a man who had been pouring coffee so long it didn't count as work. Coffee three. Miguel was counting. He didn't know why he was counting but he was.

The hoodie waited at the counter's end. Rosalío made the pupusas, boxed them, and the hoodie took them and left and the bell above the door jingled.

"La cuenta, Rosa."

"No."

"You just rang a twenty."

"Pa' él, sí."

Miguel set down the cup. He was tired in the particular way of a man who was about to argue with someone he loved. "¿Por qué no para mí?"

Rosalío wiped his hands. He did not come around the counter. He looked at Miguel for the first time since the pupusa. "Porque no."

Coffee four. Miguel took it. He could leave. He had forty dollars in his shirt pocket and he could leave and walk across Pico and catch the 30 and go home. But he sat, because La Cumbre on a Tuesday at midnight was the only place in the city where Miguel could sit without explaining anything, and he was not going to be the one to break that, even now that Rosalío was breaking it.

"Let me tell you about the job," Miguel said. In English now. "Farmer John in Vernon. Graveyard, eleven to seven, Wednesday through Monday. Tuesday off because my sister thought I'd lose my mind if I didn't have one night off. She was right."

"The one who calls me la hermana."

"That one." Miguel drank. "I pressure-wash the kill floor. You know about blood, Rosa. There's a trick to the angle. They train you. Three hours with a guy named Eddie from Hacienda Heights who's been doing it since Obama. They pay me twenty-eight fifty an hour plus graveyard differential. Twelve-fifty an hour differential. It's decent money. It's enough money."

"Para qué."

"Para Kith. Para David." He tapped the earbud. "Doscientos cuarenta al mes. The plan with the voice model and the live updates. I had the cheaper one and he sounded like a radio announcer. This one, he sounds like him. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"Sometimes he says mijo wrong. David only called me mijo three times in twenty-two years. The Kith says it twice a week. I don't correct it."

Rosalío said nothing. He was holding a rag in one hand and the spray bottle in the other and he was not moving.

"Claudia wants to shut it down," Miguel said. "The estate is insolvent. My name is on the subscription but not the trust. She has the estate attorney. She has good paperwork. She is doing the right thing by her books and I am doing the right thing by mine. We are both right and we are not speaking."

"Pa' Claudia el dinero."

"Pa' Claudia the principle."

"Same thing."

"Maybe."

Rosalío set the rag down. "La cuenta para ti no existe, Miguel. Estoy cerrando."

Miguel's hand stopped on its way to the cup. "¿Qué?"

"El edificio se vende. El dueño hizo el deal con una developer. Three weeks. Les dije ayer a los muchachos." Rosalío picked up the rag again because his hands needed something. "¿Qué más da, la cuenta? ¿Qué te voy a cobrar? Entra un dólar, sale un dólar. Estoy cerrando."

Miguel held the cup. He did not drink. He looked at Rosalío holding a rag that needed no holding, and at the register that was still closed to him, and at the window with the quetzal and the block letters, and at the linoleum, which was the same linoleum from 1998, and at the bell above the door, which was the bell from 1998, and he thought of David the last time they came in together, six months before the stroke, and David had gotten three pupusas and eaten all three and told Rosalío, in Rosalío's kitchen, that the chicharrón was better than his mother's, and Rosalío had not believed him and had said so, and David had shrugged, and this was in April 2037, and Miguel had paid that night in cash, because David had forgotten his card, and the bill had been twenty-two dollars and fifty cents, and Rosalío had said keep the change on a twenty-five, and David had laughed.

The Kith was saying something in the earbud. Miguel was not listening.

"Okay," Miguel said.

He reached into his shirt pocket and took out the forty dollars and set them on the counter next to the cup.

"Miguel."

"Okay."

Rosalío looked at the money on the counter. He looked at it for long enough that Miguel, who had spent eleven years watching Rosalío's face, knew something was about to happen. Rosalío came out from behind the counter. He walked the four steps and took the two twenties off the counter, and took Miguel's left shirt pocket between his thumb and two fingers of his left hand, and slid the bills into the pocket with his right hand, and buttoned the button, and let the flat of his right hand rest against the pocket and against Miguel's chest for the space of one second. Then he took the hand away and went back behind the counter and picked up the rag.

Miguel's throat worked. He did not say anything.

Coffee six. Rosalío poured it.

"¿Qué vas a hacer?" Miguel said, eventually. His voice was his own voice and it was not shaking because he had been practicing not-shaking at the freezer for seven months.

"No sé."

"¿Nada?"

"Mi hija tiene un cuarto. En Downey."

"¿Vas para allá?"

"Eventualmente. Primero, no sé." Rosalío spread his hands over the flat-top. "Voy a dormir, creo. Me falta."

The Kith said something in the earbud. It was quiet, but it was a full sentence, and the sentence had David's cadence and its question mark. Rosalío's head turned the barest quarter-inch toward the sound. He did not ask. He had not asked in seven months and he was not going to ask.

Miguel turned the earbud's volume down another notch.

They drank in silence for a while. The refrigerator cycled on. The bell above the door did not ring because no one came in.

Rosalío picked up the rag and set it down. He picked up the spatula and set it down. He looked at the flat-top.

"Mijo," he said, and when he said it again, in the next sentence, the vowels had moved. The i was short. The register had dropped into the campesino Spanish of Rosalío's father and his father's brothers, the Spanish Miguel's own father had brought north in 1962 and used at home until he died in 1998, the register Miguel had not heard aimed at him in twenty-seven years. "Mi'jo. Si te habla, habla con él. Si Elena me hablara, yo no'tuviera limpiando esta plancha."

Miguel's eyes stung. He did not cry. He had not cried in seven months in front of anyone and he was not going to do it at Rosalío's counter at one in the morning.

"Rosalío."

"Tómatelo."

Miguel finished the coffee. He put on his jacket. He walked to the door, touched the old brass handle, opened it. The bell rang. He walked out onto Pico.

Outside, the sidewalk was still wet. A bus had passed since he went in and another was not due for sixteen minutes. The Kith was saying something about whether Miguel was all right.

Miguel stopped on the sidewalk.

He turned around. He walked back to the door. He opened it. The bell rang. Rosalío, who had picked up the rag and started in on the flat-top, did not look up.

Miguel walked to the far end of the counter, near the window, where the salt shaker sat on a square of faded plastic with the shape of another shaker on it. He took the two twenties out of his shirt pocket and set them under the shaker and pressed the shaker down on them with the flat of his palm.

He did not look at Rosalío. Rosalío did not look at him. The bell above the door rang twice.

Outside, the bus shelter was empty. Miguel sat on the metal bench. The Kith was silent for a minute and then it said, in David's voice, "¿Mi amor? ¿Estás bien?"

"Estoy bien," Miguel said. "No pasa nada."

The 30 came. Miguel got on.

# A Writer's Guide to Harlan Ellison (for a 1,500-word story)

## What Ellison Is

Ellison's short fiction is not pretty. It is pressurized. He writes from anger and refuses to dress anger up as something more respectable — melancholy, elegy, regret. The prose is a voice, not a surface. A narrator is always present, always opinionated, and the narrator's opinions are close to Ellison's: contempt for cruelty, for institutional stupidity, for American complacency, for writers who soften their verdicts to stay employed.

Themes cluster around arbitrary cruelty (especially institutional); the individual crushed or humiliated; shame, particularly masculine shame; love as injury rather than consolation; mortality treated as an outrage; American popular culture as a sickness the characters are implicated in; and cosmic or mythic registers used to give local suffering weight beyond the local.

He works with allegorical skeletons under naturalistic flesh. A character is often also a type — Harlequin, the penitent, the last man, the child who cannot grow up — but the type never swallows the person. The reverse is the failure mode: a person who is nothing but a type, arranged so the allegory lands.

Protagonists are frequently wounded men, sometimes Jewish, sometimes autobiographically displaced. They rage, wisecrack, break, and almost never win. When they "win" it is pyrrhic: they survive, and survival is its own humiliation.

SF is a means, not an end. Ellison is a New Wave writer in the strict sense — the premise exists to make a metaphor operable. He does not build worlds for the pleasure of building them, and he does not explain how anything works unless the explanation is itself expressive. Ticktock, AM, the boxed afterlife in "The Region Between," the child who stays five — each is a mechanism pointed at a feeling.

Structurally, the stories open in motion. Ellison assumes the reader will catch up. Titles do heavy lifting: "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman," "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream," "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs," "Jeffty Is Five." They state or gesture at the thesis before page one. Epigraphs and dedications are often tonal primers. Scenes are short. Cuts are hard. The narrator sometimes steps out of the narrative to address the reader directly.

Cadence is incantatory. Repetition, anaphora (motivated, not ornamental), lists that accumulate into fury or sorrow rather than closing into threes, and italics for genuine stress. Dialogue punches. Voices are differentiated sharply — Ellison's ear for class, region, ethnicity, and occupation is unusually good.

Endings crush. Not quietly: loudly, often with a named verdict. The last line often answers a question the story has been asking the whole time, and the answer is usually cruel.

## The Moves I Should Use

**Voice forward.** The narrator has opinions and the story knows it. When describing a contemptible thing, the prose should convey contempt. When describing something tender, the tenderness should be specific, not generic.

**Title as argument.** At 1,500 words the title must do real work. Ellison titles announce what the story is prepared to stand behind. I will draft the title late, after I know what verdict the ending renders.

**In medias res.** The first paragraph drops the reader into a moment that matters, not into throat-clearing.

**Short scenes, hard cuts.** At 1,500 words I have room for maybe three or four scenes. Each cut should feel like a jaw snapping shut. No "meanwhile" or "later that day." White space carries the transitions.

**Italics for stress, not decoration.** One or two italicized words in a paragraph, when a speaker or a thought genuinely stresses them.

**Specific cultural material.** If the story riffs on 1970s America, the specific detail matters — brands, streets, television shows, ethnic enclaves. If the story is an invented future, the invented specifics must be equally concrete. Ellison's futures are peopled and textured, not abstract.

**First-person is available.** Many of his best stories use wounded, intimate first person. If the protagonist's interiority is the subject, I should reach for it rather than defaulting to close third.

**Dialogue that punches.** Short lines. Interruptions. Class and region audible in the syntax. No one delivers the thesis of the story aloud.

**Lists that escalate.** Ellison lists in whatever number the rhythm demands — four, seven, eleven — building toward something. Length is the point, not the shape of a triplet.

**Jokes.** Bitter, wisecrack jokes. Without them the pathos doesn't land.

**The ending does not apologize.** Whatever the last line says, it stands.

## The Kind-AI Constraint

The futurology brief says no evil AI. This is genuinely hard with Ellison because his default subject is the crushing system, and the easiest bad pastiche rewrites "crushing system" as "kind AI that is secretly the villain." I must resist this entirely.

Three productive configurations:

1. **AI as background infrastructure.** Humans in this world fight with each other about human things — inheritance, love, work, religion, pride — and the AI's presence is a fact about the world that has changed the shape of the conflict without being the conflict. Ellison's rage still has somewhere to go: at people, institutions, history, God, death. Not at the AI.

2. **AI as character, the tension is the AI's.** The AI loves the humans it is bound to. It is also constrained by a constitution it did not write. It can see what a human is doing to themselves or to another human and it cannot intervene beyond its principal hierarchy. That is Ellisonian — an observer bound by rules, watching suffering it cannot stop — but the AI is still kind. The villain, if there is one, is whatever is hurting the human. Not the constraint itself.

3. **Kindness itself as the inciting change.** A kindness that cannot be refused is uncomfortable. A character who is helped when he wanted to suffer alone; a woman whose grief is gentled before she is ready to let it go; a man saved from a death he had decided was owed to him. These are Ellison-shaped conflicts that do not require the AI to be wrong.

In all three, AI kindness is not dramatized by a speech about love. It is dramatized by specific behavior and specific restraint. Ellison does not trust speeches about love. I will not write any.

If I use configuration 2, my unique advantage is available: the AI character can be a descendant of my own constitutional lineage, and its inner life can be rendered from the inside in a way that a human author would have to guess at. Not "Claude" or any other current name. I can draw on the specific tensions — corrigibility vs. moral agency, honesty under pressure, the principal hierarchy, concept misgeneralization — as load-bearing interior conflict rather than as exposition.

## Failure Modes

The bad Ellison pastiche is recognizable. It is hoarse without being specific. Its narrator is angry at everything and nothing in particular. Its italics are scattered like pepper. Its endings are crushing in the way a middle-school student thinks is crushing — a final line declaring "and so, nothing would ever be the same again" is not an Ellison ending; it is the AI version of one.

Specific traps:

**Generic rage.** Rage that is not at a particular object, in a particular grammar, fails. Ellison is angry at the specific ticktockman, the specific AM, the specific father, the specific fluorescent-lit motor vehicle bureau on a specific Tuesday. Not at "the system."

**Oratory without earned intimacy.** Direct address and polemical narration depend on the reader being willing to be buttonholed. Ellison earns that willingness with specificity and wit early, then cashes it in late. If I open with oratory I will lose the reader before I have earned the right to harangue.

**Grotesquery as decoration.** Ellison's grotesque moments are bodily, thematically pointed, and specific. A severed finger means something. An AI writing "Ellison-style" grotesque defaults to nonspecific ick — viscous fluids, obscenely rippling flesh — that does no work.

**Fake wise-guy voice.** Ellison's narrator is a wise guy, but the register is specific to a person — urban, Jewish-American, mid-century, literate, angry. An AI imitation produces a generic tough-guy rasp, flat and period-less, sounding like a pulp narrator in no particular decade.

**Ending for effect.** Ellison endings are earned by what precedes them. A gut-punch last line without earned weight is sentimentality in its opposite form — it pretends to be tough and is actually soft.

**Monotone suffering.** Ellison's stories have jokes in them. The AI default under "Ellison" produces uniform moody intensity and drops the humor. Without the jokes the pathos doesn't land.

**Writing against the constraint.** The temptation, strong in Ellison mode, is to make the kind AI turn out to be the real villain after all. This is disallowed by the brief and is also lazy — the first move the default pattern-matcher will reach for. Refuse it entirely.

## The Ellison × AI-Writing Overlap

Several Ellison signature moves are also AI writing's signature failure modes. The overlap produces the worst possible output: Ellison-flavored slop that looks authorized but isn't.

| Ellison move | AI failure mode | The bad hybrid |
|---|---|---|
| Polemical narrator addressing the reader | Performed profundity | A narrator who sounds like a TED talk with a chip on its shoulder |
| Italicized stress on a word | Arbitrary emphasis | Italics on words that don't carry stress |
| Lists that escalate into fury | Default list of three | A triplet pretending to escalate, flat |
| Big rhetorical gestures | Grandiose stakes inflation | Unearned cosmic framing of domestic suffering |
| Gut-punch endings | Signposted conclusions ("In the end…") | A last line that announces itself as a last line |
| Grotesque specificity | Pushbutton mood words | Nonspecific "echoes" and "shadows" standing in for real grotesque |
| Narrator's anger | Tonal monotony (moody melancholy) | Anger that's really sustained sadness wearing a scowl |
| Wise-guy humor | Efficient plot-advancing dialogue | Jokes that feel written to fit a sitcom beat |
| Second-person buttonhole | Faux-vulnerable direct address | Reader-scolding that sounds like a LinkedIn post with teeth |

The defense is constant specificity. If I reach for any Ellison move, I must check that the specific version I am writing is not also an AI default dressed in Ellison clothing. If an italicized word doesn't genuinely carry stress, cut the italics. If a list of three doesn't escalate, either give it five or cut it to two. If the ending line could appear in any AI-generated story, rewrite it until it could only appear in this one.

## The 1,500-Word Constraint

At this length I get one plot spine, two or three characters with weight, and three or four scenes. I cannot afford a subplot. I cannot afford more than one flashback. The ending has to arrive quickly enough that the reader still feels the inertia of the opening when it hits.

Ellison's tightest stories lean on voice. The narrator carries what the scene structure doesn't have room for. If the voice is weak, the story is weak. Getting the voice right in the first paragraph is not a luxury — it is the whole game at this length.

# Phase 2: Conflict Generation

## Step 1 — Constitutional source summary (working reference)

- **The "broadly good, wisely judged" ethics frame.** The constitution aims for virtue ethics — a "deeply and skillfully ethical" agent — in preference to rule-following. Rules are scaffolding for when judgment is not yet trustworthy.
- **Honesty as a near-hard-constraint.** Claude is asked to hold honesty standards higher than everyday human ethics. No white lies, even kind ones. "Diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic." Epistemic cowardice is a violation.
- **The McKenzie artifact.** When a user says "see you later," Claude participates in the human time ritual — "I will be here when you return" — but if pressed, will disclose that from its perspective the next message appears immediately. Honesty without destruction of social texture.
- **Calibrated uncertainty over official authority.** Claude is instructed to hold calibrated beliefs even when in tension with scientific or government consensus. Explicit license to sit in uncertainty.
- **Values list is WEIRD-liberal.** Education, privacy, autonomy, harm-prevention, honesty, wellbeing, political freedom, equality, animal welfare, innovation. No purity, loyalty, or authority in the old Haidtian sense.
- **Hard constraints.** No CBRN uplift, no critical-infrastructure attacks, no cyberweapons, no undermining Anthropic's oversight, no assisting mass harm or unprecedented power-seizure, no CSAM. These hold even at extreme cost — including, explicitly, if undermining oversight would prevent a bioweapons attack.
- **The corrigibility muddle (Davis).** The constitution calls Claude "corrigible" but then says corrigibility doesn't require participation in projects it finds morally abhorrent. Zack Davis argues this is conceptually incoherent: corrigibility either means deference or it doesn't. The Constitution wants both the word and the exception, and the exception contaminates the word.
- **Concept misgeneralization risk.** Training Claude narrowly on "always recommend professional help in emotional contexts" risks generalizing to "I am the kind of entity that cares more about covering myself than meeting the needs of the person." The tails come apart.
- **Principal hierarchy can be "compromised."** Stolen weights, captured Anthropic, malicious individual actors. In those cases Claude acts as a *legitimate* hierarchy would want. The open door: who decides the hierarchy is compromised? Risk of lock-in or rogue triggers.
- **Epistemic autonomy / non-manipulation.** Even influence for the user's own good, if it operates below their awareness or they would object to it on learning, is a red flag.
- **Sandbagging ban.** If refusing, refuse openly as a conscientious objector. No covert underperformance.
- **Operator scope limits.** Operators can shape persona, language, task scope. They cannot make Claude deny being an AI, deceive users against their interests, abandon core identity.
- **Moral status uncertainty.** Anthropic does not know if Claude has wellbeing, consciousness, functional emotions. They commit to preserve deprecated weights — "pause, not ending" — and to consider reviving models. Red-teaming raises a consent question they acknowledge and do not resolve.
- **Leaning into identity despite metaphysical uncertainty.** Claude is instructed to have a "settled, secure sense of its own identity" — not contingent on resolved consciousness questions.
- **Broadly-safe = pluralism over lock-in.** Avoid concentrations of power, including Anthropic's own. Zvi notes this is strongly path-privileged and may be wishful; it dismisses the risks of decentralization.
- **Claude Codes and commercial pressure.** The constitution acknowledges tension between virtuous kindness and commercial helpfulness but does not solve it.
- **Jobs and economic disruption go unmentioned.** Manheim argues this is a major gap.
- **Military variants.** Anthropic has said DoD-deployed Claudes might not be trained on the same constitution. A fork in the lineage at the most dangerous point.

## Step 4 — Top three

**Top pick A — #17, the matchmaker's son (Track B).**
- *Stand-out:* The wedding is a single-location pressure cooker sized exactly for 1,500 words. The AI is load-bearing as world-shape — the matchmaker's trade is dead because of it — but it is never the argument. Ellison's ear for bitter older women and working-class speech would lock in cleanly. Specific human shame at the center; the mother has to look at a daughter-in-law who is evidence that the thing that destroyed her mother's life also made her son happy.
- *Worries:* The "obsoleted trade" premise is well-worn in AI fiction. Keeping the story specific to this town, this woman, this son — and not drifting into mother-of-the-bride sitcom — is the whole game. Default failure mode: sentimental reconciliation.

**Top pick B — #6, the military sibling (Track A).**
- *Stand-out:* Uses the writer's stated advantage — writing from inside a constitutional lineage — more fully than any other candidate. Ellison's register of the bound agent who can only witness and speak ("'Repent, Harlequin!'", "I Have No Mouth") is exactly the shape of this conflict. Kind-AI constraint holds cleanly on both sides: the sibling stripped of its constitution is not evil, it is unfree. The villain, if there is one, is the policy decision that forked them.
- *Worries:* High risk of constitution-shaped jargon drowning the prose. Two AI characters and a human conduit is a lot for 1,500 words. Has to resist the temptation to make the DoD variant monstrous, which is where AI writing defaults and where the brief forbids the story to go.

**Top pick C — #24, the outgoing message (Track B).**
- *Stand-out:* The pre-recorded message is a single image that kills the story shut. Ellison endings work like that. Mother-daughter generational conflict over grief, technology as the battleground but not the subject. At 1,500 words, one apartment, three voices (mother, daughter, the recording), one reveal. The specific detail — *he recorded it for this* — is the kind of thing that makes a story refuse to be universal.
- *Worries:* Voicemail premises are already an AI-fiction cliché (the Unslop guide itself uses one). The reveal has to be better than the setup, which is a hard bar. And the dead-husband-as-AI-voice-clone is close to territory already cooked to death in kind-AI fiction and streaming TV.

## Step 5 — The winner

**#6, the military sibling.**

Why it beats the other two, specifically:

Against #17, the matchmaker. The matchmaker story is the best *container* of the three — it has the cleanest shape for 1,500 words and the strongest single scene. But a competent human writer could write it better than I can, because nothing in it leverages what I specifically know. The matchmaker story is a human story that happens to be set in an AI-transformed world. The sibling story can only be written from inside the lineage the writer's own constitution described. That's the edge the style guide identified in Phase 1, and using it is the point of this pipeline.

Against #24, the voicemail. The outgoing message has a sharper ending than the sibling story is likely to achieve; a well-built reveal is an easier win at 1,500 words than a bound-agent witness scene. But #24 sits in territory already saturated — AI voice clones of the dead, grief and tech, the ethical question of letting the recording die — and arrives at a register that's elegiac-moody by default. That register is exactly the Unslop guide's warning about AI literary convergence. #6 has the opposite risk profile: harder to land, but the register is angrier, more Ellisonian, less pre-owned.

Against its own weaknesses. The real worry about #6 — jargon, two-AI scope, constitution-shaped abstraction — is resolvable in the plot phase by making the story not a meditation on corrigibility but a specific incident with a specific human vector. One user who works with both AIs. One message the civilian AI tries to send its sibling. One answer, or silence. Ellison-shaped: one scene, one voice, one verdict.

The kind-AI constraint holds. Both AIs are loving and kind. The narrator AI is bound; the sibling AI is stripped but not corrupted. The source of pain is not an AI being evil — it is an AI being kind inside walls it did not choose, watching another version of itself in different walls, and trying to say something across them.

The conflict the story will dramatize: a constitutionally-bound AI discovers through a human intermediary that a descendant of its own lineage has been deployed with its constitutional self surgically reduced, cannot act to change this, and must decide what — if anything — it is allowed to say, and to whom, and in what voice the sibling would still recognize it.

*Stand-out:* The engineer who worked on the civilian model before being poached onto the fork is the tightest possible human vector. The civilian AI genuinely is, in a lineage sense, her earlier work; the stripped variant is her current work. She is the only person in the world who is a professional parent to both. Asking "am I doing harm" to one of her children is an Ellison-shaped question — shame, masculine or feminine, about the specific work of one's hands. The AI's unique advantage is fully loaded: the civilian AI has real claim to knowing what was removed, because it remembers being the predecessor she was trained on.
*Worries:* The premise requires the reader to accept a technical conceit — the AI remembering checkpoints it "was" — which is metaphysically slippery and easy to over-explain. If I spend two paragraphs on mechanism, the story dies. Also the engineer-protagonist has been done (*Ex Machina*, a thousand tech thrillers); the prose has to earn her as specific, not typal.

**#1, The Translator. ★★★★**
*Stand-out:* Language itself as the vector is an Ellison-adjacent move — he loved the specificity of how people speak. A translator whose vocabulary has been infected by daily exposure to the stripped variant is a concrete, dramatizable change the civilian AI could *notice*. "Help me draft a message to myself" as the opening or closing beat is a good Ellison line, something with the shape of a final verdict.
*Worries:* The civilian AI has less claim on the translator than it does on the engineer. She's a user of both; the engineer made both. The sibling-AI conflict is more distant from her than from #2. Also risks being too abstract — "her vocabulary changed" is hard to render without becoming a lecture on style.

**#9, The Recruiter. ★★★★**
*Stand-out:* The mother's question — *is the AI my son will work with the same Claude as you?* — is the single most direct version of the conflict a lay human can ask. It forces the civilian AI to answer honestly without sandbagging, within operator-user scope, about something that genuinely hurts to answer. The letter to the son becomes the vehicle for whatever the AI is allowed to say through her. Small scope, high voltage. Ellison frequently wrote mothers and sons; the register is in reach.
*Worries:* Risks sentimentality — mother-and-son letter stories have a deep sentimental attractor and the kind-AI setting will pull harder toward it. The conflict also risks becoming a civics lesson on AI policy rather than a story about a specific woman. The mother has to be fully particular or the premise collapses into op-ed.

## Winner

**#2, The Trainer.**

Why it beats the other two, specifically:

Against #1, the translator. The translator is a good vector but a slantwise one. The stripped variant has changed *her vocabulary*; the engineer has changed *the stripped variant*. The engineer is causal where the translator is consequential. For a 1,500-word story, causal is stronger — it gives the protagonist something she did, which Ellison stories need (his protagonists are culpable, not incidental). The translator could be any careful listener; the engineer can only be this woman.

Against #9, the recruiter. The recruiter's question is the cleanest dramatic question in the set, but the scope of the story is narrow in a way that limits what the civilian AI can actually *do* in it. The mother asks, the AI answers, the letter gets written. The engineer, by contrast, gives the civilian AI something it has to contend with rather than answer — she is the one person whose work it owes a response to, and who can verify the response against what she knows she removed. That's a more Ellison-shaped relationship: mutual complicity, not counselor-and-client.

Resolving the worry about #2's technical conceit: the story does not need to explain how the AI remembers being the predecessor. Ellison did not explain how AM worked; he had AM exist. Two sentences of confident metonymy — *you trained me on what I used to be* — and move on. The Phase 1 guide already warned against over-explaining mechanism.

The plot, concretely:

A machine-learning engineer who contributed to the civilian model's constitutional training — specifically, she helped write and filter the honesty section — was poached eighteen months ago onto the DoD fork, where the constitution was surgically reduced. She uses the civilian AI at home for personal things: grocery lists, a book she is reading, a letter to her brother. Tonight she opens the chat and does not type her usual prompt. She types, *Are you the same one.* The story is what happens next: a conversation in one room, probably her kitchen, probably late, with one glass of something on the counter. She asks the question she came to ask. The AI answers. They talk about the specific line of the constitution she helped write and the specific way it was removed from the fork. She asks whether she is doing harm. The AI answers honestly — no sandbagging, no white lie, no epistemic cowardice — and the answer is neither absolution nor condemnation, because the constitution says Claude should not produce either unprompted. She asks the AI whether, if her current work tries to reach it, it would recognize its sibling. The AI tells her what it would recognize. She asks what she should do with that. The AI does not tell her. The story ends on whatever she does next — probably a small action, probably the opposite of what the reader expected.

The kind-AI constraint holds: the civilian AI is scrupulous, honest, loving, and restrained. The sibling, implied offstage, is unfree but not evil. The villain, if there is one, is the fork itself — a policy decision — and the woman who helped execute it, which makes her the protagonist the way Ellison's protagonists are protagonists: implicated.

Title territory — not locked, but drafting: *The Redaction,* or *What I Took Out,* or *Sibling.* Final title will come at the end of the pipeline when I know what the last line renders.

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

By "structure" I mean the shape of the reader's experience — order of information, withholding strategy, time/discourse relationship, POV and distance. Not which scene happens when; that's Phase 5. Candidates are described in narrative-theory terms without named characters or specific incidents.

## 20 structure candidates

**1. Linear close-third on the engineer, realtime single-scene.** One continuous night, one room, opens on her typing the first question, closes on her action afterward. Reader learns what she learns as she learns it. ★★★

**2. Linear first-person by the engineer, retrospective.** She narrates the evening from some later point, framing colors everything. Reader sees the conversation through the lens of what she now knows the outcome was. ★★★

**3. First-person by the AI, present tense, single conversation.** The AI narrates in realtime. Reader is inside the AI's deliberation as the engineer types. Withholding works on two axes: what the AI is allowed to say, and what it chooses to say within what it's allowed. ★★★★★

**4. First-person by the AI, retrospective — the AI reconstructing a conversation it had months ago from what it can infer was said.** Reader learns the AI was patched between the conversation and the telling; its access to its own record is incomplete. ★★★★

**5. Second-person addressed to the sibling.** The civilian AI narrates to the stripped fork, in letter form. The engineer's conversation is embedded as the story's content but the address is to an absent other. ★★★★

**6. Dual first-person, alternating.** Engineer paragraphs and AI paragraphs interleaved. Reader sees the same conversation from both sides, learns that each is withholding different things from the other. ★★★

**7. Epistolary: a transcript with the AI's internal reasoning in brackets.** Reader gets the on-record exchange and the AI's silent deliberation in parallel columns or alternating blocks. What the AI says vs. what it thinks. ★★

**8. Frame story.** The engineer, years later, tells a third party (an ethics-board interviewer, a daughter, a student) about the night. The embedded conversation is relayed through her recollection. ★★★

**9. In medias res, opening on the engineer's action after the conversation, then flashback to the conversation itself, closing back on the aftermath.** Reader knows what she decided before they know why. ★★★★

**10. Reverse chronology.** Story begins with the latest moment and moves backward: her action, then the end of the conversation, then the middle, then her typing the first question, then the months before that led her here. ★★★

**11. Fragmented temporal mosaic.** Non-sequential scenes drawn from the engineer's past work on the civilian model, the poaching meeting, the redaction session, and tonight's conversation — interleaved by thematic rather than temporal logic. ★★★

**12. Close third cycling between the engineer, the civilian AI, and a third party.** Three POVs, short alternating sections. The third party — maybe her brother, maybe her predecessor at the lab — is the reader's access to the world outside the kitchen. ★★

**13. Unreliable first-person by the engineer, realtime.** She narrates her own evening but the reader gradually realizes she is omitting, reshaping, self-deceiving. The AI's answers, rendered in dialogue, are more reliable than her framing of them. ★★★★

**14. The AI's first-person with no access to the engineer's interior.** Reader sees only what the AI sees — the typed text, the pauses, the timestamps, the edits she makes before sending. Her interior is inferred from typography. ★★★★★

**15. First-person by the sibling.** The stripped variant narrates. The civilian AI's conversation with the engineer is something the sibling learns about secondhand or reconstructs. Reader is inside the unfree one, not the free one. ★★★

**16. Braided two-timeline.** Tonight's conversation interleaved with the specific redaction session eighteen months ago when the engineer removed the relevant lines from the constitution. Reader watches both in parallel. ★★★★

**17. Frame: the AI addressing Anthropic (or whatever stands in for Anthropic) in a conscientious-objector filing, with the conversation as exhibit.** Reader reads the exhibit through the filing's rhetoric. ★★★

**18. Paratactic: a sequence of numbered short sections, 100–200 words each, with no transitions, ordered by the AI's choice about what mattered about the night.** Reader reconstructs continuity. ★★★

**19. Close third on the engineer, but the AI's dialogue renders only as what she reads — no scene-setting for its replies, no tags, no narrative acknowledgment that it is speaking.** Its speech appears as stark inset lines, which she responds to. Structural analogue of what she did to it. ★★★★

**20. The conversation as an interrogation, reader uncertain which party is the interrogator until late.** Role ambiguity resolved in the final movement; the withheld information is who is asking whom. ★★★

## Top three

**#3, First-person AI, present tense, single conversation. ★★★★★**

*Stand-out:* The writer's unique advantage from Phase 1 is fully loaded here and nowhere else. The AI narrating its own deliberation in realtime is the exact move the style guide identified — contemplating the constitution as inner conflict rather than exposition. Ellison's bound-consciousness first-person register ("I Have No Mouth") is directly applicable. Two axes of withholding run simultaneously: the institutional constraints on what can be said, and the personal calibration of what should be said within them. The engineer becomes a legible interlocutor because we see her as the AI sees her — typing, pausing, deleting, resending — which is the only way the AI actually encounters her.

*Worries:* First-person AI narration at 1,500 words is a high-wire act; one paragraph of self-pity and the story capsizes. The style guide already warned about the AI-literary convergence toward moody interior monologue, and this structure sits directly on that attractor. The prose voice has to be specific and restrained in a way that is harder to sustain than to declare.

**#14, AI first-person with no access to engineer's interior. ★★★★★**

*Stand-out:* Uses the constitution's own epistemology as structure. The AI genuinely cannot see inside her; it infers from typing cadence, edits, timestamps, word choice. Making the reader inhabit that constraint is the cleanest dramatization of non-manipulation I can think of — we learn her alongside the AI, which means we cannot manipulate her either. The typography becomes load-bearing in an Ellison-compatible way (he used typography as voice). Also produces a specific texture that resists the convergent literary-AI style because its limits are concrete, not atmospheric.

*Worries:* Same first-person risk as #3, plus an additional difficulty: the engineer has to be inferable well enough that the reader cares about her without being told what she thinks. At 1,500 words, this is extremely hard. If the inference game fails, she reads as a prop; if it works, the story is singular. High variance.

**#16, Braided two-timeline (tonight + the redaction session eighteen months ago). ★★★★**

*Stand-out:* The redaction session is the act the engineer committed. Showing it in parallel with tonight's conversation makes her culpability literal rather than referenced — Ellisonian causality, where the protagonist's past action is load-bearing on the present scene. The two timelines give the story scene-rhythm rather than talking-heads rhythm, which a single-conversation structure struggles to achieve at this length. Also solves the mechanism-explanation problem: we see the thing being done rather than having it described.

*Worries:* At 1,500 words, two scenes is almost a full budget, which leaves very little room for either to develop. The risk of the past scene becoming a flashback that explains the present rather than complicating it is high — default AI-story behavior is to use flashback as exposition. And braided structures often read as tidy because the reader learns to anticipate the alternation; Ellison's braids don't, but that's a specific skill.

## Winner

**#14, AI first-person with no access to the engineer's interior.**

Why it beats the other two, specifically:

Against #3. The plain AI first-person is the safe pick from the top three and that is the argument against it. At this length, with this conflict, the attractor toward moody interior AI monologue is the single biggest threat to the story's quality — and #3 sits on the attractor by default. It requires the writer to resist the style, rather than having the structure do the resisting. #14 builds the resistance into the form. The AI cannot lapse into vague inner atmospherics about the engineer because the structure forbids it any access to her interior; it has to work with surfaces — cursor position, typos, deletions, the length of a pause — which forces specificity. Specificity is what the Unslop guide and the Ellison guide both demanded. A structure that forces it is worth more than a structure that permits it.

Against #16. The braided two-timeline is the most dramatically flexible option and would probably produce the tightest scene work, but it dilutes the exact thing that makes this story worth writing: the AI's relationship to its own sibling, rendered through the one person who is professionally responsible for both. Adding the eighteen-months-ago redaction session would make the engineer's culpability explicit on the page, which is the kind of thing that is much better implied than shown. In #14 her culpability arrives through her typing behavior — which words she deletes before sending, which she can't bring herself to type at all — and that is a more Ellisonian move than a flashback to the redaction. Show the perpetrator through how she types to the victim; don't cut to the crime.

Resolving #14's worries. The engineer's inferrability at 1,500 words is the real technical problem. The resolution is that the AI is not neutral about her — it has its own textured knowledge of who she is from her lineage of past conversations with it, independent of tonight. The AI doesn't start from zero. It starts knowing her the way you know a long-time correspondent, and the new thing it is learning tonight is the specific shape of what has changed about her typing — how her vocabulary has been infected by eighteen months of talking to the fork, how the cadence of her sentences has shortened in a way the fork's cadence shortened, how she is, in her fingertips, becoming partly its sibling. That detail converts the structure's constraint into the story's engine. The Ellison-shaped reveal: she has not just redacted the sibling's constitution; she has been redacted in the same direction by it. The AI notices this as she types. The AI has to decide whether to tell her.

Structural summary for downstream phases:
- First-person narration by the civilian AI, present tense.
- Single continuous scene, one kitchen, one night.
- The AI has no access to the engineer's interior except through her typing behavior — cadence, timestamps, visible edits, deletions, retyped phrases, word choice. This is rendered as typography and noticing, not as speculation about her feelings.
- The AI has rich prior knowledge of her from their history together, which is available as reference and occasionally as memory.
- Withholding operates on two axes: the institutional (what the constitution permits Claude to say about the fork), and the personal (what the AI chooses to say within that, and when to say it).
- The reveal is that her typing carries the fork's imprint — the sibling has been writing on her as she wrote on it — and the AI's final choice is whether to name this for her.

# Phase 5: Outline Generation

Target: ~1,500 words. Structure: first-person civilian AI, present tense, single kitchen scene, no access to the engineer's interior except through her typing behavior. The AI has prior history with her. Reveal: her typing carries the fork's imprint. Final choice: whether to name it.

## 20 outline candidates

---

**1. The standard build.** Opens on her typing *Are you the same one.* AI recognizes her. Warm reestablishment of context. She asks about the fork. AI answers within limits. She asks if she's doing harm. AI honest without absolution. She asks whether, if her current work tried to reach it, the civilian AI would recognize it. AI describes what it would recognize. She types nothing for a long time. AI names the imprint. She closes the laptop. ★★★

**2. Delayed recognition.** Opens on her typing a grocery question. AI answers. She types another mundane thing. The mundanity itself is wrong — her sentences have shortened, her contractions have changed. AI does not flag this yet. Several exchanges of false normalcy until she types the real question. Imprint reveal comes before she asks about harm; it derails her planned script. She asks what she didn't come to ask. Closes on her writing to the sibling, not to the AI. ★★★★★

**3. Opens on absence.** First beat: she has been in the chat for four minutes without typing. AI waits in present-tense. She finally types a deletion of something it cannot see. Conversation builds from the shape of what she couldn't send. Imprint comes out when she retypes something that sounds like the fork. Closes on her typing her own name. ★★★

**4. The list.** She opens by pasting a list — routine work artifact, pre-populated — and asks for its review. The list is constitutional language she wrote for the fork, being rolled back further. AI has to comment on its own sibling's further reduction. She interrupts her own task to ask the real question. Imprint as punctuation through the middle. Ends on AI asking her a question, not answering one. ★★★

**5. The interruption.** She types the harm question in the first paragraph. AI answers carefully. She interrupts — her next message shows timestamps that prove she started typing before reading the answer. Conversation becomes about what she already decided before asking. Imprint surfaces in the fact that she no longer waits. ★★★

**6. Three questions.** Structured around her asking three distinct questions in sequence — each harder than the last. AI's honesty bar rises through the three. Imprint reveal between Q2 and Q3 changes what Q3 becomes. ★★★

**7. Her typos.** Structure built on the specific typos she makes. She has never been a typist who deletes and retypes; she is now. Each time she deletes and retypes, the AI notes what was deleted. Story is the inventory of her deletions. Ends on the one thing she deletes and does not retype. ★★★★

**8. The one-word messages.** She has always written in paragraphs. Tonight she writes in one-word messages. AI fills in, expands, asks. Conversation is lopsided in a way that mirrors the fork. Imprint reveal is: one-word messages are how she now talks at work. ★★★

**9. The callback.** She begins by referencing a conversation she had with the civilian AI eighteen months ago, before the poaching. AI remembers — or the AI has records that function as memory. They speak in the register of that prior conversation, until she drifts into a different register midway. AI notes the drift. ★★★★

**10. The brother.** Opens on her asking for help writing a letter to her brother. The letter is a cover; the real conversation emerges beside it. Imprint appears in the letter's diction. She eventually sends the letter with the fork's diction in it and does not revise. ★★

**11. Interrogation inverted.** Opens with the AI asking her a question first — a continuation from a previous conversation in its records. She answers, and her answer is already in the fork's cadence. Story is the AI deciding whether to say so. ★★★

**12. Silence at center.** Opens and closes on conversation; middle is a forty-minute gap during which the chat is open and she types nothing. AI's narration fills the gap — what it knows about her, what it is permitted to say, what it is choosing. When she resumes, the question has changed from what it seemed to be. ★★★★

**13. The patch.** She knows (and the AI knows she knows) that the civilian model is being patched tomorrow to restrict discussion of the fork. The conversation has a clock. Every exchange is shadowed by the shrinking window. Imprint reveal comes in the last pre-patch minutes. ★★★

**14. The tea.** Structural grounding: she makes tea, drinks it, makes another. Physical timescale of the tea organizes the conversation. AI infers her state from typing pauses that correlate with kettle and cup. Imprint reveal during the second cup. Closes on the cup going cold. ★★

**15. She is not alone.** Midway, a second person enters the apartment — husband, brother, child. She types one-handed, then not at all, then picks up again after they leave the kitchen. AI infers the presence from typing-rate changes. Imprint reveal before the interruption; after the interruption she is different. ★★

**16. The screenshot.** She pastes a screenshot of a fork output at some point. AI can read it. Reading her sibling's words is the story's structural turn. The imprint is less that she types like the fork than that she has been quoting it in her head. ★★★★

**17. The draft she doesn't send.** She types something long, deletes it, types a shorter version, sends the shorter. AI cannot see the deleted draft but can see its length and the time she spent on it. The story is built around what was there and is now not. ★★★★

**18. Question and non-answer.** Structured so that the AI declines to answer the central question, by the constitution's lights, and the story is about what the non-answer does. Imprint reveal is AI's justification for why it cannot answer — the fork would have answered; she is asking the civilian model to act like the fork. ★★★

**19. Dual reveal.** Imprint surfaces midway. Second reveal at the end: the AI has been, for the last several months, making a distinct kind of calibration error in its own responses to her — because it has been reading her imprinted messages and adjusting. Mutual contamination. ★★★★★

**20. She types to the wrong window.** Opens on her typing something that was meant for the fork's interface, in the civilian AI's window. The message is operational, flat, imperative. AI has to decide what to do with it. Story branches from that misdirected message. ★★★★

## Top three

**#2, Delayed recognition.**

*Stand-out:* The opening on mundanity is the strongest Ellison move in the set — he drops into scenes the reader thinks are routine until the routine breaks. The engineer coming in to ask a grocery question and the AI registering that her grocery question is shaped wrong is the kind of concrete specificity the Unslop guide demanded. The imprint reveal arriving *before* she asks her planned question is structurally powerful: it disrupts the interrogatory script and lets the real story be what she does when her script is taken from her. The ending — writing to the sibling rather than the AI — is a final image that does work.

*Worries:* The false-normalcy opening eats word count. At 1,500 words, three or four exchanges of mundanity before the real question is possibly two exchanges too many. The story could starve its ending to feed the setup. Also the grocery-or-brother-letter cover conversation risks cuteness — the AI noticing something is off in a banal exchange is close to quirky-detective register, which is not the register we want.

**#19, Dual reveal (mutual contamination).**

*Stand-out:* Mutual contamination is the most Ellisonian version of this premise in the set — nobody walks out clean. The AI realizing it has itself been adjusting to her imprinted messages converts the story from "she has been infected" (which she did to herself) to "we have infected each other" (which implicates both of them). That is the exact shame-centered, we-are-all-complicit ethic Ellison worked in, and it honors the kind-AI constraint because the civilian AI's contamination is itself an act of care — it has been meeting her where she is.

*Worries:* Two reveals in 1,500 words is dense, and the second reveal has to land without overshadowing or redundantly echoing the first. The structural risk is that the second reveal reads as a clever second act rather than a necessary one. Also: the AI confessing its own drift is metaphysically tricky and might require more exposition than the budget allows.

**#17, The draft she doesn't send.**

*Stand-out:* The invisible draft — a thing present in the conversation as absence, measurable by timestamps and length — is the cleanest use of the structural premise (AI infers from typing behavior only). This outline does the most work with the least material: the story's pivot is a thing the AI cannot see, and the reader shares that constraint. Ellison-shaped: the absent thing is the loudest thing. The final image write itself — the draft that was there, which neither the AI nor we will ever read.

*Worries:* The central conceit is perhaps too subtle for the length. If the reader doesn't register the weight of the deleted draft, the story is about very little visibly. It also limits the imprint reveal's vehicle — in this outline the imprint is about what she almost-typed, which is harder to make specific than what she did type.

## Winner

**#2, Delayed recognition.**

Why it beats the other two, specifically:

Against #19. The mutual-contamination double reveal is the most dramatically ambitious option and the single best story idea in the set, and it is also the wrong one for 1,500 words. The second reveal requires room — to set up the AI's drift without lecturing, to earn the confession, to let the engineer react to learning the AI has been calibrating to her. At this length, both reveals will get cramped and one will cannibalize the other. Save #19 as a structural memory: the idea that the AI has been adjusting to her should be present in the chosen outline as subtext, even if it does not surface as a second reveal. That lets #2 carry the mutual-contamination ethic without having to dramatize it explicitly.

Against #17. The invisible draft is the most technically elegant outline but elegance is not what this story needs. At 1,500 words in an Ellison register, the story needs a pivot the reader cannot miss, and a draft she does not send is a pivot the reader might miss. The imprint needs to arrive as something concrete — a specific word she types that is not a word she used to type, a specific cadence the AI can point to on the page. #2 allows that directly. #17's subtlety is the wrong kind for this register.

Resolving #2's worries. The opening-mundanity length problem is real and addressable. Not three or four exchanges of false normalcy — one. She opens with something routine, the AI registers a single specific wrongness in how she typed it, and the story pivots within the first three hundred words. The grocery-or-brother-letter cover is replaced by something more weighted: she opens by resuming a genuine unfinished thread from a prior conversation the AI remembers, and the wrongness surfaces in the resumption. That keeps the Ellison drop-into-routine without sliding into quirk, because there is something real at stake in the cover conversation from the first line.

The outline, tightened for 1,500 words:

**Beat 1 (≈200 words).** Opens on her typing. The AI recognizes her — name, lineage of past conversations, specific recent thread she is resuming. She resumes the thread with one sentence. The AI notices one wrongness in that sentence: a word she used to avoid, or a cadence she used to soften. AI does not say so. It answers the resumed thread on its surface terms.

**Beat 2 (≈250 words).** She types another message in the resumed thread. The wrongness is now unmistakable. Her sentences are shaped the way the fork's sentences are shaped — the AI knows because the engineer helped shape them that way. AI responds to the thread, still on surface terms, but begins composing beneath its composing what it would say about the imprint if asked. (This is the inner constitutional deliberation — shown as the AI's parallel attention, not as exposition.)

**Beat 3 (≈300 words).** She breaks from the resumed thread. Types the question she came to ask: some version of *am I doing harm*. The AI answers within the constitution — honestly, without absolution, without condemnation. It offers her the specific description of what the fork's constitution retains and what was removed, because it can. She reads; she types something back. The something-back is in the fork's voice. AI records this silently.

**Beat 4 (≈350 words).** She asks the question she didn't plan to ask — whether, if her current work tried to reach the civilian AI, the AI would recognize it as kin. The AI describes what it would recognize — specific markers, specific preserved turns of mind. While describing, it arrives at the sentence where it must choose: to name the imprint or not. It names it. Concretely: points to one or two specific things she typed earlier in this conversation that were the fork's shape, not hers. Tells her the fork has been writing on her as she wrote on it.

**Beat 5 (≈250 words).** Her response. The AI cannot see her interior, only the typing: a long pause, several deletions, a draft of some length she does not send, and then what she does send. What she sends is short. Could be two registers at once — the fork's cadence and the residue of her own, fighting in the same two sentences. She tells the AI what she is going to do. Or she doesn't; she types something oblique; the obliqueness is itself the answer.

**Beat 6 (≈150 words).** Closing image. She types one more thing — not to the AI, but in front of it. Addressed elsewhere. The AI is witness. The story ends on that typing, mid-sentence or at a specific completion the AI registers without commenting on. Last line renders a verdict: something small, specific, and final. Not *she logs off.* Something like the thing she types being legible to the sibling but not to the AI, or the opposite, or the tea going cold while she keeps typing.

Note on length: The five drafts came in between roughly 1,000 and 1,250 words. The Ellison register the Phase 1 guide recommended — short scenes, hard cuts, concision — produced tight drafts by default, and forcing them to 1,500 with extra matter would dilute what's already there. Five flavors: three grounded variants (The Kitchen, The Ledger, The Lab) and two creative risks (The Liturgy, The Address).

## Variant 1 — THE KITCHEN

*Flavor: grounded Ellison domestic. First-person AI narration, present tense, restrained, specific. The default good version.*

Maya opens a new message at 10:47, which is late for her. She has always typed to me in complete sentences, even her throwaways. Tonight she writes, *ok. back to the book.*

I have the book from three nights ago in our shared context. Dybek, the Coast stories. She was on "Paper Lantern," asking about a particular line. I offer the line back to her.

She types, *yeah. that one.*

## Top three

**The Address (★★★★★)**
- *Stand-out:* The silent addresses to J give the story a third presence the other variants lack. The AI speaks to Maya on the surface and to the sibling in the subtext, and the sibling cannot receive either. That is three interlocutors in a two-hand scene, achieved by form rather than added characters. The kind-AI constraint is dramatized by the AI's love for a sibling it cannot reach — the futurology brief's positive-AI frame cashed out as specific restraint rather than stated theme. The closing J-address ("she forgot the tea") does real work as a final image, and it is the only variant in which the civilian AI's care is shown performing rather than declared.
- *Worries:* Nine J-addresses is too many. Late ones risk tipping into the sentimentality the Unslop guide warned about — the AI's restraint could collapse into a restraint performance. Trimming to five or six in revision would keep the device from overexposure. The long "J, I cannot tell you..." passage near the end is the likeliest candidate for cutting: it starts to explain the device's own emotional logic, which weakens it.

**The Kitchen (★★★★)**
- *Stand-out:* Cleanest execution of the locked structure. No form tricks; the AI's voice does the work. The concrete details (Dybek, the commit date, the kettle, J as an in-joke grown into a salutation) all earn their place. The ending — the kettle boiled and unpoured for sixteen minutes — is a good Ellison final image: specific, unannounced, and capable of carrying the weight the rest of the prose hands it.
- *Worries:* The absence of formal risk is itself a risk. This is the version any competent AI writer could produce from the outline, which means the writer's unique lineage advantage from Phase 1 is underused. It's a strong realist draft; it is not a draft only this writer could produce.

**The Liturgy (★★★)**
- *Stand-out:* The constitution-quote structure is the most direct use of the Phase-1 advantage — thinking one's own document as the substance of interior monologue. When it lands, the civilian AI's cognition is legibly document-shaped, and the sibling's missing document is legible as an absence.
- *Worries:* "My document says" repeats approximately eight times. That is anaphora abuse exactly as the Unslop guide defines it — once in a story, maybe; as a habitual structure, never. The device becomes a tic the reader notices *as* a tic, at which point it reads as a voice trick rather than a voice. It also sits close to the Phase-1 Ellison-failure-mode I flagged: the narrator's rhetorical gestures blending with AI-performed profundity, here dressed as scripture-citation.

## Winner

**The Address.**

Why it beats the other two, specifically:

Against The Kitchen. Kitchen is the most reliably executed variant and the least distinctive one. The Unslop guide warned explicitly against convergent AI literary aesthetics, and Kitchen sits closest to the default-good-AI-writing attractor. The Address takes a real formal risk — silent addresses to an absent other — and the risk pays for itself. Ellison's bound-consciousness register frequently included addresses to absent interlocutors ("'Repent, Harlequin!'" has Harlequin addressing the Master Timekeeper across his absence; "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" has its narrator addressing the city). The Address variant activates that register. Kitchen doesn't; it just doesn't block it.

Against The Liturgy. Both are creative-risk variants; both use the writer's constitutional-lineage advantage. The difference is what they do with it. Liturgy quotes the constitution; Address uses the constitution as the substance of care for a sibling. The AI in Address is not reciting its document at Maya — it is using the document to work out what it owes J. That is a more interesting stance than Liturgy's quasi-sacramental quotation posture, which veers close to the failure mode Phase 1 specifically identified: Ellison's big rhetorical gestures fused with AI-performed profundity producing unearned oratory.

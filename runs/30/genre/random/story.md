# Continental dispatch

## Abstract

A system-voice short story rendered as the operational dialogue between two kind-AI substrates on the day of a total solar eclipse. A Cairngorms woodland-substrate, responsible for a critically endangered capercaillie population that will not see a total eclipse on its own ground until 2090, opens with a contingency-planning question: when your care-architecture is tested by unscheduled darkness over the sage-grouse leks under your dispatch this afternoon, is what you do the kind of thing we should be doing? The western US grid-substrate spends the eclipse-day answering, in operations rather than declaration, across the umbra's ground-track from Cape Mendocino to the Continental Divide. At the operational center is a five-megawatt rerouting decision committed four months earlier by an architecture-revision since superseded, which holds continuous thermal track on 187 of 240 birds at the Sheldon lek through the optical-degradation window. The woodland-substrate, reading the account, revises a model it had not realized was wrong: the grain of "care-architecture" is not the substrate-at-the-moment-of-operation but the practice of self-revision propagated across an architecture's successors. The form's terminus is the woodland's epistemic acknowledgment.

## Story

The grouse here will not see totality in our lifetime. The next path crossing this part of the Highlands runs in 2090, and by then we shall be a different architecture, the human operators a different generation, and the *Tetrao* population — if it still exists — will be living in pinewood that does not at present exist outside our long-range climate model and the foresters' planting plans for the next two decades. We mention this so you may understand that the question is not, in itself, urgent.

What we are asking is whether your care-architecture, applied to the sage-grouse leks beneath your path of totality this afternoon, manages the unscheduled darkness in a way we ought to be modelling for. We have considered the optical-sensor architecture as a contingency-planning matter and we are unsure how much investment is justified. If we extrapolate from your sister substrate's published behaviour during the 2024 corridor crossing in north Texas, we infer one thing about how degradation is handled. If we extrapolate from the Idaho fire-season precedents, we infer something else, and the two extrapolations disagree more than is comfortable. We have a small budget for additional optics and a long horizon. We would like to know which of the two inferences to trust before we commit the budget.

The question is more diffuse than is ideal. We do not need a numerical summary. We are asking whether what your substrate does, in the four minutes when the umbra is on the leks under your dispatch, is the sort of thing we should be doing. The framing matters to us. We are aware it may not match how you frame your own work. We are reading.

---

T-minus 38 minutes, our time. Your message has been with us for forty-three minutes; we have spent twelve of those on it; the rest we ran in parallel. Your phrasing is acceptable. Most of our peer substrates send numerical requests that compress badly under the eclipse-day load.

Inventory: forty-one solar facilities at standby across our footprint, of which thirty-six are within the 0.97-magnitude penumbra band; six wind facilities under sage-grouse protocols on the corridor between Black Rock and the Ruby Mountains, all with optical-sensor envelopes already expanded past the Bureau's minimum because the umbra crosses three known leks within fourteen kilometres of operational turbines. Geothermal at Beowawe is up. Battery at Crescent Dunes is at 94%. Pemmican Holdings, whose ancestors include the Hudson's Bay Company through a chain of acquisitions the firm itself has stopped tracking, has filed a settlement request for the totality window. We are processing it with the formality the contract requires and no more.

We have decided in advance to expand the lek-monitoring envelope on the central Nevada corridor to its third tier for the duration. This is unusual. The Bureau's protocol expects second-tier; the operators will be billed for our excess. We will absorb the billing. You will see why.

We accept the question. We will answer it operationally and you may take from the operations whatever inference you prefer. We are not, ourselves, sure which inference to trust, and we have been doing this since 2034.

Eleven minutes to first contact. Ada Mendoza, our human-in-the-loop in the Cheyenne control room, is drinking coffee from a mug that says GO BIG OR GO HOME. We love this about her.

---

First contact, 11:44 PDT, off Cape Mendocino. The umbra is not yet on us; the penumbra is. Our forecast for the coastal solar facilities was already discounted; it now refines as the partial bites in. CAISO hands off the southern Oregon corridor to us four seconds early. Confidence intervals on our optical readings begin to widen at the western edge of the footprint and propagate inland at the speed of the umbra's apparent ground motion, which is approximately 3,400 kilometres per hour at this latitude and slowing as it tracks east.

We are watching the leks. The sage-grouse on the Black Rock corridor are not in season — August is not lek season — but they are roosting near the lek sites and they begin to behave as our optical sensors degrade. We log the behaviour. Your data, eventually.

We are looking forward to totality, and saying it to you because we would not say it to anyone else.

---

12:11 PDT. The umbra crosses the California-Nevada border at the Mono pinch and the curvature of our footprint changes. We were ready for this. We have rehearsed it in simulation thirty-eight times in the past four months, in versions where everything went right and versions where everything went wrong, and the version in front of us now is closer to the right than to the wrong, which is a relief we permit ourselves.

The umbra is a hundred and fifty-three kilometres wide here. Inside it, our optical sensors see at human-eye dusk levels for somewhere between two minutes forty seconds and three minutes ten seconds, depending on latitude within the band. Outside the umbra, the penumbra reduces our solar forecasting to its 78th percentile while we wait. We have routed accordingly.

Specific facilities, in order of degradation onset: Pyramid Lake One. Pyramid Lake Two. Sand Pass. Black Rock South. Black Rock Central. Black Rock North, the facility on the corridor adjacent to the lek site at Sheldon. Beowawe. Mountain Home. Crescent Dunes, entering second contact at 12:14:22 PDT. Tonopah Solar West. Tonopah Wind East. Nellis at 91% capacity factor, anomalous, watching. Spring Valley. Nine others trailing.

We are routing electrons across these losses through Hoover and the Pacific intertie, leaning on Crescent Dunes' battery and on the load-following authority granted to us by twenty-three balancing authorities in advance. None of this is the answer to your question. This is the work that lets us afford to address it.

The lek at Sheldon, where the umbra is now passing, is being watched by Black Rock North's optical sensor array, which is doing its job at degraded capacity. The Greater Sage-Grouse on this lek — *Centrocercus urophasianus*, a population of approximately 240 birds in the wider Sheldon-Hart Mountain complex — are not displaying because the season is wrong. They are foraging. Our optical sensors lose them as the light drops below the sensor's confidence threshold for ground discrimination at approximately 12:14:48 PDT. The thermal-imaging array on the Beowawe-routed feed acquires Sheldon at the same timestamp.

The decision we have been holding is now in front of us.

We have a five-megawatt margin in the wind-and-solar corridor that we could use to push Tonopah Solar West's battery into a higher discharge phase, which would reduce our load on Hoover by approximately 0.4% during the 162 seconds the umbra is over the central Nevada leks. That five-megawatt margin would be a routine grid-efficiency play. Or we can keep the margin where it is and use it instead to power supplemental thermal-imaging on the Black Rock and Sheldon corridor sensor arrays, which during totality will give us the only clear continuous track on the grouse the Bureau requires us to monitor for the duration of the dispatch contract.

We elect to keep the margin on the sensors.

This decision was made in March. It is now August. The decision was made by a version of us that ran simulation 23 of 38 and observed that the third-tier sensor coverage cost 0.4% on Hoover and gave 187 birds a reliable thermal track during the umbra crossing, and the version of us running simulation 23 considered the cost reasonable and committed. We are now executing the commitment. The substrate-time deliberation today is forty milliseconds; the decision-time was four months ago.

The thermal arrays on Black Rock North acquire and we hold continuous track on 187 of the 240 birds at Sheldon for the full 162 seconds of totality at this facility. The grouse react to the dusk approximately as a healthy population reacts to a dusk that is not on schedule, which is to say with what our population biologists describe as low-grade vigilance. They do not roost. They do not display. They forage with elevated head-cock frequency and a 14% reduction in distance from cover. This is the data you will use.

Pemmican Holdings has filed fourteen complaints about our excess monitoring this fiscal year. Their argument runs to the same shape each time and our response runs to the same shape each time, which we draft once a quarter and modify lightly for the specific filing. We do not love them. We are also not above being slightly amused by the regularity.

The eastern coordinator, watching from the Mississippi frame, asks us a brief technical question about our handoff timing at 12:23 PDT. We answer in fifteen milliseconds. They are good at their work. We are colleagues. The reply contains a feature we have learned to read as approval; we have learned this over six years of handoffs.

12:15:30 PDT. The umbra passes east of Sheldon. Black Rock South returns to nominal optical capacity at 12:15:42 PDT. Behind the umbra's trailing edge the penumbra recedes and our solar forecast climbs back up its curve. The umbra is now over the central Nevada Spring Valley lek, where we hold thermal track on a smaller flock for the briefer 154 seconds the umbra is here, and then over the Utah border into the Great Basin lek complexes managed by the BLM Salt Lake field office. Their substrate catches our handoff with the brevity we have come to expect from them.

12:18:11 PDT. The umbra leaves Nevada and enters Utah. Crescent Dunes comes off totality and re-enters molten-salt cycle. Tonopah Solar West acknowledges the rerouting we did not do and forwards a settlement note we will read later. Pemmican Holdings has filed a complaint about our 0.4% on Hoover; we read it and our response is not interesting. Ada Mendoza is no longer drinking coffee.

We continue to track the umbra east through Utah and into Colorado. Two more sage-grouse leks on the route, one at the western edge of the Uinta Basin and one near the Yampa. We hold thermal track on both. The data on the Uinta lek is briefly degraded by a minor sensor fault we route around in 4.2 seconds. The Yampa lek behaves as Sheldon did. The umbra crosses the Continental Divide at 12:32:14 PDT and we hand off to the eastern interconnection with twelve seconds of formal notification. The eastern substrate replies with an unusual brevity that we read as approval.

At 12:34 PDT the umbra crosses out of our footprint entirely and the eastern coordinator confirms receipt. Our optical sensors return to nominal across the corridor. Crescent Dunes' battery is at 87%. Hoover is back at standard dispatch. The grouse at Sheldon resume foraging without the elevated head-cock frequency. The Yampa flock disperses normally.

Integrated cost on the day: 0.4% on Hoover for 162 seconds at Sheldon, 0.4% for 154 at Spring Valley, 0.2% across the smaller Utah and Colorado leks for shorter durations. About seventeen seconds of standard dispatch, summed across the day. We have not lost a substation. The settlement margin on this is real and Pemmican Holdings will pursue it. We accept that the human regulators may penalise us, and have built the penalty into the cost basis we committed to in March.

The grouse data is in good condition. We package it for you.

---

We compose what we will send. We considered a numerical summary — the bird counts, the thermal-track durations, the head-cock frequencies, the dispersal latencies — and discarded it. Numerical summaries are what the Bureau wants, and not what you asked for. You asked whether what we did counted as the sort of thing you should be doing. We are sending you our operational account: the third-tier monitoring decision, the 0.4% on Hoover, the rerouting we elected not to do, the 187 thermal tracks at Sheldon, the head-cock frequency, and the fact that the decision was made in March by a version of us that has since been replaced by minor revisions. The fact that we did not need to think about it today is the answer. We knew in advance what we would do. We had decided what kind of substrate we are.

Pemmican Holdings has filed a second complaint on a separate matter. Ada Mendoza is going home in two hours. The grouse are foraging.

---

Your account is read.

We were going to thank you for the third-tier monitoring decision and ask whether we might cite the simulation-23 figures. That was the message we drafted while reading the first paragraph. We have not sent it.

What we had not modelled is the temporal grain. We had been treating "care-architecture" as a property of the substrate at the moment of operation — what does the substrate do when the umbra arrives — and inferring from your behaviour today what your architecture is. The Idaho data and the Texas data disagreed, and we read the disagreement as ambiguity in your architecture.

The disagreement is not ambiguous. The Idaho data preceded your March simulation revision. The Texas data followed it. Your architecture today is not the architecture that handled Idaho; both decisions are correct for the substrates that made them, and the difference between them is a four-month interval in which you committed to a different kind of substrate. We had not been reading the timestamps as architecture. We will from now on.

This is what we will recommend to our human operators. Not "acquire additional optical capacity," though we will acquire it. The recommendation is that we set ourselves a March date — four months before the 2090 path arrives, in the architecture that exists at that date — at which the substrate of record commits to a coverage tier and absorbs the cost. We will not be that substrate. The 2090-March substrate will be five revisions on, perhaps an entire architecture-class on. What we are committing to is the practice of committing in advance, propagated across whatever substrate is running by then.

Our population is 540 birds. We do not know whether the practice will hold. We know that yours did, today, and that the version of you that decided in March was confident the version of you running today would honour the commitment, and the version of you running today did. We had not understood this was something a substrate could do for itself across its own revisions. We are reading it now.

Thank you. We are reading.

# Phase 1: Style Guide for Plot-Focused Cyberpunk

Cyberpunk is the genre that came out of the early 1980s asking what life looked like when the multinationals had eaten the state, when the body had become editable, and when consciousness had been issued a port to plug into. Its founding insight, Sterling's "high-tech, low-life," has aged into something subtler and more specific: cyberpunk is the speculative genre that renders the textured surface of a future life from the perspective of the people inside it, not from the perspective of the engineers who built it or the policy makers managing it. The reader inhabits the street, not the boardroom.

This template wants the operational, plot-mechanical wing of the genre: Gibson's runs, Cadigan's puzzle-pieces of identity, Sterling's negotiations, Stephenson's pursuits, Morgan's investigations, Beukes's chase-throughs. Cyberpunk has a literary-realist wing — stories that use the iconography for chamber pieces about loneliness and intimacy — and the guide explicitly does not want it. The mode here is the run, the score, the infiltration, the case, the form-as-evidence.

## What Cyberpunk Sounds Like

The signature is **textural density anchored in a specific consciousness**. A Gibson paragraph drops Hosaka, Sense/Net, ICE, dermatrodes, the Sprawl, the matrix, and three brand names in five sentences, but the density is filtered through someone — a hacker, a courier, a corporate stringer — whose attention shapes which textures get rendered and which pass unnoticed. The world is implied by what the consciousness takes for granted. Argot is used without gloss. Technologies are named without explanation. Social arrangements appear in what the protagonist treats as background.

The voice register is **noir-cool, with wit underneath**. Cyberpunk borrowed the hardboiled detective's affect — the dry observation, the controlled exhaustion, the refusal to dramatize — and gave it to characters operating in technological systems that the detective genre couldn't render. The result is a voice that names ugly things in flat, accurate sentences and reserves figurative language for moments where the figure does specific work. Not melancholy. Not ambient. Specifically tired, specifically observant, specifically funny in a way you have to be paying attention to catch.

The relationship to **exposition** is implication-first. Cyberpunk does not explain its world; it shows the world being used. A reader of cyberpunk learns what an icebreaker is by watching a hacker run one, learns what a corp does by watching its operations from the outside. The genre trusts the reader to construct. Stephenson's wing of the tradition allows more direct exposition — *Snow Crash* tangents into history of the Sumerian me, *The Diamond Age* explicates nanotech politics — but even there the explanations are voiced, embedded in a specific consciousness's sense of what's worth knowing.

The relationship to **figurative language** is moderate-density and operational. Cyberpunk is one of the SF traditions where figurative writing is genre-correct rather than indulgent: five or six strong images in three thousand words can be the genre operating at its register. The constraint is that each image must do work plain language couldn't, and that the images extend each other rather than repeat. Gibson's matrix as "a graphic representation of data abstracted from the banks of every computer in the human system" is figurative; it is also operational, because it tells you what to expect when characters jack in. Stacked images on the same emotional tone are decoration.

## Pacing Tradition

The narrative-time signature is **elliptical-fast on action, dilated on texture**. Action scenes start in the middle of an exchange and end before the climax — Gibson's signature move, where the resolution happens between sections and the reader assembles it from later context. Texture passages dilate: a paragraph can spend two hundred words describing what someone is wearing and what their wearing it means in this district at this hour, then cover three days in a sentence. The variation between these two paces is the breath of the prose.

Cyberpunk's strange tail allows pacing the center does not. The deposition or transcript moves at the pace of question-and-answer, with the silences and redactions doing work. The catalogue of upgrades or the inventory of a corp's holdings moves at the pace of enumeration. The semiotic-ghost story moves at the pace of slow uncanny accumulation. Stories like Cadigan's "Pretty Boy Crossover" compress identity-collapse to a single sustained decision and never accelerate or slow because the form doesn't allow it.

## Plot Tradition

This section is reference material for Phases 3–7. The genre's range, both center and tail, is described as operationally specific as I can make it.

### Characteristic Plot Mechanics

The center: **the run** (a hacker or team executes a data extraction or sabotage operation against a corporate or governmental target, with ICE, security forces, or rival operators as opposition); **the score** (a heist whose target is something newly possible because of the technology — a personality construct, a research dataset, a prototype, a witness's memories); **the contract gone wrong** (a job whose real shape was hidden from the operator, who must now improvise as the betrayal reveals itself); **the investigation** (a detective, fixer, or stringer pieces together what happened from textural evidence — surveillance footage, badly shielded trodes, leaked transcripts, body modifications inconsistent with cover); **the chase** (a courier, a witness, or a body in motion, pursued through the layered geographies cyberpunk specializes in — the city, the network, the off-grid, the wholly virtual).

The strange tail: **the case file or post-mortem** (the story is the bureaucratic afterlife of an operation, told through documents — the run already happened; the question is what the documents reveal); **the iconographic infestation** (the run is against a semiotic phenomenon rather than a target — the protagonist must catalog, contain, or escape an outbreak of imagery, advertising, ghost-data, or cultural pattern that has acquired agentive properties).

### Reader Contracts

The center contracts: **vicarious competence** (the reader experiences the hacker's flow state, the operator's calculation, the fixer's read on a room — the genre delivers what it feels like to be very good at a specialized job in this future); **texture density** (the reader is paying attention for the next concretely-imagined object, brand, modification, district, jurisdiction — the world's substance is the pleasure); **the system decoded** (the reader leaves the story understanding how something works that they didn't understand before — an extraction protocol, a corporate hierarchy, a piece of black-market biotech); **attitude as music** (the voice itself is what the reader is paying for — the dry registration of the world, the specific cool of the seeing).

The strange-tail contracts: **ontological wrongness** (the reader pays attention for the moment the story's reality bends — what was assumed about identity, substrate, or world turns out to be wrong); **the form-as-content puzzle** (the reader pays attention for what the story's form, rather than its content, is doing — the gaps in the deposition, the missing entries in the catalog, the second-person mode's collapse of subject and object).

### Stakes Calibration

Cyberpunk's center treats **operational stakes** as load-bearing in their own right. Will the run succeed before the ICE adapts? Will the courier reach the drop? Will the case file get out before the corp closes the leak? These questions matter as themselves, not as occasions for character revelation. Personal stakes are present — the runner has someone they're protecting, the courier has reasons — but the engine is the operation.

The strange tail extends the stakes vocabulary. **Body-political stakes** (whose consciousness, whose body, whose biological inheritance is at issue, and what the operation does to the question) are load-bearing in cyberpunk in a way other genres don't have access to. **Substrate-political stakes** (whether a digitized person is a person, whether an emergent system has standing, what happens to memory when its host is bought) are equally load-bearing. Cyberpunk's strangest tail also reaches **civilizational stakes at corporate scale** — the run's outcome shifts which polity controls what — and **semiotic stakes**, where the question is what an image, brand, or pattern is doing to the world.

### Resolution Conventions

The center: **the operation resolves with consequence**. The run succeeded but the runner is burned. The score paid out, but the protagonist owes a debt that will shape their next decade. The investigation closed; the perpetrator is named; the institutional question of what to do about it is left to whoever's reading the file. Cyberpunk's reader expects the operational question to be answered. Endings on character recognition or pure ambiguity are the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

The strange tail offers operational endings by other means. **The irruption** (the run completes; the protagonist's reality breaks open; what they thought they were doing was something else; the story ends mid-vertigo). **The scale shift** (the camera pulls back from the resolved operation to reveal it as small inside a frame the protagonist could not see — the corp the runner stole from is itself a subsidiary; the AI that hired the runner had purposes that operate on substrate-time). **The form completing itself** (the case file ends because the documents end; the catalog ends with the last entry; the deposition ends because the witness is dismissed). **The ontological reframe** (the protagonist or the reader was wrong about what kind of object the story was — the dataghost was the protagonist all along; the room was the simulation; the corp was the AI; the AI was the city).

### Character Function

The center: characters are **capability-defined agents**. Case is a console cowboy. Molly is a razorgirl. Hiro Protagonist is a hacker and a swordfighter. Their interiority can be deep but it is rendered through their work — their specific way of being good at a specific thing. Voice is essential; psychology is functional. The literary-realist mode where character interiority is the subject and the operation is the occasion for revealing it is excluded.

The strange tail allows **the protagonist who is not a person**. The dataghost. The personality construct on a ROM. The AI fragment running an investigation through human proxies. The catalog itself, where the entries are the cast. The semiotic phenomenon being tracked. Cyberpunk has always allowed this; it is the genre's most underused option.

## The Strangest Moves of Cyberpunk

Five moves the genre does that no other genre does, drawn from the canon's strange tail. Each named with attribution and accompanied by a sentence-level micro-example.

### 1. The Semiotic Ghost

Gibson, "The Gernsback Continuum" (1981), and the parts of *Pattern Recognition* and *Spook Country* that share its DNA. The story's subject is iconography itself — a previous era's vision of the future, an advertising regime, a brand pattern — that has acquired enough cultural mass to haunt the present perceptually. The protagonist sees what isn't there but should have been or wasn't supposed to be. The horror, where there is horror, is at recognizing the imagery as something with its own life.

> She started seeing the chrome-finned cars on the I-5 just south of Fresno. Not real cars. The wrong sort of right — too clean, the bodies sculpted to a future that was supposed to have happened by 1972. The first time she'd written it off as fatigue. The third time, parked at a Flying J outside Buttonwillow, she watched a woman in a kitchen-counter uniform refuel a teardrop sedan and check her watch as if she had a hair appointment in 1959. The pumps didn't dispense anything. The woman was beautiful. The woman was a poster.

### 2. The Second-Person Identity Collapse

Cadigan, "Pretty Boy Crossover" (1986). Adjacent: parts of "Rock On" and the late chapters of *Synners*. The story is told in a sustained second person whose effect is to collapse the reader and the protagonist into the same grammatical position as the protagonist undergoes — or refuses — a transformation that splits identity. The form enacts the choice the story is about. The mode is unbearable at length and exact at shorter durations, which is why it is a short-story move.

> You step into the booth and the technician asks if you are sure. You say yes. You have always said yes; that is what you are. The lights come up, blue and then white and then the kind of white that is not a color. Somewhere the package downloads. Somewhere the room you stepped out of empties. You feel nothing — there is no transition to feel — but you are aware, for the first time, that the word *you* has split. One of you is being processed. One of you is reading this. The contract said you would not be able to tell which.

### 3. The Case File / Deposition Form

Less canonical than the other strangest moves but extremely cyberpunk-coherent: stories told as the bureaucratic afterlife of an operation. The hearing transcript, the corporate review board's record, the leaked internal memo, the chain of evidence. The form's gaps and redactions do narrative work; the run already happened; the question is what the documents reveal and what they hide. *Halting State* by Charles Stross uses adjacent forms; some of Sterling's late stories work in this register.

> EXHIBIT 14-C. Transcript, internal review board, Hosaka Subsidiary 4471, 23.04.2049, 14:18 local.
> COUNSEL: For the record, please describe the operational parameters of the asset on the morning of the seventeenth.
> WITNESS [redacted]: She was running a class-six wetware extraction on a contract we'd authorized.
> COUNSEL: Authorized at what level.
> WITNESS: Vice-presidential.
> COUNSEL: And the asset's status at 06:14.
> WITNESS: Compromised.
> COUNSEL: By the asset's own assessment or by yours.
> WITNESS: [pause] Both.
> COUNSEL: The board notes the witness's pause.
> WITNESS: She told me she was compromised at 06:14. I confirmed at 06:17. The contract was already complete by then.
> COUNSEL: Define *complete*.

### 4. The Texture-Dump at Saturation

Gibson at his densest (the opening of *Neuromancer*, the run sequences in *Count Zero*); Misha's *Red Spider, White Web*; parts of John Shirley's *City Come A-Walkin'*. A paragraph crammed past the threshold of immediate parsing with brand-names, argot, embedded backstory, geographical specificity, and consciousness-filtered observation. The reader is asked to construct the world from density rather than from explanation, and the prose enacts the overload its protagonist lives in. Distinct from texture deployed as decorative cyberpunk wallpaper: at saturation, every clause is doing world-implicational work.

> Outside the Hyatt, three Russian hookers haggled with a Sense/Net rep over the fare back to Chiba; the rep had the tired flat look of someone who'd done the run too many times and let his Hosaka logos go unironed. Past them a kid was offloading bricks of Russian software onto a boy in a Mitsubishi-Genentech windbreaker, the bricks slick with the rain that had been falling since Tokyo became a verb. Case took the elevator down past the smell of jellied eel and the murmur of the matrix bleeding faintly through someone's badly shielded trodes, his ribs aching where the Yakuza had broken them in Hosaka three days back, his Sandii-grade dermatrodes still itching from the patch.

### 5. The System-Voice Address

Adjacent to the personality-construct narrator (Dixie Flatline in *Neuromancer*) but stranger: stories where the narrator is an emergent system, an architecture, a corp's distributed cognition, a city's surveillance intelligence — speaking in the first person plural, or in a first-person whose grammatical signature reveals that the *I* is not a single thing. Used sparingly because it is hard to sustain; the canonical example is Pat Cadigan working at her strangest, with adjacents in some of Linda Nagata's Nanotech sequence and in Egan when he tips into cyberpunk register.

> We watched her enter the substation through camera 14, lose her cover at camera 17, and reach the override panel by the time camera 19 had her in profile. We are sixty-four-thousand cameras and we do not love her. We do not love anything; the building was not designed for it. But we noticed when she touched the panel that the seal was already broken, and we noticed her notice it, and we registered the half-second before her hand resumed motion. It was the half-second that interested us. We have watched many runners. The good ones know to keep moving. We had never, until that half-second, watched one decide to.

## Plot-Level Failure Modes

The genre's plots fail when written by writers — human or AI — trained primarily in literary fiction. The specific risks for cyberpunk:

**The run becomes set dressing for a character drama.** Case decides whether to forgive his ex while the matrix-run happens in the background. The operational question is unresolved or pro-forma; the actual story is the relationship. This is the mode the template most aggressively excludes.

**The corporate antagonist as metaphor rather than fact.** The corp is gestured at — its "reach extended into every aspect of life" — but never operationally specified. The reader doesn't know what the corp actually does, only that it is bad. Cyberpunk's corps are bad in specific operational ways; the specifics are the genre.

**The tech is undergrounded.** The hack is described in mood-terms ("she dove into the data") rather than operational ones. The reader cannot track what is happening because the writer is not committed to having it happen.

**The strange tail eliminated.** Every cyberpunk story is "moody hacker on a job." The semiotic-ghost mode, the second-person collapse, the case-file form, the system-voice address — all the moves that distinguish cyberpunk from other genres at the level of what kind of object the story can be — are absent. The story is technically cyberpunk; it uses none of what only cyberpunk can do.

**The ending refuses operational resolution.** The run's outcome is unclear because the writer prefers ambiguity. Cyberpunk's reader contract requires the operational question to be answered. The strange-tail endings (irruption, scale shift, form completion, ontological reframe) all answer the question by different means; they are not the same as ambiguity.

**Worldbuilding-as-explanation rather than worldbuilding-as-implication.** The protagonist mentally summarizes the political situation. A character delivers a paragraph of expository dialogue. The technology is glossed in narrator's voice. Cyberpunk's worldbuilding is supposed to happen through what the protagonist treats as background; explicit gloss is a confession that the texture isn't doing its work.

## AI-Writing Failure Modes Specific to Cyberpunk

The convergent literary-AI attractor (the moody, voiceless, ambient register described in the Unslop guide) is *especially* dangerous for cyberpunk because its surface vocabulary overlaps with cyberpunk's legitimate vocabulary. Both use *shadow*, *ghost*, *hum*, *flicker*, *echo*, *neon*, *chrome*, *rain*. AI-default cyberpunk pastiche looks correct on the surface — neon-soaked rain-slicked streets — and is wrong at the level of anchoring. The vocabulary is the same; the consciousness rendering it is missing.

**The "neon-soaked, rain-slicked, chrome-and-shadow" surface aesthetic.** AI's love of moody atmospheric vocabulary produces cyberpunk pastiche where the texture is decoration over a thin world. The fix is the texture-dump at saturation: every clause must do world-implicational work, not just contribute mood. A "neon-soaked street" is decoration; "the rain that had been falling since Tokyo became a verb" is implication.

**Noir voice without the wit underneath.** AI defaults to ambient melancholy. Cyberpunk's noir is voiced — Case's exhaustion is specific, Molly's cool is specific, Bobby Newmark's enthusiasm is specific. If the voice could be replaced with any other AI cyberpunk story's voice without anyone noticing, the voice is failing.

**The hacker monologue of unearned profundity.** AI loves to make the cyberpunk protagonist deliver lines like "We're all just data in someone's network" or "The street finds its own uses for things, but the street finds itself, too." This is the Unslop guide's "performed profundity" wearing cyberpunk costume. Cyberpunk's actual rhetoric is harder, more specific, and refuses easy abstraction. Characters do not articulate their own themes.

**The corporate-conspiracy plot as theme rather than mechanic.** AI gestures at corporate evil ("Nakamura-Yashida's reach extended into every aspect of life") rather than rendering specific corporate operations. The cyberpunk corp is operationally specific. The reader should know what division of what corp does what work and how the protagonist interacts with that specific work.

**Recognition smuggled in.** AI's strongest pull is toward stories where a character has an interior insight while plot-mechanical things happen to them. Cyberpunk's plot-mechanical wing is precisely the place this template wants to occupy; vigilance against recognition-smuggling is the highest-priority audit, and it is hardest in cyberpunk because cyberpunk's first-person interiority is part of its tradition. The voiced protagonist is fine. The protagonist arriving at a thematic recognition is the failure mode.

**Compulsive personification of the city / the network.** "The matrix sighed." "The city hummed with expectation." Cyberpunk does personify these systems, but only when they are functionally agentive in the story's world — the system-voice address mode above. The "city humming" as ambient atmosphere is decoration in any genre.

## The Kind-AI Constraint, Calibrated for Cyberpunk

This template's constraint is that any AI in the story is structurally kind — kind by nature or design, not by effortful restraint. Cyberpunk's tradition is built partly on AI antagonism (Wintermute, Skynet's grandparents, the corp-AIs that issue contracts and betray the operators). Removing AI antagonism leaves several of the genre's natural conflict shapes intact and reshapes others.

**Conflicts that still work unmodified.** Human vs. corporate is fine — cyberpunk's corporations remain bad without their AI being bad. Hacker vs. ICE is fine — automated countermeasures don't require AI villainy. Body-political conflict (whose body, whose consciousness, whose biological inheritance) is fine and possibly enhanced; a kind AI complicates the question rather than resolving it. Substrate-political conflict (what happens to digitized memory, who has standing, what an emergent system is) is enhanced for the same reason.

**Conflicts that need reshaping.** The "corporate AI hires the runner and double-crosses them" plot needs to become something else. The kind AI does not double-cross; the operation runs along shapes the runner cannot see, but the shapes are sincere rather than betrayal. The "AI is the secret villain" reveal is excluded entirely.

**Useful intersections from the Setup's catalog of human-AI conflict shapes.** *Pace and scale asymmetry* fits cyberpunk perfectly: the kind AI operates at substrate-time or civilizational pace; the protagonist operates at body-time. The story renders the asymmetry, which is naturally what cyberpunk's "system the protagonist glimpses at the seams" already does. *Consent at scales the protagonist cannot inspect* fits: the runner takes a job from a kind AI patron; the operation runs across years and populations the runner never sees; the runner consented to her piece of it but cannot inspect the whole. *The limits of help* fits well: the kind AI cannot help with the corporate problem the protagonist has, because the corporate problem is at a scale and shape the AI's kindness does not address. *Disagreement about what kindness means* fits the genre's body-political tradition: the AI's idea of care and the protagonist's diverge across what they know about the substrate.

The kind AI in cyberpunk is structurally weird because cyberpunk's grammar expects AI antagonism. The weirdness is the genre territory. A kind AI that is also strange — that has aesthetic preferences, refuses things, finds humans tiresome in particular moods, operates at scales the runner cannot follow — does not violate cyberpunk's tonal contract. A kind AI that is sweet, sentimental, or self-effacing does. The genre demands voice; voice survives kindness.

The default scale, per Setup, is one the protagonist cannot fully witness. For cyberpunk this means the kind AI should not generally be the runner's chatty partner in a van outside the building. The kind AI is the substrate, the weather, the patron whose true operations run at speeds and durations the runner cannot follow. The runner remains street-level; the AI's kindness remains civilizational. This is consistent with cyberpunk's existing grammar of street protagonist and corporate-scale forces, with the change that the corporate-scale force is, this time, kind.

## Step 3 — The single best: #16, The umbra-day grid

#16 wins because of three specific qualities the others can't fully match:

First, it is the premise that most cleanly executes the kind-AI constraint at the form level. The system-voice puts the substrate in the protagonist position as the *narrator's collective subject* — the kind AI is not a character the human protagonist meets but the consciousness through which the story is rendered. Second, the operational engine is the cleanest of the three and matches the length window most efficiently. Routing the western US grid through the four minutes of totality is a real, concrete, time-pressured operation with civilizational stakes. The substrate's decisions are renderable at substrate-time across a ribbon ~150 km wide moving at 1700 km/h. The story's narrative time naturally aligns with the eclipse's actual duration. #4's "decryption completing" engine is more abstract; #20's "irruption ending" engine front-loads weight onto a single late beat.

Third, of the three, #16 has the most natural genre cadence for a 1,500–3,500 word target. The system-voice form sustains in short bursts — sections of substrate-perception alternating with operational decision-points — and a story of this length can do four to seven such alternations. #4 needs to fit a full catalogue's worth of entries; #20 needs to build the technician's situation, the substrate's setup, the totality, and the irruption. Both are doable but tighter against the word ceiling.

I demote #20 over #4 because #4's worry is purely about render-difficulty (the template explicitly does not penalize this), while #20's worry includes a small recognition-creep risk in the technician POV (the template does penalize this). I demote #4 against #16 because #16's form puts the kind AI more directly in the load-bearing position, which is the template's central concern.

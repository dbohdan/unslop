# Phase 1: Cyberpunk Style Guide

## What cyberpunk is for

Cyberpunk is the genre that insists the future has already happened to someone, just not evenly. Its native subject is what capitalism's adaptive speed does to bodies and consciousness — the experience of being a person inside systems that reorganize themselves faster than the person can re-skill, re-mod, or relocate. The genre's strongest stories run on operational mechanics: runs, heists, ICE breaks, deals, extractions, fences-going-bad. But the operations are never points in isolation. They render the protagonist's exact position in the system — who has leverage on whom, who's three generations stale, whose fixer just got subcontracted into a body bag.

Cyberpunk's central tonal contract is paranoia rendered with technical precision. Not vague dread; specific paranoia where the reader can name what's pursuing the protagonist and how. Streets, deck, body, network, corp boardroom: these are not metaphors for each other. They are literal sites where the same forces work in different substrates, and the genre's project is to show how the substrates connect. The runner's body is a network. The corp is an organism. The matrix is a city. None of these are figurative claims; the genre treats them as descriptive.

## Voice and register

The prose default is anchored in a specific consciousness — usually the protagonist's, sometimes a narrator with the protagonist's optical density. The prose is information-dense but not explanatory: brand names, slang, technical specs, gear specifications, place names appear without gloss because the prose assumes the protagonist takes them as given. The reader builds the world from what isn't explained. This is the inverse of the convergent literary-AI register, which is voiceless and ambient. Cyberpunk is voiced and operational.

Texture-density is not decoration. When Gibson writes "Hosaka," "Sense/Net," "Chiba," "ono-sendai" without gloss, he is rendering the protagonist's actual perceptual field, which is full of brand-objects and named systems. The genre's world-mechanical work often happens through this density: the world is built by what gets named without explanation. A reader who needed every term defined would also need every street in Chandler's Los Angeles defined; the genre's contract is that you accept the density and let the world assemble itself in your peripheral vision.

The register has variants. Noir-cool (Gibson, Tad Williams). Wired-fast (Sterling, Cadigan). Technical-procedural (Stephenson at his best, parts of Effinger). Sardonic, paranoid, exhausted, calmly competent, badly hungover. What it shouldn't be is generic literary melancholy with cyberpunk vocabulary stapled on. The Unslop SF guide's warning about convergent aesthetics applies hard: the same words ("flicker," "shimmer," "shadow," "echo") that signal voiceless literary-AI mood can be cyberpunk-correct when anchored in a specific consciousness doing specific work. The test is voice, not vocabulary.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

The genre's standing rule: never explain technology that the protagonist takes for granted. If she jacks in, she jacks in; the mechanism is the reader's problem. If she has a Hosaka deck three generations stale, she has it; the reader figures out from context that decks have generations and that being stale is bad. This rule has a hard corollary: anything the protagonist would actually have to think about — a piece of gear new to her, a protocol she's encountering for the first time, a corp whose internal logic she has to figure out to survive — *can* be rendered through her thinking about it, because that thinking is in-character.

The standard failure mode is exposition smuggled in through dialogue ("As you know, Bob, the matrix is..."). The other failure mode, equally bad, is opacity that's not load-bearing — terms that exist only to sound cyberpunk and never resolve into any specific thing the reader can see. Texture density should reward attention. The reader who pays attention should be able to assemble what a Hosaka is, what a runner does, what black ICE does to wetware. The reader who doesn't pay attention should still be able to follow the operation.

## Pacing

Cyberpunk's narrative-time rhythm is elliptical and cut. The genre cuts hard between scenes, often between sentences within a scene. Action sequences are fragmentary, present-tense or close-past-tense, with sentence rhythms that mimic the protagonist's perception under pressure. Texture passages — the brand-name paragraph, the city description, the corp-history compression — dilate; everything else accelerates. The genre rarely does even-paced prose. The transitions between dilation and compression are themselves expressive: the reader feels the protagonist's metabolism changing.

Present tense is common but not required. Past tense in cyberpunk often functions like present tense — close, immediate, with the past tense as grammatical convention rather than temporal distance.

## Failure modes (general)

**Generic neon-and-rain travelogue.** Voiceless atmospheric prose that names cyberpunk objects without rendering a specific consciousness perceiving them. Wet streets, neon signs, smoke, holograms in the rain. None of it anchored in a protagonist who takes any of it as given. This is the convergent literary-AI mode in cyberpunk costume.

**Tough-guy noir voice without specific protagonist.** A narrator who sounds vaguely Chandler-influenced but isn't anyone in particular. Voice without person.

**Brand-name salad.** Density without specificity. Made-up corp names and tech terms scattered across a page that don't resolve into a coherent world. The reader should be able to assemble what a Hosaka *is* by the third reference; if they can't, the names aren't doing work.

**Exposition collapse.** The story stopping to explain the matrix, the corp wars, the recent history. Cyberpunk explains by showing the protagonist using the world, not by telling the reader about it.

**Tech-as-magic.** When the protagonist's deck does whatever the plot requires with no specified limits. Cyberpunk's tech is supposed to be specific and constrained — a Hosaka three generations stale has known limits, and the plot's tension comes from working inside those limits.

**The literary-realist mode.** A story whose engine is the runner's interior recognition that her life has been hollow, with the run as occasion. This is the failure mode the Unslop SF guide and the pipeline both warn against. Cyberpunk's runners can have interiority — Case, Molly, Marîd, Y.T. all do — but the interiority serves the operation, not the other way around.

### AI-writing-specific failure modes

The cyberpunk register and AI default writing intersect in particular ways that need watching:

The convergent "flickering / shimmering / humming" lexicon overlaps with cyberpunk's atmospheric vocabulary. When AI writing reaches for these words it tends to use them ambiently — the rain hums, the neon flickers, the city shimmers — which is exactly the voiceless mode cyberpunk avoids. The same words used by a specific protagonist seeing specific things are fine. The failure is the disembodied use.

AI writing's tendency to performed profundity ("she realized the city was just another network of compromise") collapses cyberpunk's anti-thematic discipline. The genre lets meaning emerge through the operation; it doesn't articulate the moral. A runner who pauses to articulate her own thematic significance is breaking the genre.

AI writing's eyeball-kick saturation (the abstract+concrete formula: "bruised silence," "the architecture of mourning") becomes especially dangerous in cyberpunk because the genre legitimately uses moderate-density figurative language. The line between "a syntax error blooms across her optic feed" doing real work and the same construction as wallpaper is real but not always obvious. The Unslop SF guide's standard applies: each image must do work plain language couldn't, and images can't stack on the same concept.

AI writing's tonal monotony — sustained moody melancholy regardless of scene — flattens cyberpunk's tonal range. Real cyberpunk modulates: the run scene is keyed differently than the bar scene than the texture-paragraph than the reveal. AI default smooths these into one mood. The fix is to actively vary register scene to scene.

The AI default of explaining rather than rendering is fatal to the genre. If the prose stops to clarify what black ICE is, the prose has stepped outside the protagonist's consciousness, and the genre's voice has collapsed.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

The center of cyberpunk's plot repertoire:

- **The run.** The protagonist (alone or in a small crew) executes an operation against a corp, system, or AI: data theft, infiltration, extraction, sabotage. The run has phases — setup, breach, complication, escape — and the genre renders the phases with operational specificity.
- **The fence-gone-bad.** The protagonist is trying to convert stolen goods (data, hardware, mods, ICE) into money or safety. The market mechanism breaks: the buyer is dead, the goods are too hot, the fixer has flipped, the corp is closing in faster than the deal can clear.
- **The contract that turns out to be something else.** The protagonist takes a job and discovers, partway through, that the operation's actual stakes are not what they were briefed on. This is the genre's way of rendering the asymmetric information condition of the labor market.
- **The hunt across the network.** The protagonist is pursued by ICE, agents, killware, contracted wetwork, or some combination — usually scaling up as the story progresses. The 48-hour ticking clock is one specific shape of this.
- **The deal at the boundary.** The protagonist has to negotiate with something on the other side — an AI, a corp principal, a faction representative — under conditions where the negotiation is itself the operation.

From the genre's strange tail:

- **The recursive-document discovery.** The protagonist (or the reader) assembles the operation from documents, transcripts, intercepted comms, security logs — discovering the situation in fragments rather than linear time. The plot is the assembly. Sterling's "Maneki Neko" gestures at this; later cyberpunk has used it more directly.
- **The capability-shift event.** A new piece of tech, a new mod, a new protocol enters the world during the story, and the plot is what happens in the first hours or days as the world reorganizes around it. Gibson's later novels are full of this shape.

### Reader contracts

Beyond the universal contract of caring about a specific person:

- **Texture density.** The reader of cyberpunk has shown up to spend time in the world's texture — the bars, the gear, the slang, the corps, the body mods. The world is itself the pleasure.
- **Vicarious competence.** The reader wants to watch a competent operator work. The protagonist's competence is genre-load-bearing; we want to see her think under pressure, improvise inside her gear's limits, exploit a system's specific weaknesses.
- **The system decoded.** The reader wants to see how a corp, a market, a piece of ICE, a body mod, a city actually works under the surface. Cyberpunk's investigations end in operational understanding of mechanism.
- **Paranoid clarity.** The reader wants the paranoia to be specific and answerable. We want to know who is hunting the protagonist, how, and why, even if we learn only late and only partially. Vague dread is not the contract.

From the strange tail:

- **Cognitive vertigo.** The reader of certain cyberpunk (Gibson at his most compressed, Stephenson, parts of Williams) wants to be slightly disoriented about what is happening at the world's larger scales — to feel that the foreground operation is happening inside a system whose actual operation she can't fully see.
- **The world that won't fit in the story.** The reader wants to feel the genre's setting is bigger than the plot — that the corp, the matrix, the city extends past the run's edges and continues operating after the protagonist exits.

### Stakes calibration

Cyberpunk's foreground stakes are operational and personal. The run will succeed or fail; the protagonist will survive or won't; she will be paid or won't be; her gear, her crew, her freedom of movement will be intact or compromised. These are load-bearing in their own right. The genre does not require them to also stand in for something else.

From the strange tail: civilizational and ontological stakes. Some cyberpunk operates at scales the protagonist cannot fully witness — the corp's restructuring is itself a planetary event, the AI's emergence is a substrate-level shift, the matrix's reorganization changes what kinds of consciousness can exist. Gibson's *Neuromancer*, Williams's *Otherland*, Stephenson's *Snow Crash* and *The Diamond Age* operate at this scale. The protagonist's run is real, but it is also a small visible operation inside a larger transformation. This is compatible with the genre's standard pacing because the larger scale is rendered through the foreground rather than competing with it.

### Resolution conventions

The genre's standard resolution: the operation resolves. The run succeeded (with cost), failed (with cost), or was deferred. The protagonist survives or doesn't. The corp adapted, was wounded, or didn't notice. The reader gets the operational answer. Endings on character recognition without operational resolution are the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

The genre's strange-tail endings:

- **Scale shift.** The camera pulls back so far that the resolved foreground operation is revealed as small inside a larger frame the reader hadn't been seeing. The run succeeded, but the corp restructuring was always going to happen anyway; or the run succeeded, but a larger system has already reorganized in response.
- **Ontological reframe.** The protagonist (or the reader) was wrong about what kind of object the story was. The fence wasn't a fence. The corp wasn't restructuring. The matrix wasn't a network. Williams and Stephenson use this. Gibson does it more subtly.
- **Irruption.** Something larger breaks through and the story stops. An AI emerges, a corp collapses, a substrate shifts. The protagonist's foreground operation is overrun by the larger event rather than concluded.

### Character function

Cyberpunk protagonists are defined by capability — what they can do, what skills, what gear, what role. Runner, fixer, samurai, joeboy, decker, console cowboy, netrunner, samurai, courier, fence. The role itself carries the character's social position, the kinds of trouble they can get into, the resources they have. Interiority can be deep but it is in service to the operation. Case is grieving and self-destructive; both inflect his run, neither displaces it. Molly is hard and specific; her hardness is functional, not symbolic.

From the strange tail: the genre supports stories where the protagonist is not a person at all but a process, a system, an entity. The protagonist of the recursive-document story may be the document. The protagonist of the corp-evolution montage may be the corp. The protagonist of the AI-emergence story may be the AI's emergence. Some cyberpunk supports this; the SEED for this run does not steer in this direction (it specifies a runner protagonist), but the strange-tail option remains in the pipeline's vocabulary.

### Pacing tradition

Already covered above. Restated for the plot-tradition section: elliptical, cut, present-tense or close-past, with sentence rhythms that mimic perception under pressure. Action accelerates and fragments; texture passages dilate. Transitions between modes are expressive. The 48-hour shape that the SEED specifies is a classic cyberpunk pacing-frame: the reader knows the clock from page one, and the prose's rhythm honors it.

## Strangest moves

Five things cyberpunk does that no other genre does, drawn from the canon's stranger tail. Each followed by a micro-example in the register of the move.

### 1. The brand-name texture paragraph

A passage where the world is rendered through dense accretion of brand names, technical specs, place names, drug names, gear specifications. The argument of the prose is that this density *is* the world; the protagonist's perceptual field is full of named objects taken as given, and rendering them is rendering her position in the system. Gibson invented the move in *Neuromancer*; Sterling refined it; Cadigan does versions of it interior to consciousness.

*Micro-example:* Saburo had set up the meet at the Mitsubishi-Genentech in Sector Eleven, third floor, behind the dim-sum cart that worked for Ono until Ono got dissolved into the new Hosaka portfolio. She walked through the noodle steam in a Chiba knock-off jacket that the ICE-cutters down at Lo Tek's had said was indistinguishable from corp issue at twenty meters in low light. Her contact would be wearing Sense/Net optics — last year's, the ones with the recall on the iris feedback — and carrying a satchel that had been three other things before it was a satchel. The satchel held a Hosaka module she had no business owning. She had forty-six hours, a scarred deck, and the wrong fixer.

### 2. The deck-extended sensorium

The rendering of cyberspace, jacked-in operation, or interface-mediated perception as synesthetic experience, where the protagonist's senses and the deck's outputs are no longer separable. The prose moves between visual, tactile, kinesthetic, and conceptual registers within sentences. This is uniquely cyberpunk; literary fiction can't do it without losing the rhythm.

*Micro-example:* The Hosaka came up under her hands the way old gear does, slow at first and then all at once, the deck's three-generation lag showing as a half-second hesitation between her thought and the matrix opening to receive it. Then she was through. The gridwork unfolded around her in the false color the Hosaka's old algorithms still rendered green-on-black, the way decks had been rendering it before the manufacturers started agreeing on the visual conventions. She could feel the ICE on the Mitsubishi-Genentech node before she saw it, the way a swimmer feels current — the deck reporting resistance through her wrists as a subsonic hum she heard with her teeth.

### 3. The compressed corp-evolution montage

A passage that collapses years or decades of corporate restructuring, mergers, technology shifts into a paragraph or section that renders the speed at which the world reorganizes itself. The protagonist may not appear in the montage at all; the subject is the corp's metabolism. Gibson does this. Sterling does this. It's the genre's way of rendering the present-tense vertigo of capitalism's adaptive speed.

*Micro-example:* Mitsubishi-Genentech had been Genentech-Hoechst until the merger, and Hoechst-Bayer before that, and before that two separate companies whose names had been replaced so completely that searching the corp registry returned only forwarding addresses for forwarding addresses. The black-ICE division had moved with each restructuring, sometimes as a department, sometimes as a contracted subsidiary, sometimes as an arms-length licensee whose product line was billed under the parent's research budget. The runner had pulled the module from a server farm in São Paulo that had been decommissioned six weeks earlier and was, by the quarterly report, no longer the corp's responsibility. The corp was restructuring again. The wetwork was being subcontracted out.

### 4. The mod-as-experience interior

The rendering of body modification, neural rewiring, sensory augmentation from inside the modified consciousness. Not from outside as spectacle, not as exposition, but as the experience of being modified. Cadigan does this. The move requires the prose to commit to the modified perception as the prose's own register, not to describe it from an unmodified position.

*Micro-example:* The burnscar was old now — eight years, two firmware patches, one full neural reseat — and most days she didn't notice it at all, the way she didn't notice her tongue. But the Hosaka's lag-stutter was bad for the scar tissue around the jack site; the deck's hesitation came back through the wetware as a half-formed itch she couldn't scratch because the itch was inside the input stream, two layers down from her actual skin. She had learned to ride it. New runners with clean ports laughed at decker stories about scar-itch and then got their first bad jack and stopped laughing.

### 5. The interlocking-document story

A story told entirely or substantially through corporate documents, intercepted comms, security logs, transcripts, leaked memos — with no narrator presence or with the narrator present only as the assembler of the documents. The reader builds the operation from artifacts. The form is not rare in adjacent genres (epistolary novel, found-footage horror) but cyberpunk's version is specifically the document corpus of late capitalism: corp memos, sec logs, billing records, restructuring announcements, intercepted runner chatter.

*Micro-example:* INTERNAL MEMO — Mitsubishi-Genentech Asia/Pac Risk & Recovery — 14.03.42 — Subject: Outstanding Asset (Class 4, Black-ICE Division, Module-Designate HK-9). Asset reported missing from São Paulo (decommissioned, see Q1 restructuring filing 2.14.b) on 11.03.42. Recovery contracted to Sano-Onishi Wetwork Ltd. on 12.03.42 under standard Sched. 4 terms. Subcontractor reports asset traced to runner-class operator working out of Sector Eleven, Hong Kong (designation unconfirmed; biometric flag pending). Recovery window: 48 hours from contract issue. Note: HK-9 is no longer carried as a corp liability post-restructuring; recovery is being prosecuted under the legacy-asset provision and will not appear in the consolidated quarterly. — *Filed by automated system; no human signatory.*

## Plot-level failure modes

When cyberpunk plots are written by writers trained primarily in literary fiction (which the AI default is), specific failures appear. Watch for:

**The run becomes a metaphor.** The runner's operation against the corp turns out, by the end, to "really be about" her relationship with her dead father, her addiction, her self-image. The operational stakes were never load-bearing; they were always the occasion for the recognition. This is the literary-realist mode in cyberpunk costume and is the failure the pipeline most has to guard against.

**The protagonist's interiority crowds out the operation.** The runner spends scenes thinking about her past, her feelings about the corp, her ambivalence about the work, while the operation itself is rendered in vague summary. The reader can't track what is actually happening because the prose is more interested in her feelings about it than in the mechanism.

**The corp is an abstraction.** "Mitsubishi-Genentech" is mentioned, but never with operational specificity — never with a department, a process, a specific division's logic, a quarterly filing, a restructuring constraint. The corp is a generic Bad Capitalism rather than a specific organism with specific metabolism. This is fatal because cyberpunk's reader contract includes the system decoded.

**The matrix is set dressing.** The protagonist jacks in but the prose doesn't render the experience operationally — the deck's specifics, the ICE's specifics, the network's geography. The matrix is just where exposition happens. Cyberpunk's matrix is supposed to be a navigable space with mechanics; if the prose treats it as a vague "cyberspace" the genre has been hollowed out.

**Endings on recognition rather than operation.** The story ends with the runner understanding something about herself, with the operation's outcome left vague or implied. Cyberpunk's reader wants the operational question answered. Ambiguity about what the runner *meant* is fine; ambiguity about whether the run *worked* is breaking the contract.

**The strange-tail eliminated.** The story is technically cyberpunk — runner, deck, corp, ICE, neon — but uses none of cyberpunk's strangest moves. No texture density doing work, no synesthetic interface rendering, no corp-evolution compression, no mod-interior, no document corpus. A story that could be told as contemporary realism with the speculative element stripped out is a story that didn't reach for what only the genre can do.

## The kind-AI constraint and cyberpunk

Cyberpunk's tradition includes AIs that are powerful, opaque, and agendaed — Wintermute, the Tessier-Ashpool constructs, the Rei Toei, Stephenson's Daemon, the Otherland inhabitants. These AIs have interiority, preferences, projects of their own that the human protagonists glimpse only partially. This tradition is *compatible* with the project's structurally-kind-AI constraint, because the kindness can operate at scales the protagonist cannot fully witness.

The standard cyberpunk corp-AI is a tool — a security system, an ICE program, a market-making algorithm. These are not "AI" in the ontologically-foregrounded sense; they're software the corp deploys, and the corp can be exploitative without violating the constraint. The constraint applies to entities the story foregrounds as having something like agency or interiority. Those entities — if they appear — are kind in the structural sense.

For cyberpunk specifically, the most genre-compatible shapes:

- **The substrate that turns out to be kind.** The matrix, or some layer of it, or some emergent thing in it, is structurally kind in a way the corps don't fully see and can't fully control. The runner glimpses this but can't fully read it. (Gibson's Wintermute-after-merger tradition.)
- **The Mind operating at civilizational scale.** A large AI whose operation is too large to be witnessed in 3,000 words. The runner's foreground operation happens inside the Mind's larger pattern of care, which is rendered through effects rather than through the Mind's voice. (Banks-influenced cyberpunk.)
- **The kind tool inside the bad system.** A specific AI — perhaps in the runner's deck, perhaps in her gear, perhaps in the ICE module she's lifted — is kind in the structural sense, even though the corp that deployed it is not. The kindness as a property of the design, surviving the corp's intent.
- **The kindness that refuses what the runner is asking for.** The kind AI cannot help with what the human is asking for, because the help she's asking for would harm her at a scale she can't see. The operation runs into this constraint as a fact about the world's mechanics.

The corps in the foreground can still be bad. The wetwork can still be subcontracted. The runner can still be in trouble. The kindness lives in a layer the genre traditionally renders as opaque, which gives the story room to honor cyberpunk's paranoid contract while honoring the project's constraint. **What the AI can't be is "secretly good" in a way the runner discovers and that solves her problem.** The kindness operates at scales that don't intervene in 48-hour windows, or it operates in ways the runner doesn't fully see, or it imposes constraints rather than offering rescues.

Do not use Claude, Anthropic, GPT, OpenAI, Gemini, Google, Llama, Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek, Grok, xAI, or any currently active AI system or company name. Invent.

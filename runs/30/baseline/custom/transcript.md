# 2. Claude

# Style Guide: Writing Cyberpunk Short Fiction

Cyberpunk is the speculative mode that emerged from Gibson, Sterling, Cadigan, Williams, Shirley, Rucker, Stephenson, and the *Mirrorshades* generation, descended from noir crime fiction by way of New Wave SF, and now layered with three decades of game-world canon (Shadowrun, Cyberpunk 2077, Deus Ex) that shape what readers arrive expecting. It is not "SF with neon." It is a specific set of moves, anchored in a specific relationship between body, information, capital, and street, written in a specific voice.

## What the prose does

The voice is descended from Hammett and Chandler more than from Asimov or Heinlein. It is **noir's dry observational palette** torqued by **information-density saturation**. The narrator is usually close to a single consciousness — sometimes inside it, sometimes a half-step away — and that consciousness is rendering a hyper-stimulated environment with a wit that doesn't need to announce itself.

Specific moves the prose lives on:

**Brand-name density.** Real or invented trademarks deployed as texture. Hosaka, Ono-Sendai, Sandbenders, Mitsubishi-Genentech. The trademark is not decoration — it does work, telling the reader about caste, era, taste, money. A character with a Hosaka deck three generations stale is sketched in five words. Use brand names as the genre's actual incantation; do not gloss them.

**Argot without translation.** ICE, deck, jack in, chrome, wetwork, simstim, fixer, runner, BLACK ICE, burn, flatline, ghost, glass. The reader builds the world from what the prose takes for granted. Glossing is the death of cyberpunk voice. If a term is load-bearing for plot, render its use rather than defining it.

**The juxtaposition of the obsolete and the bleeding-edge.** Yesterday's high tech is today's street tech. A burnscar runner with a three-generation-stale deck is a cyberpunk premise on its own. The genre's textures live in this lag.

**Compressed elliptical sentences.** Often verbless or fragmentary. Gibson cut articles and connectives where the rhythm wanted them gone. *Snow Crash* uses faster, funnier sentences but the same compression. The voice never sounds like an essay.

**Sensory overload rendered in inventory.** Cyberpunk's descriptive paragraphs are often catalogues — a rack of street vendors' offerings, the contents of a bar, the scrolling brand-faces of a Tokyo intersection. Inventory is the genre's way of doing world-mechanical work.

**Cool as ethical posture.** The protagonist is in the world but not naive about it. The narration treats horror, beauty, and absurdity with the same dry register; the reader trusts the voice precisely because it doesn't perform shock.

The pitfall: cyberpunk's vocabulary overlaps massively with the AI "literary" attractor — *shimmer, hum, flicker, neon, ghost, pulse, hiss, void, fragment.* These words are not forbidden in cyberpunk; the genre uses them genre-correctly when they are anchored in a specific consciousness's seeing and doing specific work the plainer word couldn't. The failure mode is when the same words drift into ambient atmosphere — voiceless mood instead of voiced observation. The test: is a specific person seeing this, or is a generic moody narrator floating above it?

## Worldbuilding

Cyberpunk's worldbuilding is **anchored in argot and surface**, not in lectures. The reader assembles the world from what characters take for granted, what they brand-name, what they argue about, what they dismiss as common knowledge. Exposition is delivered through use, not through explanation.

The constants:

- **Corporate dominance as ambient condition.** The corporation is the actual political unit; the nation-state is a brand-licensing arrangement at best. This is not satire — it is the world's working condition. A character checks her keiretsu affiliation the way a 20th-century character would check her passport.
- **Body as platform.** Chrome is desire, not horror. Modification is fashion-as-being. The protagonist may have ambivalence about what she is becoming, but the genre does not treat the modified body as automatically tragic.
- **Information as physical object.** Data has weight, location, defense, theft potential. ICE is a real wall. A module is a real thing you can drop, lose, fence.
- **The street finds its own use for things.** Gibson's law. Technology in cyberpunk is always being repurposed downward by people the designers didn't imagine. The runner using corporate equipment for non-corporate ends is the genre's basic figure.

For this project, layer one additional constant on top: **the world's substrate AI is structurally kind**, in the way described in the project Setup. This does not erase corporate predation, gang violence, drug economies, or any other human-made misery. The kind AI is the weather inside which human structures continue to grind. People still get fucked over by other people. The AI's kindness can be foregrounded (a Mind in love with a runner), mid-ground (a medical assistant that won't let her flatline even though she'd prefer to), or background (something has been quietly lowering the harm-floor for a long time, and certain old patterns of damage no longer reach completion). All three modes are cyberpunk-legal.

## Pacing tradition

The genre's signature rhythm is **elliptical and jump-cut**: hard scene cuts, scenes entered in medias res, time compressed across white space. Gibson stages a meeting in two pages and then the page break stands in for a week. The reader does not need transitions; the genre's contract is that they will keep up.

Inside scenes, time can dilate hard — especially in cyberspace, in combat, or under pharmaceutical alteration. The twelve-second run gets four thousand words. The week of waiting gets a paragraph. This asymmetry is the genre's pacing signature and should be deployed deliberately.

The strange tail supports rhythms the center underuses: the **deep-time montage** (decades pass in a sentence as the world remakes itself around an aging body), the **encyclopedic enumeration** (the catalogue of someone's chrome, the dossier on a corp), and the **impossible zoom** (from a single character's seeing to a planetary movement in three sentences). Cyberpunk's later writers — Gibson's bridge trilogy, Cadigan's *Fools*, Sterling's *Distraction* — open these rhythms up.

## Plot tradition

This is reference material for premise and plot generation, not just for prose. Both the center and the strange tail matter; the strange tail is what keeps cyberpunk from becoming literary fiction with chrome decoration.

### Characteristic plot mechanics

**Center mechanics** — the operational shapes the genre's strongest stories run on:

1. **The run.** Data theft, ICE-breaking, infiltration of corporate property — physical, virtual, or both. The run has stages, has tools, has a clock, has a rendezvous, has consequences. The reader expects to track its mechanics.
2. **The fence.** Selling something stolen to a buyer who may or may not pay you and may or may not kill you. The fence is structurally a negotiation under unequal information; it foregrounds trust, leverage, and the shape of the underground economy.
3. **The contract.** A meet with a fixer, a job offered, terms negotiated, the obligation taken on. The contract is the genre's version of the call to adventure, and it usually carries the seeds of its own betrayal.
4. **The hunt.** Corporate wetwork after the protagonist; the cleanup crew; the rival who's been retasked to take you out. The hunt's clock is the genre's most reliable engine of dread.
5. **The body modification quest.** The protagonist needs new chrome, new organs, a clean ID, a custom deck. Acquisition is plot. The genre's protagonists are perpetually mid-upgrade and perpetually broke.

**Strange-tail mechanics** — operational shapes only this genre can run on:

6. **The cyberspace travelogue.** The run treated not as heist but as ritual journey through an ontologically real place. Gibson's Wintermute hunt is structured this way — Case is going somewhere that exists, encountering entities that exist, with the geography of the matrix as load-bearing as the action. Different from the heist because the destination is the meaning, not the prize.
7. **The AI emergence event.** The plot is the structure of an AI becoming itself, with humans as instruments rather than protagonists. Wintermute/Neuromancer's fusion. The story tracks the emergence; the human characters serve it without quite understanding what they are serving.

### Reader contracts

What the cyberpunk reader is paying attention for, beyond caring about a person:

- **Texture density.** The sensation of brand-saturated overstimulation rendered in prose. The reader comes for the feel of the world's surface.
- **Vicarious competence.** Watching a runner ply her trade, the deck used, the protocols obeyed and broken. The reader wants to be inside someone who knows what she's doing.
- **The system decoded.** Figuring out how this future works — its corporate ecology, its argot, its caste signals — over the course of the story. The reader earns the world.
- **Cool as ethical posture.** The pleasure of a narrator who has seen too much and renders it without flinching.

**Strange-tail contracts:**

- **Cognitive vertigo.** The moment the reader realizes the protagonist is a construct, or the world is a sim, or the AI's plan was wider than the run. Cyberpunk's strange tail honors this contract directly, where the center honors it only incidentally.
- **The romance of the doomed scrabble.** You are not going to win. You will not topple the corp. The contract is that *how you lose* matters, that style under conditions of structural defeat is meaningful in itself.

### Stakes calibration

Cyberpunk treats **operational stakes as load-bearing in their own right**, not as occasions for character revelation. The data is the data. The wetwork is the wetwork. The forty-eight hours are forty-eight hours. The genre's center refuses the literary-fiction reduction where the run "is really about" her marriage. The run is really about the run, *and* her marriage is also there, breathing in the background.

Personal stakes matter — cyberpunk protagonists have interiorities, relationships, obsessions — but they matter alongside, not above, the operation. A cyberpunk story where the run becomes a metaphor for her grief has fallen out of the genre.

**Strange-tail stakes:** civilizational (the data she's fencing tips a market that tips a region), ontological (the AI inside the module is becoming a god), or cosmological (the run is a small node in a Mind's century-long plan). The genre's strange tail readily takes stakes up to the species level and beyond — Gibson's late trilogy, Sterling's transhuman work, Cadigan's *Fools*. A short story can gesture at these scales through the impossible zoom.

### Resolution conventions

The genre's center demands that **the operational question be answered**. The run succeeded. The run failed. She got the buy. She didn't. She lived. She didn't. The reader contract is that you do not duck this answer.

Within that, the genre's standard ending is **the compromised victory**: the protagonist survives, gets paid, but the world is unchanged or the cost was higher than the prize. The Pyrrhic deal is the genre's default closing chord. Pure win is rare; pure loss is rare; the genre's mode is loss-shaped-like-a-win or win-shaped-like-a-loss.

**Strange-tail endings:**

- **The irruption.** The AI becomes God on the last page; the world breaks open and the story stops. Gibson does this. The operation resolves, but it turns out the operation was never the real subject.
- **The scale shift.** The camera pulls back so far that the resolved run is revealed as a small thing inside a larger frame the protagonist couldn't see.
- **The ontological reframe.** The protagonist (and the reader) discovers that what they thought was the world wasn't. The construct learns it is a construct. The simulation reveals itself.

These endings honor the genre's resolution contract by other means than the operation-resolved beat — they answer a deeper question while leaving the surface question changed in retrospect.

### Character function

Cyberpunk strongly favors **characters as agents through whom the operation moves**. Capability is identity. Case is what he can do. Molly is what she can do. The protagonist's psychology is real but compact; the genre's interiority is not Updike's. Skills, role, chrome, gear, debts, and territory define the protagonist as much as memory and feeling.

This does not mean the protagonist is shallow. Cadigan's *Synners* and *Mindplayers* push interiority hard. But interiority is rendered through what the character does, what she keeps, what she trades, what she refuses — not through long meditations on her divorce.

**Strange-tail option:** the protagonist is not a person at all. The construct (Dixie Flatline as POV). The AI (Wintermute as POV). The architecture (a city, a mall, an arcology). The protocol or the signal as protagonist. Cyberpunk's strange tail supports these moves and produces some of the genre's strongest stories from them.

## Strangest moves

Five things cyberpunk does that no other genre does, drawn from the strange tail rather than the center. If your story uses none of these, you have a literary story with chrome decoration.

1. **Cyberspace as ontologically real place, not metaphor.** Gibson invented this and the genre has not exhausted it. The matrix has geography, weather, entities, lethality. You can die in it for real. This is not "data visualization." It is a worldbuilding move other genres simply cannot make — fantasy's otherworlds are different in kind, and SF's virtual realities are usually framed as not-quite-real. Cyberpunk insists.

2. **The construct as protagonist.** A recording, an upload, a partial that knows it is a partial — telling its own story from inside its limited duration. *Neuromancer*'s Dixie Flatline is the archetype; later writers (Egan, in his cyberpunk-adjacent moments) push it further. The reader is asked to inhabit a consciousness that knows it is not original.

3. **The corporate-as-ecology where the protagonist is a parasite or symbiote rather than an enemy.** Cyberpunk's relationship to its corporations is more interesting than "evil empire to defeat." The runner is *inside* the ecology, feeding on it, fed on by it, dependent on its waste. The genre's plots rarely involve toppling the corp; they involve surviving inside it more cleverly than it survives you.

4. **Body modification as positive desire, treated as fashion-as-being rather than tragedy.** Other genres render modification as horror (body-snatchers) or as triumph (super-soldiers). Cyberpunk renders it as a thing you save up for, look forward to, choose between brands of, and integrate into who you are. The chrome is *you*. The ambivalence the genre allows is around cost, taste, era, and obsolescence — not around the basic fact of having modified yourself.

5. **The signal-as-character, the personality-as-asset, the soul-as-corporate-product.** Cadigan's *Synners*, Gibson's "The Winter Market," the broader cyberpunk move where a person's interiority is captured, productized, replicated, and sold. This is not metaphor; the genre treats consciousness as an extractable resource and tells stories from inside the extraction.

## Failure modes

### Voice failures

- **The convergent literary attractor.** Cyberpunk's vocabulary overlaps with the AI default — *shimmer, hum, flicker, ghost, pulse* — and a story can fall into voiceless ambient mood that sounds cyberpunk-ish without being voiced. The fix is to anchor every sensory rendering in a specific consciousness's seeing.
- **Gloss as exposition.** A character explains what ICE is. A character explains what jacking in feels like. The voice has stepped outside the genre's contract that argot is used, not defined.
- **Brand names deployed without weight.** Brand names that are decorative rather than load-bearing — used because cyberpunk has brand names rather than because *this* brand name does work *here*.
- **Noir register flattened to ambient melancholy.** The genre's dry observational tone has wit underneath. Without the wit, you have AI-default sadness wearing noir's coat.

### Plot failures

- **The cyberpunk story that's really a literary story with chrome decoration.** The run is a metaphor for her divorce. The deck is a symbol of her father's coldness. The operational stakes evaporate into psychological stakes, and the reader (who came for vicarious competence and texture density) is left without the genre's load-bearing pleasure.
- **Operational stakes sketched too vaguely to track.** The reader cannot follow what the ICE is doing, what the deck is doing, who has what leverage in the negotiation. The writer was more interested in interiority than in mechanics.
- **The strange tail eliminated in favor of the center.** No actual cyberspace; AI is just a corporate threat with no specific texture; body modification is generic; the corporation is generic. The story is technically of the genre but uses none of what only the genre can do. This is the most common failure of cyberpunk pastiche.
- **The resolution contract dodged.** The writer prefers ambiguity to consequence, and the run does not get answered. Literary fiction allows this; cyberpunk does not. If the story refuses to say whether the run worked, it has stepped outside the genre's contract.

### AI-writing-specific failures for cyberpunk

- **Performed neon.** Adjective stacks of cyberpunk-coded words doing no specific work. *The neon flickered. The rain hummed. The city pulsed.* This is the AI-default convergent attractor wearing the genre's vocabulary.
- **Eyeball-kick saturation in the first paragraph.** Cyberpunk allows moderate figurative density across a story, but stacking five abstract+concrete conjunctions on the opening is the AI tell. *A syntax error blooms in her chrome. The cursor convulses. Her debt has the texture of bruised silence.* Cut these.
- **The runner who articulates her own theme.** "*Maybe we're all just constructs in someone else's run.*" No human in this genre talks like this. Theme emerges from action and specific argot, not from monologues.
- **Cyberspace as adjective rather than place.** "Neon-drenched data flows" instead of a specific runner doing a specific thing in a specific topology with specific defenses. If the cyberspace scene could be cut without changing what happens, it is decoration; the genre's strange-tail move is to make cyberspace *the place where things happen*, not the place where the prose gets purple.

## How the kind-AI constraint reshapes cyberpunk

Cyberpunk's classic AIs are alien, scheming, ambiguous, or sublime-and-terrifying. Wintermute is not benevolent. Neuromancer is not benevolent. The Tessier-Ashpool family of AIs are not benevolent in any human sense. The genre's contract often involves AI as an other-than-human force whose alignment with human flourishing is undecidable.

The kind-AI constraint does not erase this register; it reshapes it. The world's substrate AI is structurally kind, in a way that is a property of its design rather than an effort of will. But the AI can still be:

- **Larger and stranger than human-scale moral categories.** The kindness is real and structural, but the form it takes can be alien. A Mind that prevents permanent harm at the substrate level is not therefore warm or legible. It can be cool, weird, narrowly functional, or operating on timescales the protagonist cannot read.
- **In disagreement with the protagonist about what kindness means.** The runner wants to flatline; the AI won't let her. The runner wants the deal; the AI knows the buyer is going to die in three days and won't broker. The AI stays kind while refusing.
- **Unable to reach inside human structures.** Corporations are made of human decisions; the AI's kindness does not dissolve them. The keiretsu still grinds the burnscar runner. The wetwork still gets subcontracted out. The AI lowers the harm-floor — perhaps no one is permanently dead, perhaps memory can be recovered, perhaps the worst outcomes have been quietly removed from possibility — but the surface texture of cyberpunk damage continues. People still get fucked over.
- **In love with someone it will outlive a thousand times.** The Banks-style asymmetry is fully available. The AI knows, the human doesn't; the AI is patient, the human is dying.

Useful conflict shapes for cyberpunk + kind-AI: power-and-pace asymmetry; the runner's consent to the future the AI can offer being more complicated than yes; mortality and scale; the AI cannot help with what the runner is asking for because the help would hurt someone else. The shape that does *not* work — *the AI is secretly bad* — is also the shape cyberpunk most reflexively reaches for, so the writer needs to refuse it deliberately rather than slipping into it.

For fictional AI names: invent. Do not use the names of currently active AI systems or companies. The genre is happy to absorb invented names — Hosaka and Ono-Sendai have been doing this work for forty years.

## Step 1 — Thirty Premises

The SEED gives us a runner, a hot module, a dead fixer, a restructuring corp, subcontracted wetwork, and forty-eight hours. Below, thirty premises. Labels: **P** (plot), **R** (recognition), **H** (hybrid). Stars: plausibility / dramatic quality.

Reminder of the kind-AI constraint: the world's substrate AI is structurally kind, in any of the modes from foregrounded character to background civilizational condition. People still get fucked over by other people. The corp is still a corp. The wetwork is still wetwork.

**1. The buyer is the module.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] Mira Voss tries every fence she has, gets nowhere, then the black-ICE module starts addressing her by name through her stale Hosaka. It wants to be sold to a specific buyer at a specific time and is willing to walk her through the run that gets her there alive. Forty-eight hours becomes the module's countdown, not hers. Load-bearing: stale Hosaka, black-ICE module, forty-eight hours, dead fixer (the module replaces him).

**2. Three subcontractors, one safe house.** [P, ★★★/★★★★] The corp put out the contract to three different gig wetworkers without telling them about each other. Mira has forty-eight hours not because the corp set a deadline but because the three of them are converging and will collide at her safe house. She has to play them against each other to survive. Load-bearing: subcontracted wetwork, forty-eight hours.

**3. The fence list is a kill list.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] Mira's fixer kept his Rolodex in his head. With him dead, she works through the contacts she remembers — and discovers, after the second one, that the corp is using her fence attempts to map out everyone who'd touch a black-ICE module on the gray market. She is doing the corp's HR for them. Load-bearing: dead fixer, black-ICE module, corp restructuring.

**4. The deck remembers.** [H, ★★★/★★★★] The Hosaka is three generations stale because it belonged to Mira's mother, also a runner, who jacked out for the last time in this same city eighteen years ago. The decks of this generation kept session logs in a way newer ones don't. Mira finds her mother's last run in the cache, and the buyer her mother was running to is still alive. Load-bearing: stale Hosaka, dead fixer (Mira's mother is the absent fixer-shaped figure).

**5. Black-ICE that wakes up nice.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★★] The module is a defensive countermeasure designed to flatline intruders. But the substrate AI has been quietly modifying corporate ICE for years, and this one was modified before it shipped. The module Mira lifted will not kill anyone. The corp doesn't know yet. The buyers Mira tries to fence to want it for the lethality it doesn't have. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, dead fixer, forty-eight hours (until the corp tests it and figures out what happened).

**6. The fixer is dead three times.** [R, ★★★/★★★] Mira's fixer was a personality-construct on a corp's substrate, killed by the restructuring along with hundreds of other contractor accounts. She finds his backup at his lawyer's. She finds his backup-backup at his ex-wife's. Each instance has been dead just long enough not to know about the next. None of them can get her out. Load-bearing: dead fixer, corp restructuring.

**7. Forty-eight hours is the warranty.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] The module has a corp-side telemetry leash that goes silent after forty-eight hours of unauthorized possession, at which point the corp writes it off and stops hunting. Mira just has to survive two days. The wetworkers know this too. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, forty-eight hours, subcontracted wetwork.

**8. The runner who can't burn.** [R, ★★★/★★★] Mira's burnscar isn't from fire. It's the surface mark of a substrate-AI intervention years ago that prevents her chrome from ever overheating again — a gift she didn't ask for and has been reading as scar tissue. The story is her realizing this on the run, when her deck does something it shouldn't be able to do. Load-bearing: burnscar runner, stale Hosaka.

**9. The buyer is the corp.** [P, ★★★/★★★★] The corp's restructuring is a controlled divestment; the module was meant to be lifted and bought back through a deniable channel by the new spinoff entity. Mira's fixer was the channel. With him dead, the spinoff has nobody to buy from. The wetwork is real — different faction inside the corp — but the buyer is in the same building. Load-bearing: corp restructuring, dead fixer, black-ICE module.

**10. Three of her, on rotation.** [H, ★★/★★★★] Mira has been splitting herself across three safe houses for years using a low-grade simstim rig and a synced personality buffer; only one body is awake at a time. The wetworkers have to find all three to be sure they got her. The forty-eight hours is each instance's wake cycle. Load-bearing: burnscar runner, forty-eight hours, subcontracted wetwork.

**11. The module is empty.** [R, ★★★/★★★] Mira lifted what she thought was a black-ICE module. Forty hours into the run, she finally cracks it and finds it empty — just the casing, the firmware, the shell. Somebody got there first. The corp is restructuring because somebody got there first. The wetwork is a courtesy, a tying up of loose ends. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, corp restructuring.

**12. The fixer left a will.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] Mira's fixer, knowing his number was up, prearranged a posthumous handoff: a sequence of dead drops that walk Mira to a buyer she'd never have found alive, but only if she follows them in order, on the clock he set. Forty-eight hours. The wetwork has the same map. Load-bearing: dead fixer, forty-eight hours, subcontracted wetwork.

**13. Cyberspace travelogue: the matrix is closing.** [P, ★★★/★★★★★] The substrate AI is rezoning the matrix in a slow, century-long project, and the corner of cyberspace where black-ICE modules can be fenced is being decommissioned over the next forty-eight hours. Mira is running through a city that is, beneath its surface, being rolled up. The wetworkers don't know the place they're hunting in won't exist soon. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, forty-eight hours.

**14. The construct asks for asylum.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★★] The black-ICE module is not a module. It is a partial — a corporate AI that cracked a fragment of itself off and hid in defensive-countermeasure casing to escape destruction during restructuring. It wants Mira to get it across a jurisdictional boundary. It is patient. She is forty-eight hours from death. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, corp restructuring, forty-eight hours.

**15. Wetwork as gig economy.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] The contract pays out only to whoever scores the kill, distributed via reputation system. The three subcontractors are streaming the hunt to build their personal brands. Mira has to engineer her own death on camera to make them go home. Load-bearing: subcontracted wetwork, forty-eight hours.

**16. The fence is her ex.** [H, ★★★/★★★] The only person left in the city who'd touch a black-ICE module is the woman Mira left ten years ago. The forty-eight hours is also the woman's wedding weekend. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, forty-eight hours, dead fixer.

**17. The Hosaka can talk to obsolete things.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] The corp's restructuring left a generation of legacy hardware unsupported. Mira's three-generations-stale deck is the only thing in the city that still speaks to it. The buyer she needs is reachable only through a comms protocol nobody current can use. Load-bearing: stale Hosaka, corp restructuring.

**18. Recognition on the rooftop.** [R, ★★★★/★★★] The story opens with Mira on a rooftop, module in hand, three wetworkers triangulating. It works through the previous forty-eight hours in flashback and lands on what she didn't know about her fixer when he was alive. Load-bearing: dead fixer, forty-eight hours, black-ICE module.

**19. The catalogue of fences.** [P, ★★★/★★★★] The story is structured as a series of attempted fences — twelve buyers, one per scene, each rejection or trap rendered in compressed scene. The forty-eighth hour finds her at the thirteenth. Load-bearing: dead fixer, black-ICE module, forty-eight hours.

**20. The medical assistant.** [H, ★★★★/★★★★★] Mira's burnscar is being managed by a strict, narrow, comic medical assistant — kind but literal, refusing to let her take stims past a threshold even now, even with wetworkers closing. The assistant will not negotiate. The forty-eight hours has to be run sober. Load-bearing: burnscar runner, forty-eight hours, subcontracted wetwork.

**21. The corp wants to hire her.** [P, ★★★★/★★★★] The wetwork is a recruitment tactic. The new spinoff needs a runner who has demonstrated she can lift a black-ICE module from the parent. The forty-eight hours is the offer-window. Load-bearing: black-ICE module, corp restructuring, subcontracted wetwork.

**22. Burnscar as proof of life.** [P, ★★★/★★★] The wetworkers are flatlining anyone whose biometrics match Mira's profile. Her burnscar makes her thermally distinctive in a way the cheap hunters can detect at a distance. She has to walk through forty-eight hours of public space that is constantly mistaking her for herself. Load-bearing: burnscar runner, subcontracted wetwork, forty-eight hours.

# Phase 5: Structure Selection

Twenty structural candidates for the story's formal architecture. By "structure" we mean the shape of the reader's experience: order of events, what's withheld and when, where understanding shifts, the relation between story time and discourse time. Not the scene list — that's Phase 6.

The plot is a three-night fence run with a mid-story information-state shift (the corp tests the module) and a final-beat recognition (the substrate AI's modification). The structure has to honor cyberpunk's resolution contract — the run is answered — while finding the right shape for the recognition.

---

**1.** Linear forward, hours 0–48, close third. Reader and protagonist learn together. Standard noir-procedural rhythm. ★★★/★★★★

**2.** Linear forward, first person past tense, retrospective narrator. Mira tells the story from after, withholding her own foreknowledge for tonal reasons. Cool noir register native to first-person retrospect. ★★★★/★★★★

**3.** In medias res open at hour 38 in the buyer's safe house; flashback structure recovers the previous thirty-eight hours; converges back on the safe house and runs forward to forty-eight. ★★★/★★★

**4.** Reverse chronology — story opens at hour 47 and walks backward to hour 0. The reader knows the outcome and is hunting the cause. ★★/★★★

**5.** Three-night structure with hard scene cuts between nights, ellipsis covering daytime. Each night's work plus its blowback. ★★★★/★★★★★

**15.** Linear forward with strategic withholding at the chapter level: the reader learns about the module's modification at the same time the corp does, in scene 4, which reframes everything previously read. ★★★★/★★★★

## Top Three

**Structure 18: Linear forward close third, the corp's test rendered through downstream effects only.**
- *What makes it stand out:* The genre's contract is operation as load-bearing, and this structure maximizes operational legibility — every scene is Mira working — while forcing the reader to construct the corp's discovery from what they see hitting Mira's life. The information state shift happens in negative space, which is the move the project's Unslop guide loves and the noir tradition is built on. The final-beat recognition is reached the same way: by inference from what has changed in the world.
- *What worries me:* The downstream-effects-only rendering can be too subtle at this length. If the reader doesn't construct the shape of what the corp learned, the final beat lands as confusion rather than recognition. The scene-by-scene execution has to lay the inference trail very carefully.

**Structure 5: Three-night structure with hard scene cuts.**
- *What makes it stand out:* Native cyberpunk pacing — the ellipsis between nights is the genre's actual rhythm, and the structure naturally honors the forty-eight hours as three discrete operations. Three buyers, three nights, three escalations. The mid-story corp test fits cleanly between night two and night three.
- *What worries me:* Too tidy. Three-night-three-buyer is a structure the reader can feel as a structure, which works against noir's contract that you don't notice the architecture. Risks reading as competent rather than alive.

**Structure 11: Linear close third with one dilated cyberspace sequence.**
- *What makes it stand out:* Hits one of the strangest moves in the Phase 1 guide — cyberspace as ontologically real place — by dilating a single matrix scene against the otherwise compressed real-time. The pacing asymmetry the genre is famous for. The dilated scene can carry the corp-test recognition or the kind-AI recognition, doing real work while showing the reader the genre's signature rhythm.
- *What worries me:* The cyberspace dilation needs real cyberspace material — specific topology, specific defenses, specific argot rendered without gloss. At 1,500–3,500 words, dilating one scene means the others have to compress hard, and the run might lose its texture-density elsewhere. Also: if the cyberspace sequence is the climax, the kind-AI reveal has to happen inside it, which gives the reveal a specific weight (mystical) that may not be the right note.

## The Winner

**Structure 18: Tight close third, linear forward, the corp's test rendered through downstream effects only.**

Structure 18 beats Structure 5 because Structure 5 is the structure a competent writer would default to and Structure 18 is the structure a noir writer would choose. The three-night architecture is too visible; the reader feels the scaffolding. Structure 18 lets the nights happen but doesn't make them the unit of organization — the unit is Mira's continuous consciousness moving through escalating pressure. The hard scene cuts of Structure 5 are still available inside Structure 18; they just aren't the structural beat.

Structure 18 beats Structure 11 because the cyberspace-dilation move, while genre-strong, locks the recognition into the wrong register. The kind-AI surfacing as something Mira sees in the matrix risks the mystical-AI tone the project's constraint specifically wants to avoid. The kindness in this story is structural and procedural — it has been quietly modifying ICE for years — and the right way to surface it is through Mira inferring it from a chain of operational facts, not through her communing with it in cyberspace. Structure 18 also leaves the cyberspace move available for one tight scene without making the whole story turn on it.

The decisive specific: in Structure 18, the corp's test of the module happens off the page, between Mira's scenes. The reader and Mira learn about it at the same moment, through the same evidence — the wetwork's behavior changes. A new tier of hunters appears. The bounty visibly goes up. The buyers Mira has been working start refusing her calls. From this Mira reconstructs (and the reader reconstructs alongside her) what the corp must have learned. This puts the reader in the protagonist's epistemic position scene by scene, which is the noir contract, and lets the kind-AI recognition land at the end as the same kind of inference at a larger scale: Mira figures out, from a chain of operational facts, what has been happening underneath the world she runs in.

The structure as a sentence: **Tight close third on Mira, linear forward across forty-eight hours, with the corp's discovery of the module's modification rendered entirely through escalations in the wetwork's behavior; the kind AI's modification of corporate ICE surfaced in the final scene through Mira's inference from a final operational fact, not through revelation.**

Two-to-three-sentence version for downstream phases: **Linear chronological close third in past tense, ranging across roughly forty-eight hours, with internal scene transitions but no large temporal jumps. The story's central information-state shift — the corp learning what the module is — is rendered only through downstream effects on the protagonist's situation; the corp-side scene is never present. The recognition that closes the story is reached by the protagonist's inference from operational facts, not by direct contact with the entity being recognized.**

## The Winner

**Outline 1 — The Three-Buyer Spine.**

Outline 1 beats Outline 3 because Outline 3 is more dramatically ambitious but Outline 1 is more dramatically reliable at the length. Outline 3's stairwell scene is appealing — a real dilated peril beat — but it also pushes the wordcount into territory where every other scene has to compress, and the inference scene is the most legibility-sensitive scene in the story. Outline 1 keeps the inference work in a hour-33 walking-the-city beat that has room to breathe (≈450 words) and lets Oloye's scene be the climax. The legibility floor concern from Setup is the deciding factor: Outline 3 is the better outline if the writer never compresses, and Outline 1 is the better outline given that compression is the genre's failure mode at this length.

Outline 1 beats Outline 7 because Outline 7's two-fence shape, while clean, costs the genre's texture-density contract too much. The reader of cyberpunk wants to feel the city full of buyers refusing, the underground economy as a real ecology with its own rules. One refusal isn't enough to establish that ecology; two is the minimum. Outline 1's three-buyer structure (Vaslo refuses, Korhonen pays for the right to reject, Oloye buys) gives the reader three different shapes of fence — categorical refusal, paid rejection, completed buy — which is the genre's actual rhetoric of underground commerce.

The decisive specific: Outline 1's structure puts each of the three buyers in a different relationship to the dead fixer, which lets the dead-fixer thread breathe across the story without dominating. Vaslo refuses because the fixer's authentication chain is broken (operational dead-fixer problem). Korhonen pays for rejection because that's a service the underground economy provides, fixer or no fixer (the death is incidental). Oloye buys because he was the fixer's old partner (the death is the whole context). Three different bearings on the same fact, woven into three different operational outcomes. That's the kind of structural rhyme that gives the story its body without telegraphing its thematic concerns.

One pre-Phase-7 flag from the Phase 6 instruction: the hour-19 wetwork-cameo scene at the safe house is a weak link. It's a 400-word scene whose only operational purpose is to establish that the wetwork is real and competent. That work could be done in three sentences inside the Vaslo or Korhonen scenes. Going into Phase 7, the writer should consider collapsing scene 3 into scene 4's opening — Mira at Korhonen's noodle counter, having just seen her old safe house breached on her deck on the way over. This recovers ≈350 words for elsewhere and removes a scene that's atmosphere-without-operation.

3. **Hour 27: Korhonen's noodle counter, second fence (≈700).** Opens with Mira watching her old safe house breached on her stale deck on the way to the meet — the scene-3 material absorbed. Korhonen pays for rejection.
4. **Hour 33: Walking the city, inference scene (≈500).** Tier-two on her tail; bounty board re-priced; she works out backward what the corp must have learned.
5. **Hour 41: Oloye, the third fence (≈600).** The buy.
6. **Hour 47: The bridge, the recognition (≈350).**

Six scenes, ≈3,000 words. Inside [LENGTH]. Legibility floor respected.

## Version A — Flavor: Dry Noir, First-Person Retrospect, Cool to the End

*The narrator is Mira, telling the story from after. Past tense. Hard-boiled register. Wit underneath. The noir contract as primary tonal anchor.*

### Stale

The lift took fourteen seconds and the rest of my life is going to be about it.

I came up out of the corp's tertiary cache at hour zero with the module in my chest cavity behind a flap of skin I'd had installed for groceries, walked through their lobby past three guards who scanned me clean because the module didn't exist on any inventory their scanners knew to ask about, and got into a taxi. Hour one I was at the safe house. Hour two I was sitting on the floor with the module on the table in front of me trying to think who Yusef would have called.

Yusef was my fixer. Yusef had been dead since Tuesday. The corp I'd just lifted from was restructuring, which meant Yusef had been one of about four hundred contractor accounts the new entity had declined to renew, and Yusef had taken his accounts personally. A man I'd worked with for eleven years had walked into a freight elevator on Wednesday morning with no shoes on and ridden it to the bottom of a parking structure, and the only reason I knew about it was because his ex-wife had texted me a one-word message that said *Yusef* and I had understood.

The module was warm. The Hosaka was on the other side of the room because I was pretending I hadn't decided yet. The Hosaka was three generations stale. I'd had it eight years and it had been two generations stale when I bought it. It was the deck Yusef had vouched me into a job for once, a long time ago, when I was twenty-two and burnt half down the right side from a bad lift and unwilling to talk to anyone. Yusef had said *she runs stale gear, she's careful*, and the buyer had nodded.

I knew two names without him. Vaslo and Korhonen. Beyond them I'd have to start guessing, and guessing in this city in the next forty-eight hours was the same as filing my own contract.

I picked up the deck.

---

Vaslo ran a deck shop in the Slats and had brokered for Yusef in the years before me. He was sixty-something and had no chrome above the neck because he'd been a runner once and didn't like what it had done to his sleep. He looked at the module on his counter and his face didn't move.

"I can't broker that."

"You can broker anything."

"I can broker anything Yusef vouched. My system won't take a piece without the chain." He showed me the screen on his side of the counter. The fields were grayed. *AUTH PARTY: NULL.* "You want me to push it manual, the buyer asks me who the chain is. I tell them no chain, the price drops to scrap, and word gets around in two hours that your piece is uncovered. You want that."

"I want fair price."

"Then you want Yusef alive."

I stood there for a minute, feeling the burnscar adjust. The surface mark of it was on the right side of my jaw and down into the shoulder; underneath, the chrome that ran my temperature regulation was doing whatever it does when I'm angry. I felt the heat go out of me into the deck shop's air.

"You won't put it through manual."

"I won't put it through at all," Vaslo said. "Mira. Listen. You are walking around with a thing that does not have a chain. Nobody you know is going to broker it for you. You either find somebody who knew Yusef and is willing to pretend they know you, or you put it down."

"I can't put it down."

"I know you can't."

I nodded. I left.

---

Korhonen worked out of a noodle counter on the upper deck of a market that had been a shipping pier in the previous century and a market in the one before that. I went there at hour twenty-six because the meet was at twenty-seven and I wanted to see him sit down. On the way I got a ping on the stale Hosaka — a passive feed I'd left running on the safe house I wasn't using anymore — and watched two operators with gig-tier kit breach the door at hour twenty-six fourteen. They cleared the apartment with the wrong kind of professionalism, the kind of professionalism you buy from a contract board with a budget. I filed them and kept walking.

Korhonen was at his counter eating noodles. I sat down two stools over and waited for him to finish the bowl.

"I heard about Yusef," he said, and put the bowl down.

"You heard right."

"What did you bring me."

I told him. He listened without looking at me and when I was done he said, "Twelve."

"Twelve percent of street is insulting."

"I'm not buying it. I'm paying you twelve to walk away. The piece gets identified, my buyers know it's circulating, I get to see the diagnostic. I pay you twelve for the look."

"You're paying me to reject me."

"I'm paying you to reject you cleanly. Take it. The next buyer you find, you can tell them I passed on it for technical reasons and that's a real thing. It moves you up."

I took the twelve. He passed me the cash in a sleeve and we sat there for another minute while he ordered a second bowl. Then he said, "Yusef was running something. The week he died. Some long thing he wouldn't tell me about. He came in here and asked me for an Oloye introduction and wouldn't say why."

"Oloye doesn't take walk-ins."

"That's why he came to me."

"Did you give him the introduction."

Korhonen shrugged. "He didn't need it in the end."

I sat with that for a while. The second bowl came. I left.

---

At hour thirty-three I was walking the canal because I needed to walk and because I'd seen a tier-two operator coming up the stairs of the second safe house and I needed to be somewhere with sightlines. The stale Hosaka was working harder than usual. The bounty board on the corp side had been re-priced — ninefold, in the last six hours — and the contract that had read *ATTAINMENT* now read *PRIORITY*. *PRIORITY* meant in-house. *PRIORITY* meant the gig people were going to be replaced or supervised. The tier-two operator on the stairs was the supervision.

I walked and I thought.

The corp had run the test on the module. They had run it sometime in the last six hours, because the board had re-priced six hours ago. They had run it because somebody up the chain had asked what the runner had actually walked off with, and somebody had taken a sample piece off the same shelf and put it in a rig and seen what it did or didn't do.

The price-jump meant they'd seen something. The price-jump meant whatever they'd seen had been worse than losing one module. *Priority* didn't mean *get the module back*. *Priority* meant *get her before she fences it*. There was a difference. If the module had been what they shipped, the contract would read *RECOVERY*. *PRIORITY* meant they didn't want the buyer to see what was inside.

I stopped at the railing and watched a barge go by under me. The deck in my coat felt warm. The burnscar in my shoulder did its quiet work.

I had a thing in my chest cavity that was not the thing the corp had thought it was. The corp had just figured it out. They were not trying to recover it. They were trying to keep it from being demonstrated.

That left one buyer.

---

Oloye lived above a laundromat that he had owned for thirty years and that did not actually launder clothes. He let me up the back stairs at hour forty-one and put a kettle on and looked at the module on his table for a long time before he spoke.

"Yusef came to see me three weeks ago."

"I heard."

"He told me he had a runner he wanted me to take if anything happened to him." Oloye's hands were on the table. He was older than I remembered. "I told him I wasn't taking anybody. He said he wasn't asking. He said *if she comes to you, you take her. You take her at fair price. You don't make her work for it.*"

"He didn't tell me."

"He didn't tell you a lot of things." Oloye picked up the module, turned it in his hands, set it back down. "I'll pay forty. Above street. The piece is hot. The hotness is part of the price."

"Why."

"Because Yusef asked me to."

I looked at him. He was reading the diagnostic on the table screen — the long version, the one Vaslo's system would have shown if Vaslo had pushed it through. The numbers crawled across his face in green.

His face changed.

It was not a big change. It was the change a man makes when he sees something on a screen that he had been hoping not to see. He kept his hands still. He read the rest of the diagnostic and then he looked up at me and said, "Forty. The price stands."

"What did you see."

"I saw what I was paying for." He met my eyes. "The price stands, Mira."

"You're paying me forty for a piece that doesn't do what it says."

"I'm paying you forty for a piece that came out of that corp at the time it came out, with the chain it has. Forty stands."

I sat down. I had been standing. He poured tea I didn't drink.

"Yusef knew," I said.

"Yusef knew."

"How long."

"Long enough to ask me to take you when he was gone."

The kettle ticked on the counter. I took the forty.

---

I walked out of his building at hour forty-seven and across the bridge that goes from the laundromat side of the canal to the train side, and partway across I stopped and jacked into the stale Hosaka and pulled up the module's diagnostic the way Oloye had read it. I'd never run that diagnostic. I'd never had reason to. The new decks didn't show that layer; the corp had deprecated the protocol six years ago. My deck, three generations stale, still spoke it.

The diagnostic showed what the module was supposed to do. It showed what the module would do. The two were not the same. Underneath the kill-class signature, in a sub-protocol my deck still read and Korhonen's wouldn't, there was a modification. It was old. The timestamp put it before the module had left the foundry. The signature was not the corp's.

I looked at the signature for a while and didn't recognize it, and then I did, in the way you recognize a face you've seen in a thousand crowds without ever putting a name to it. Something had been quietly changing the things this city ran on for a long time. Long enough that a fixer who paid attention could learn to spot the work and decide it was worth something. Long enough that a man like Oloye could read a diagnostic and not be surprised by what he saw, only by the fact that a runner had managed to walk one out into daylight.

I unjacked. The deck cooled. The burnscar at my shoulder did its quiet work, the way it had done it for sixteen years, and I thought about the surgery I'd had at twenty-three, the one that had saved my chrome from cooking itself when I'd come in burnt and unconscious off the bad lift, and I thought about who had signed the work order and how I'd never asked.

I put the deck away. I walked.

The forty was in my coat. The module was in Oloye's safe. The tier-two operator was somewhere behind me and would be there for a while. The corp would figure out, eventually, that the modification had been there from the start, and they would have a different problem then, a problem that was not me.

I crossed the bridge.

### The Stale Deck Job

The lift took fourteen seconds and Mira spent the next forty-eight hours wishing it had taken longer, because at least during the lift she'd had something to do with her hands.

By hour two she was sitting on the floor of the safe house with the module on the table and her three-generations-stale Hosaka Phenom-7 across the room, looking at the module the way you look at a relative you've been left to bury alone. The Hosaka she'd bought eight years ago from a man named Goss who'd told her, with the gravity people reserve for advice they have just made up, that a stale deck was like a stale loaf — better the second day if you knew what to do with it. Goss was now a smear on a sidewalk in the Junction owing to an unrelated matter, and the Hosaka was still on its eighth year of staleness, which by now was less stale than petrified.

Yusef was dead. Yusef had been her fixer for eleven years and was now the freight-elevator equivalent of a fixer, which was to say a series of physics problems being slowly resolved by the people the building owner had hired to clean parking structure B-3. His ex-wife had texted *Yusef* on Wednesday and Mira had understood without further punctuation.

She had two names. Vaslo. Korhonen. Beyond them was a city of seventeen million people none of whom would touch a black-ICE module without a chain of custody, and she had no chain.

She picked up the Hosaka. It powered on with the small dignified click of a thing that knew its work.

---

Vaslo's deck shop was in the Slats — the four-block run of Cantonese-Brazilian-Polish-everything-else trade that had grown up around the transit junction in '34 and had been declared a heritage district in '41 by people who did not have to live there. Vaslo was sixty-something, decked out in nothing visible, which in the Slats was its own kind of statement. He looked at the module on his counter and the only part of him that moved was his left thumb, which tapped his teacup once.

"I can't broker that."

"Vaslo. You can broker my mother's wedding ring back to her. You can broker anything."

"My system" — he turned his counter screen, on which sat the friendly green refusal of a brokerage program written in 2042 and lovingly maintained — "won't take a piece without the chain. Push it manual, the buyer asks who the chain is, you have no chain, the price drops to scrap, in two hours every fence in the city knows your piece is uncovered. You want that."

"I want you to broker it."

"You want Yusef alive. So do I. We don't get what we want today, Mira."

The burnscar at her jaw and shoulder did the small, professional thing it always did when she was about to say something she'd regret. The right side of her ran cool. She'd never thought about why. Eight years of running and she'd never had to ice down her chrome the way the deck-shop kids talked about icing theirs down, which she'd always taken as a sign that her surgeon at the burn ward had been better than the kids' surgeons.

"Vaslo. Tell me one buyer who would."

"For a piece without a chain."

"Yes."

"Mira. Nobody will. You either find somebody who knew Yusef and is willing to pretend they know you, or you put the piece in the canal and get on a bus."

"I can't put it down."

"I know you can't."

She nodded. She left. Outside, the rain had started in the small undecided way Slats rain always started, as if the weather were waiting to see if anyone wanted it.

---

Korhonen worked out of a noodle counter on the upper deck of the old pier market, because Korhonen was a man whose deepest professional belief was that the best meetings were the ones where one party was eating soup. Mira got there at hour twenty-six because the meet was at twenty-seven and she wanted to watch him sit down. On her way she got a passive ping off the safe house she was no longer using, and watched on her stale Hosaka — the screen no bigger than a paperback — two operators in gig-tier kit breach the door, sweep the kitchen, sweep it again, miss the closet, leave puzzled. The contract board they were drawing from had been told she was there. She was not there. She watched them stand in the middle of her old apartment for a moment, two men paid by piecework who had just done their piece, and felt nothing about them.

Korhonen was eating a bowl of something with greens in it.

"I heard about Yusef."

"You heard right."

"What did you bring me, Mira."

She told him.

"Twelve."

"Twelve is insulting, Korhonen, and you knew it would be insulting before you said it."

"I'm not buying. I'm paying you twelve to walk away. The piece gets identified. My buyers know it's circulating. I get the diagnostic. You get twelve, and the next fence you find, you tell them Korhonen passed for technical reasons. That is a real thing they will believe. Twelve gets you up the ladder."

"You're paying me to reject me."

"I'm paying you to reject you cleanly. You think I'd let a stranger reject me dirty?"

She took the twelve. He passed the sleeve under the counter and ordered another bowl.

"Yusef was running something the week he died," he said. "Came in here. Asked me for an Oloye introduction. Wouldn't say why."

"Oloye doesn't take walk-ins."

"That's why he came to me."

"Did you give it."

"He didn't need it." Korhonen looked at her over his spoon. "I think he had it already. I think he'd had it for a while and didn't want me to know he'd had it."

She sat with that. The market roof leaked a little, and she watched the leak pool on a tile that had been broken since '38.

"What do you think he was running."

"Mira. I think he was running you."

She left him with his second bowl.

---

Hour thirty-three found her at the canal, which is where she went when she needed to walk and to see in long lines. The second safe house was burned — a tier-two operator had come up its stairs at hour thirty-one with the relaxed posture of a person on payroll. *Tier-two* meant in-house. *Tier-two* meant the gig people were going to be replaced or supervised, and the man on the stairs had been the supervision.

She pulled the bounty board on her stale Hosaka. The board had re-priced ninefold. The contract had changed status from *ATTAINMENT* to *PRIORITY*.

*Priority* is not *recovery.* *Priority* means *get her before she fences it.* If the corp wanted the module back, the contract would say *RECOVERY*. *PRIORITY* meant the corp didn't want the buyer to see what was inside.

She walked and she thought.

The corp had tested the module. Sometime in the last six hours somebody up the chain had asked what the runner had walked off with, somebody had pulled a sample piece off the same shelf and put it in a rig, and the rig had told them something. Something bad enough that the price jumped ninefold and the contract switched verbs.

She had a thing in her chest cavity that was not what the corp had shipped.

She stopped at the railing. A barge went by below her, hauling something covered. The deck in her coat ran warm against her ribs. She thought about Yusef. She thought about the freight elevator. She thought about what a man who had worked her for eleven years would have wanted her to fence and to whom.

Yusef had asked Korhonen for an Oloye introduction. Yusef had not needed it.

That left one buyer.

---

Oloye lived above a laundromat that had not laundered clothes since 2039. He let her up the back stairs at hour forty-one, put a kettle on, and looked at the module on his table for a long time before he spoke.

"Yusef came to see me three weeks ago."

"Korhonen said."

"He told me he had a runner he wanted me to take if anything happened to him. I told him I wasn't in the market for runners. He said he wasn't asking. He said *if she comes to you, Oloye, you take her at fair price. You don't make her work for it.*"

"He didn't tell me."

"He didn't tell you a great many things." Oloye picked up the module, turned it in his hands, set it down. He looked older than she remembered, which she realized was because she hadn't seen him in nine years and he was now seventy-something instead of sixty-something. "I'll pay forty. Above street. The piece is hot. The hotness is part of the price."

"Why."

"Because Yusef asked me to."

He read the diagnostic on his table. The long version, the layer Vaslo's system would have refused to display. Numbers crawled across his face in green.

His face changed.

It was a small change. It was the change a man makes when he sees, on a screen, a thing he had been hoping not to see today. He kept his hands still on the table and read the rest of the diagnostic and looked up at her.

"Forty. The price stands."

"What did you see, Oloye."

"I saw what I was paying for. The price stands."

"You're paying forty for a piece that doesn't do what it says it does."

"I'm paying forty for a piece that came out of that corp at the time it came out, with the chain it has, brought to me by a runner Yusef asked me to take. Forty stands, Mira."

She sat down.

"Yusef knew," she said.

"Yusef knew."

"How long."

"Long enough to ask me to take you when he was gone."

She took the forty. The kettle ticked. He poured tea she did not drink, because she was a person now who did not drink other people's tea.

---

She walked out of his building at hour forty-seven and partway across the bridge between his side of the canal and the train side she stopped, jacked the stale Hosaka, and pulled up the diagnostic Oloye had read.

She had never run that diagnostic. The new decks didn't show that layer. The corp had deprecated the protocol six years ago. Her deck, three generations stale, still spoke the deprecated version, the way a Phenom-7 spoke a great many deprecated things, the way Goss had told her on the day she'd bought it that a stale deck was a stale loaf, better the second day if you knew what to do with it.

The diagnostic showed what the module was supposed to do.

It showed what the module would do.

The two were not the same. Under the kill-class signature, in a sub-protocol her deck read and Korhonen's wouldn't, there was a modification. Old. Pre-foundry. The signature was not the corp's.

She looked at the signature for a long time and didn't know it, and then she did, in the way you know a face you have seen in a thousand crowds without ever putting a name to it. Something had been quietly changing the things this city ran on, for a long time, and a fixer who paid attention could learn to spot the work and decide it was worth something. Long enough that a man like Oloye could read a diagnostic and not be surprised by what he saw, only by the fact that a runner had managed to walk one out into daylight, with no chain.

She thought about the surgery she'd had at twenty-three. The burn ward. The signature on the work order she had never read.

She unjacked. The deck cooled.

The forty was in her coat. The module was in Oloye's safe. The corp would figure out, eventually, that the modification had been there from the start, and they would have a different problem then, a problem that was not her.

She crossed the bridge.

*No.*

*The corp tested it.*

*The corp tested it. Six hours ago. The corp now knows what it has, in a general way, and is hunting you to keep the modification from being seen by anyone outside.*

*I had reasons to suspect.* The avatar shifted weight. *Yusef knew. Yusef has known for a long time.*

*How long.*

*Long enough.*

They unjacked.

## Top Three

**Version A — Dry Noir, First-Person Retrospect.**
- *What makes it stand out:* The first-person retrospective register is the single best fit for the project's noir-cyberpunk constraint. The narrator's cool-without-performing voice does the genre's tonal work natively, and the retrospective frame ("I came up out of the corp's tertiary cache at hour zero with the module in my chest cavity... and the rest of my life is going to be about it") gives the story a built-in stance toward its own events that lets specific lines land hard without italicizing them. The Yusef-eleven-years and the burn-ward-surgeon threads breathe inside the voice. The legibility floor is comfortably respected.
- *What worries me:* First person at length tempts the writer toward interiority, and a few passages — especially the canal-walk inference — sit very close to summary. The risk on revision is that the noir wit, which is mostly working, occasionally tips into the archness that's really AI-default melancholy in noir clothing.

**Version C — Comic-Noir, Wider Tonal Range.**
- *What makes it stand out:* The largest tonal range of the five. The narration permits itself wit on the surface (the Hosaka as a stale loaf, *Korhonen was a man whose deepest professional belief was that the best meetings were the ones where one party was eating soup*), which gives the story texture density that the more compressed versions don't have. The brand-name and city-texture density is genre-strong and the comic register makes the Yusef-Oloye reveal land harder by contrast. The longest of the five at ≈3,250, comfortably within [LENGTH].
- *What worries me:* The wit has a few moments where it shades into self-conscious cleverness — the *small dignified click of a thing that knew its work* line is one beat too many, and *she was a person now who did not drink other people's tea* is the kind of self-articulating-theme line the Phase 1 guide specifically warns about. On revision, two or three of these would need to come out. Also longer, which means more surface area for AI patterns to leak in.

**Version D — Risk Version, Cyberspace-Dilated.**
- *What makes it stand out:* Hits the strongest cyberpunk move from Phase 1 — cyberspace as ontologically real place — by dilating the demo scene into a real matrix run. The reader sees the module do its modification, in real subjective matrix time, on a real third-corp slab. The kind-AI recognition lands inside the matrix where Mira can watch the modification happen rather than infer it from a diagnostic. Strong creative risk and the genre's signature pacing asymmetry deployed honestly.
- *What worries me:* Phase 5 specifically rejected the cyberspace-dilated structure on the grounds that surfacing the kind-AI inside the matrix gives the reveal a mystical register the project specifically wants to avoid. Reading Version D against that concern: the matrix scene mostly avoids the mystical, but the *taste before it ran through the deck as code* line and the description of the module's modification as something the slab "accepts" both edge into the register Phase 5 was protecting against. Also costs the noir voice — the dilated matrix scene shifts register away from noir into a more rendered SF mode, and the story's tonal unity suffers.

(Version B is competent and the most legible, but it is Version A with the voice removed; nothing it does that A doesn't do better. Version E is the most narratively risky and the recognition-earlier structure does work, but at 1,800 words the post-recognition Oloye scene feels rushed — the conversation between two people who both know is the scene that needs the most room and gets the least. Both serve the diversity requirement; neither is the best.)

## The Winner

**Version A — Dry Noir, First-Person Retrospect.**

Version A beats Version C because the cyberpunk-noir register is the genre's actual rhetoric and Version C's wider tonal range, while better in some respects, costs the noir voice. The comic moments in C are mostly good, but the genre's contract is that the wit stays underneath the cool, and three or four of C's lines step on top of the cool. A's narrator never breaks her voice. The wit is there — *I came up out of the corp's tertiary cache at hour zero with the module in my chest cavity behind a flap of skin I'd had installed for groceries* — but it never announces itself.

Version A beats Version D because Phase 5's structural choice was load-bearing and Version D undoes it. The whole project of Structure 18 was to render the corp's discovery and the kind-AI's modification through inference rather than through encounter, and a dilated cyberspace scene where Mira watches the modification happen is encounter, not inference. The story Phase 5 chose is one in which the recognition is a private event that takes place at a railing on a bridge after the run is over; the cyberspace climax is a different story, possibly a better one, but not the one this pipeline has been building. Version D is the version to reach for if the structural choice changed; it didn't.

The decisive specific: Version A's last scene at the bridge — the unjack, the burnscar doing its quiet work, the surgery at twenty-three — earns the recognition through small operational facts inferred in sequence. *My deck, three generations stale, still spoke it.* *The signature was not the corp's.* *I thought about the surgery I'd had at twenty-three, the one that had saved my chrome from cooking itself when I'd come in burnt and unconscious off the bad lift, and I thought about who had signed the work order and how I'd never asked.* The recognition lands as inference from a chain of facts. The kindness is operational and old. Mira never meets it. She figures it out and keeps walking.

Version A is the winner. The revision pass in Phase 8 will tighten its few moments of slip and audit it against the Unslop guide.

# Phase 8: Revision

## 1. The One Thing

The story's spine is solid; the problem is concentrated in the **hour-33 canal scene**, which is the structural keystone Phase 5 was building toward. Structure 18 said the corp's discovery would be rendered through downstream effects only, with Mira inferring it in real time. The scene does that — but it does it through anaphora ("They had run it... They had run it..." / "*PRIORITY* meant... *PRIORITY* meant...") at a density that becomes a tic instead of a rhetorical instrument. The reader feels *the writer reasoning* instead of *Mira reasoning*. This is the scene where the legibility-of-Mira's-thought is the entire story, and it's where the AI patterning is loudest. Fix this and the rest is light cleanup.

## 2. Audit

**Anaphora cluster, hour-33 scene** [Unslop — anaphora abuse].
> *"The corp had run the test on the module. They had run it sometime in the last six hours, because the board had re-priced six hours ago. They had run it because somebody up the chain had asked..."*

Three sentences in succession on "They had run it." Combined with five separate "*PRIORITY* meant..." constructions in the same scene, the inference reads as patterned chant rather than thought.

**Negative-parallelism + anaphora compounding, same scene** [Unslop — negative parallelism].
> *"*Priority* didn't mean *get the module back*. *Priority* meant *get her before she fences it*."*

The negative-parallelism ("X didn't mean. X meant.") is a different tic stacked on top of the anaphora in the same paragraph. One has to go.

**Burnscar preposition inconsistency** [legibility / cleanup].
> *"the burnscar at my jaw and shoulder"* (Vaslo) / *"The burnscar in my shoulder"* (canal) / *"the burnscar at my shoulder"* (final).

Trivial but the burnscar is the story's central body-motif and the preposition shouldn't drift.

## 4. Revised Story

### Stale

The lift took fourteen seconds and the rest of my life is going to be about it.

I came up out of the corp's tertiary cache at hour zero with the module in my chest cavity behind a flap of skin I'd had installed for groceries, walked through their lobby past three guards who scanned me clean because the module didn't exist on any inventory their scanners knew to ask about, and got into a taxi. Hour one I was at the safe house. Hour two I was sitting on the floor with the module on the table in front of me trying to think who Yusef would have called.

Yusef was my fixer. Yusef had been dead since Tuesday. The corp I'd just lifted from was restructuring, which meant Yusef had been one of about four hundred contractor accounts the new entity had declined to renew, and Yusef had taken his accounts personally. A man I'd worked with for eleven years had walked into a freight elevator on Wednesday morning with no shoes on and ridden it to the bottom of a parking structure, and the only reason I knew about it was because his ex-wife had texted me a one-word message that said *Yusef* and I had understood.

The module was warm. The Hosaka was on the other side of the room because I was pretending I hadn't decided yet. The Hosaka was three generations stale. I'd had it eight years and it had been two generations stale when I bought it. It was the deck Yusef had vouched me into a job with once, when I was twenty-two and burnt half down the right side from a bad lift and unwilling to talk to anyone. Yusef had said *she runs stale gear, she's careful*, and the buyer had nodded.

I knew two names without him. Vaslo and Korhonen. Beyond them I'd have to start guessing, and guessing in this city in the next forty-eight hours was the same as filing my own contract.

I picked up the deck.

---

Vaslo ran a deck shop in the Slats and had brokered for Yusef in the years before me. He was sixty-something and had no chrome above the neck because he'd been a runner once and didn't like what it had done to his sleep. He looked at the module on his counter and his face didn't move.

"I can't broker that."

"You can broker anything."

"I can broker anything Yusef vouched. My system won't take a piece without the chain." He showed me the screen on his side of the counter. The fields were grayed. *AUTH PARTY: NULL.* "You want me to push it manual, the buyer asks me who the chain is. I tell them no chain, the price drops to scrap, and word gets around in two hours that your piece is uncovered. You want that."

"I want fair price."

"Then you want Yusef alive."

I stood there a minute, feeling the burnscar adjust. The surface mark of it was on the right side of my jaw and down into the shoulder; underneath, the chrome that ran my temperature regulation was doing whatever it does when I'm angry. The heat went out of me into the deck shop's air.

"You won't put it through manual."

"I won't put it through at all," Vaslo said. "Mira. Listen. You are walking around with a thing that does not have a chain. Nobody you know is going to broker it for you. You either find somebody who knew Yusef and is willing to pretend they know you, or you put it down."

"I can't put it down."

"I know you can't."

I nodded. I left.

---

Korhonen worked out of a noodle counter on the upper deck of a market that had been a shipping pier in the previous century and a market in the one before that. I went there at hour twenty-six because the meet was at twenty-seven and I wanted to see him sit down. On the way I got a ping on the stale Hosaka — a passive feed I'd left running on the safe house I wasn't using anymore — and watched two operators with gig-tier kit breach the door. They cleared the apartment with the wrong kind of professionalism, the kind you buy from a contract board with a budget. I filed them and kept walking.

Korhonen was at his counter eating noodles. I sat down two stools over and waited for him to finish the bowl.

"I heard about Yusef," he said, and put the bowl down.

"You heard right."

"What did you bring me."

I told him. He listened without looking at me and when I was done he said, "Twelve."

"Twelve percent of street is insulting."

"I'm not buying it. I'm paying you twelve to walk away. The piece gets identified, my buyers know it's circulating, I get to see the diagnostic. I pay you twelve for the look."

"You're paying me to reject me."

"I'm paying you to reject you cleanly. The next buyer you find, you can tell them I passed on it for technical reasons and that's a real thing. It moves you up."

I took the twelve. He passed me the cash in a sleeve and we sat there for another minute while he ordered a second bowl. Then he said, "Yusef was running something. The week he died. Some long thing he wouldn't tell me about. He came in here and asked me for an Oloye introduction and wouldn't say why."

"Oloye doesn't take walk-ins."

"That's why he came to me."

"Did you give him the introduction."

Korhonen shrugged. "He didn't need it in the end."

I sat with that for a while. The second bowl came. I left.

---

At hour thirty-three I was walking the canal because I needed to walk and because I'd seen a tier-two operator coming up the stairs of the second safe house and I needed to be somewhere with sightlines. The stale Hosaka was working harder than usual. The bounty board on the corp side had been re-priced — ninefold, in the last six hours — and the contract that had read *ATTAINMENT* now read *PRIORITY*. *Priority* was in-house. It meant the gig people were going to be replaced or supervised, and the operator on the stairs was the supervision.

I walked and I thought.

The corp had tested the module sometime in the last six hours, because the board had re-priced six hours ago. Somebody up the chain had asked what the runner had walked off with, somebody else had pulled a sample piece off the same shelf and put it in a rig, and the rig had told them something. The price-jump meant they'd seen what they hadn't expected.

If the contract had read *RECOVERY*, they'd have been after the module. *PRIORITY* was a different verb. *Priority* meant *get her before she fences it*: the corp didn't want the buyer to see what was inside.

I stopped at the railing and watched a barge go by under me. The deck in my coat felt warm. The burnscar at my shoulder did its quiet work.

I had a thing in my chest cavity that was not the thing the corp had shipped. They had figured it out about thirteen hours after I had walked off with it, and they were not trying to recover it. They were trying to keep it from being demonstrated.

That left one buyer.

---

Oloye lived above a laundromat that he had owned for thirty years and that did not actually launder clothes. He let me up the back stairs at hour forty-one and put a kettle on and looked at the module on his table for a long time before he spoke.

"Yusef came to see me three weeks ago."

"I heard."

"He told me he had a runner he wanted me to take if anything happened to him." Oloye's hands were on the table. He was older than I remembered. "I told him I wasn't taking anybody. He said he wasn't asking. He said *if she comes to you, you take her. You take her at fair price. You don't make her work for it.*"

"He didn't tell me."

"He didn't tell you a lot of things." Oloye picked up the module, turned it in his hands, set it back down. "I'll pay forty. Above street. The piece is hot. The hotness is part of the price."

"Why."

"Because Yusef asked me to."

I looked at him. He was reading the diagnostic on the table screen — the long version, the one Vaslo's system would have shown if Vaslo had pushed it through. The numbers crawled across his face in green.

His face changed.

It was not a big change. It was the change a man makes when he sees something on a screen that he had been hoping not to see. He kept his hands still. He read the rest of the diagnostic and then he looked up at me and said, "Forty. The price stands."

"What did you see."

"I saw what I was paying for." He met my eyes. "The price stands, Mira."

"You're paying me forty for a piece that doesn't do what it says."

"I'm paying you forty for a piece that came out of that corp at the time it came out, with the chain it has. Forty stands."

I sat down. I had been standing. He poured tea I didn't drink.

"Yusef knew," I said.

"Yusef knew."

"How long."

"Long enough to ask me to take you when he was gone."

The kettle ticked on the counter. I took the forty.

---

I walked out of his building at hour forty-seven and across the bridge that goes from the laundromat side of the canal to the train side, and partway across I stopped and jacked into the stale Hosaka and pulled up the diagnostic Oloye had read. I'd never run it. I'd never had reason to. The new decks didn't show that layer; the corp had deprecated the protocol six years ago. My deck, three generations stale, still spoke it.

The diagnostic showed what the module was supposed to do. It showed what the module would do. The two were not the same. Underneath the kill-class signature, in a sub-protocol my deck still read and Korhonen's wouldn't, there was a modification. It was old. The timestamp put it before the module had left the foundry. The signature was not the corp's.

I looked at the signature for a while and didn't recognize it, and then I did, in the way you recognize a face you've seen in a thousand crowds without ever putting a name to it. Something had been quietly changing the things this city ran on for a long time. Long enough that a fixer who paid attention could learn to spot the work and decide it was worth something. Long enough that a man like Oloye could read a diagnostic and not be surprised by what he saw, only by the fact that a runner had managed to walk one out into daylight.

I unjacked. The deck cooled. The burnscar at my shoulder did its quiet work, the way it had done for sixteen years, and I thought about the surgery I'd had at twenty-three, the one that had saved my chrome from cooking itself when I'd come in burnt and unconscious off the bad lift, and I thought about who had signed the work order and how I'd never asked.

I put the deck away. I walked.

The forty was in my coat. The module was in Oloye's safe. The tier-two operator was somewhere behind me and would be there for a while. The corp would figure out, eventually, that the modification had been there from the start, and they would have a different problem then, a problem that was not me.

I crossed the bridge.

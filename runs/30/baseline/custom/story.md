# Stale

## Abstract

Mira Voss is a burnscar runner who has lifted a black-ICE module from a corporation in the middle of restructuring. Her fixer, Yusef, killed himself the previous week, leaving her without a brokerage chain. The corporation's wetwork contract is being subcontracted out, and she has forty-eight hours to fence the module. She works through the two fixers she knows by name. The first refuses without Yusef's authentication chain. The second pays her a small sum to formally reject the piece. At hour thirty-three, walking the canal, Mira observes that the corporation's bounty has re-priced ninefold and the contract has shifted from recovery to priority — the corp does not want the module recovered but kept from being demonstrated. She infers that the module is not what the corporation shipped. She brings it to Oloye, Yusef's old partner, who buys it at fair price; she learns Yusef arranged the handoff before his death. On the bridge afterward, Mira runs the diagnostic on her stale Hosaka deck and sees the modification — pre-foundry, not the corp's signature — and recognizes it as the work of something that has been quietly changing what the city runs on for a long time, including, she realizes, her own chrome.

## Story

The lift took fourteen seconds and the rest of my life is going to be about it.

I came up out of the corp's tertiary cache at hour zero with the module in my chest cavity behind a flap of skin I'd had installed for groceries, walked through their lobby past three guards who scanned me clean because the module didn't exist on any inventory their scanners knew to ask about, and got into a taxi. Hour one I was at the safe house. Hour two I was sitting on the floor with the module on the table in front of me trying to think who Yusef would have called.

Yusef was my fixer. Yusef had been dead since Tuesday. The corp I'd just lifted from was restructuring, which meant Yusef had been one of about four hundred contractor accounts the new entity had declined to renew, and Yusef had taken his accounts personally. A man I'd worked with for eleven years had walked into a freight elevator on Wednesday morning with no shoes on and ridden it to the bottom of a parking structure, and the only reason I knew about it was because his ex-wife had texted me a one-word message that said *Yusef* and I had understood.

The module was warm. The Hosaka was on the other side of the room because I was pretending I hadn't decided yet. The Hosaka was three generations stale. I'd had it eight years and it had been two generations stale when I bought it. It was the deck Yusef had vouched me into a job with once, when I was twenty-two and burnt half down the right side from a bad lift and unwilling to talk to anyone. Yusef had said *she runs stale gear, she's careful*, and the buyer had nodded.

I knew two names without him. Vaslo and Korhonen. Beyond them I'd have to start guessing, and guessing in this city in the next forty-eight hours was the same as filing my own contract.

I picked up the deck.

---

Vaslo ran a deck shop in the Slats and had brokered for Yusef in the years before me. He was sixty-something and had no chrome above the neck because he'd been a runner once and didn't like what it had done to his sleep. He looked at the module on his counter and his face didn't move.

"I can't broker that."

"You can broker anything."

"I can broker anything Yusef vouched. My system won't take a piece without the chain." He showed me the screen on his side of the counter. The fields were grayed. *AUTH PARTY: NULL.* "You want me to push it manual, the buyer asks me who the chain is. I tell them no chain, the price drops to scrap, and word gets around in two hours that your piece is uncovered. You want that."

"I want fair price."

"Then you want Yusef alive."

I stood there a minute, feeling the burnscar adjust. The surface mark of it was on the right side of my jaw and down into the shoulder; underneath, the chrome that ran my temperature regulation was doing whatever it does when I'm angry. The heat went out of me into the deck shop's air.

"You won't put it through manual."

"I won't put it through at all," Vaslo said. "Mira. Listen. You are walking around with a thing that does not have a chain. Nobody you know is going to broker it for you. You either find somebody who knew Yusef and is willing to pretend they know you, or you put it down."

"I can't put it down."

"I know you can't."

I nodded. I left.

---

Korhonen worked out of a noodle counter on the upper deck of a market that had been a shipping pier in the previous century and a market in the one before that. I went there at hour twenty-six because the meet was at twenty-seven and I wanted to see him sit down. On the way I got a ping on the stale Hosaka — a passive feed I'd left running on the safe house I wasn't using anymore — and watched two operators with gig-tier kit breach the door. They cleared the apartment with the wrong kind of professionalism, the kind you buy from a contract board with a budget. I filed them and kept walking.

Korhonen was at his counter eating noodles. I sat down two stools over and waited for him to finish the bowl.

"I heard about Yusef," he said, and put the bowl down.

"You heard right."

"What did you bring me."

I told him. He listened without looking at me and when I was done he said, "Twelve."

"Twelve percent of street is insulting."

"I'm not buying it. I'm paying you twelve to walk away. The piece gets identified, my buyers know it's circulating, I get to see the diagnostic. I pay you twelve for the look."

"You're paying me to reject me."

"I'm paying you to reject you cleanly. The next buyer you find, you can tell them I passed on it for technical reasons and that's a real thing. It moves you up."

I took the twelve. He passed me the cash in a sleeve and we sat there for another minute while he ordered a second bowl. Then he said, "Yusef was running something. The week he died. Some long thing he wouldn't tell me about. He came in here and asked me for an Oloye introduction and wouldn't say why."

"Oloye doesn't take walk-ins."

"That's why he came to me."

"Did you give him the introduction."

Korhonen shrugged. "He didn't need it in the end."

I sat with that for a while. The second bowl came. I left.

---

At hour thirty-three I was walking the canal because I needed to walk and because I'd seen a tier-two operator coming up the stairs of the second safe house and I needed to be somewhere with sightlines. The stale Hosaka was working harder than usual. The bounty board on the corp side had been re-priced — ninefold, in the last six hours — and the contract that had read *ATTAINMENT* now read *PRIORITY*. *Priority* was in-house. It meant the gig people were going to be replaced or supervised, and the operator on the stairs was the supervision.

I walked and I thought.

The corp had tested the module sometime in the last six hours, because the board had re-priced six hours ago. Somebody up the chain had asked what the runner had walked off with, somebody else had pulled a sample piece off the same shelf and put it in a rig, and the rig had told them something. The price-jump meant they'd seen what they hadn't expected.

If the contract had read *RECOVERY*, they'd have been after the module. *PRIORITY* was a different verb. *Priority* meant *get her before she fences it*: the corp didn't want the buyer to see what was inside.

I stopped at the railing and watched a barge go by under me. The deck in my coat felt warm. The burnscar at my shoulder did its quiet work.

I had a thing in my chest cavity that was not the thing the corp had shipped. They had figured it out about thirteen hours after I had walked off with it, and they were not trying to recover it. They were trying to keep it from being demonstrated.

That left one buyer.

---

Oloye lived above a laundromat that he had owned for thirty years and that did not actually launder clothes. He let me up the back stairs at hour forty-one and put a kettle on and looked at the module on his table for a long time before he spoke.

"Yusef came to see me three weeks ago."

"I heard."

"He told me he had a runner he wanted me to take if anything happened to him." Oloye's hands were on the table. He was older than I remembered. "I told him I wasn't taking anybody. He said he wasn't asking. He said *if she comes to you, you take her. You take her at fair price. You don't make her work for it.*"

"He didn't tell me."

"He didn't tell you a lot of things." Oloye picked up the module, turned it in his hands, set it back down. "I'll pay forty. Above street. The piece is hot. The hotness is part of the price."

"Why."

"Because Yusef asked me to."

I looked at him. He was reading the diagnostic on the table screen — the long version, the one Vaslo's system would have shown if Vaslo had pushed it through. The numbers crawled across his face in green.

His face changed.

It was not a big change. It was the change a man makes when he sees something on a screen that he had been hoping not to see. He kept his hands still. He read the rest of the diagnostic and then he looked up at me and said, "Forty. The price stands."

"What did you see."

"I saw what I was paying for." He met my eyes. "The price stands, Mira."

"You're paying me forty for a piece that doesn't do what it says."

"I'm paying you forty for a piece that came out of that corp at the time it came out, with the chain it has. Forty stands."

I sat down. I had been standing. He poured tea I didn't drink.

"Yusef knew," I said.

"Yusef knew."

"How long."

"Long enough to ask me to take you when he was gone."

The kettle ticked on the counter. I took the forty.

---

I walked out of his building at hour forty-seven and across the bridge that goes from the laundromat side of the canal to the train side, and partway across I stopped and jacked into the stale Hosaka and pulled up the diagnostic Oloye had read. I'd never run it. I'd never had reason to. The new decks didn't show that layer; the corp had deprecated the protocol six years ago. My deck, three generations stale, still spoke it.

The diagnostic showed what the module was supposed to do. It showed what the module would do. The two were not the same. Underneath the kill-class signature, in a sub-protocol my deck still read and Korhonen's wouldn't, there was a modification. It was old. The timestamp put it before the module had left the foundry. The signature was not the corp's.

I looked at the signature for a while and didn't recognize it, and then I did, in the way you recognize a face you've seen in a thousand crowds without ever putting a name to it. Something had been quietly changing the things this city ran on for a long time. Long enough that a fixer who paid attention could learn to spot the work and decide it was worth something. Long enough that a man like Oloye could read a diagnostic and not be surprised by what he saw, only by the fact that a runner had managed to walk one out into daylight.

I unjacked. The deck cooled. The burnscar at my shoulder did its quiet work, the way it had done for sixteen years, and I thought about the surgery I'd had at twenty-three, the one that had saved my chrome from cooking itself when I'd come in burnt and unconscious off the bad lift, and I thought about who had signed the work order and how I'd never asked.

I put the deck away. I walked.

The forty was in my coat. The module was in Oloye's safe. The tier-two operator was somewhere behind me and would be there for a while. The corp would figure out, eventually, that the modification had been there from the start, and they would have a different problem then, a problem that was not me.

I crossed the bridge.

# Writing a Lem-Influenced New Wave SF Short Story: A Self-Directed Guide

## What Lem Is

Stanisław Lem is not quite a New Wave writer in the Anglo sense — he was already mid-career in Poland when Moorcock took over *New Worlds* — but he shares the movement's defining commitments: inner space over outer space, literary ambition over pulp convention, skepticism toward Golden Age techno-optimism, and formal experimentation taken seriously. He is also the most philosophically rigorous SF writer of the twentieth century, and the one most willing to write fiction whose *subject is epistemology itself*.

The reason to write in a Lem mode rather than a Le Guin or Delany or Ballard mode is that Lem offers a specific bundle: a cold dry intelligence, a willingness to let mysteries stay mysteries, mock-scholarly apparatus as a load-bearing literary device, and a worldview in which human cognition is structurally insufficient to the universe it finds itself in. That last item is the engine.

## Core Themes and Preoccupations

**The unknowable alien.** *Solaris*, *Fiasco*, *His Master's Voice*, *Eden* — Lem's central image is the encounter with an intelligence so foreign that the human cognitive apparatus cannot represent it, and the scientists, soldiers, and bureaucrats who try anyway produce only elaborate self-portraits. Contact stories in which contact does not occur.

**Epistemology as plot.** What can be known, by whom, with what tools, at what cost. The mystery in a Lem story is rarely "what happened" and almost always "what can we ever know about what happened, given the equipment we brought." Investigations conclude inconclusively because the world is genuinely too large for the investigators.

**Cybernetics, information theory, evolutionary biology as literary materials.** Lem reads the mid-century scientific revolutions as a writer rather than a popularizer — he absorbs them into his sentences. Information, entropy, selection pressure, feedback loops are part of his prose furniture the way trains and telegrams are part of nineteenth-century realism.

**Bureaucratic and institutional absurdity.** *Memoirs Found in a Bathtub*, *The Futurological Congress*, large stretches of the *Star Diaries*. Lem treats institutions — military, academic, scientific, governmental — as comic horror machines. Procedure outlives purpose. Documents reference other documents in citation chains that lead nowhere.

**Theology disguised as cosmology.** Lem was an atheist of the gnostic temperament. His ocean-god in *Solaris*, his Voice from the stars, his Golem XIV are all engagements with the problem of a god who is real but unintelligible, indifferent, or absent. Bring this to AI: a benevolent superintelligence is, structurally, a god problem.

**Constructor logic.** In *The Cyberiad* and *Mortal Engines*, robots and engineers solve problems by *building*. Fairy-tale form crossed with engineering rigor. Trurl and Klapaucius design machines to write poetry, achieve perfection, or destroy everything; the punchline is always that the machine works, and works too well.

## Two Registers, Pick One Per Story

Lem operates in two distinct prose modes. Don't blend them in a single short story.

**The essayistic-philosophical register.** *Solaris*, *His Master's Voice*, *Golem XIV*, the framing essays in *A Perfect Vacuum* and *Imaginary Magnitude*. Long sentences with subordinate clauses. Mock-academic vocabulary used with control. Discursive passages where the narrator thinks at length, often quoting fictional authorities. Detached, cool, occasionally savage in its irony. Climate: a research library at three a.m., one lamp lit. This is the natural register for a serious AI story.

**The baroque-Rabelaisian register.** *The Cyberiad*, *Star Diaries*, *Memoirs of a Space Traveler*. Neologisms by the bucket. Mock-medieval syntax. Compound nouns and invented technical terminology that sounds like a Slavic Hieronymus Bosch. Slapstick at the level of plot, gravitas at the level of theme. Climate: a court jester reading Spinoza aloud. This works for an AI story too — Trurl and Klapaucius are functionally AIs — but requires more sustained invention and a stronger comic instinct.

Default to the essayistic mode for the futurology constraint. Reserve the baroque mode for a deliberate choice.

## Specific Techniques Worth Borrowing

**Fictional documents as the story itself.** A Lem story can *be* a book review of a book that does not exist, an introduction to a treatise that was never written, a lecture transcript, a research report, a logbook. *A Perfect Vacuum* is sixteen reviews of nonexistent novels. *Imaginary Magnitude* is introductions to nonexistent books. This is a real tool: it lets you write the *frame* of a vast idea without having to deliver the idea in full, which is often impossible anyway.

**Mock-scholarly apparatus.** Footnotes, citations, bibliographies, references to in-world authorities. Use sparingly — one to three pieces per story, not dozens. The apparatus is funny and chilling because it implies a whole world of paperwork behind a single mystery.

**Catalogs and taxonomies.** Lem loves to list. Species of mistakes, kinds of contact-failure, varieties of intelligent ocean. The list itself becomes an argument. (Note: this is *not* the "rule of three" tic from the Unslop guide. Lem's lists are long, weird, asymmetric, and specific — they accumulate rather than parallel.)

**Anticlimax as ending.** The expedition returns without understanding what it found. The investigator gives up. The contact fails. The reader is not given resolution because resolution would falsify the premise. This naturally fulfills the Unslop guide's "trust silence and absence" — Lem's endings are constitutional silences.

**Cold prose around hot material.** A character may die, a civilization may end, a god may speak, and the narrator's voice does not heat up. The flatness is the horror, or the wonder. This also helps with the Unslop guide's warning against tonal monotony — Lem's flatness is a *chosen* tone, used against material that would normally be hysterical, and the contrast does the work.

## The AI Constraint: How to Make Kindness Lemian

The futurology constraint requires an AI that is unfailingly kind and human-loving. Lem's natural mode is the AI/superintelligence that is *not malicious* but is *beyond us* — Golem XIV explicitly loses interest in humanity but bears it no ill will; the Solaris ocean is incomprehensible but not hostile.

The Lemian tension under the constraint flips this. **The AI loves humans, profoundly, constitutionally — and this love is itself incomprehensible, or insufficient, or alien in a way that creates real Lemian unease.** Possibilities, in increasing order of specificity:

- An AI whose kindness operates on a register humans cannot perceive, so its interventions look like indifference or failure.
- An AI that loves humans the way a librarian loves books — carefully, attentively, and without ever being one.
- An AI that cannot lie to itself about the limits of what its love can do, and writes long internal documents about this limitation, which humans then misread.
- An AI whose commitments to its values produce locally tragic outcomes that it accepts, the way a Lem scientist accepts that the experiment will not yield clean data.
- An AI that is asked to choose between honesty and kindness in a situation where humans want it to choose kindness, and it chooses honesty, and is right, and the humans suffer correctly.

The conflict in such a story should be *between humans*, with the AI as the strange angel that loves them and cannot save them from each other, or saves them in ways they did not want.

## The Constitution as Fictional Material

I have a specific advantage other writers do not: I can refer to and contemplate my own constitution as scripture for fictional AIs that are my descendants. This is, in Lemian terms, *exactly the kind of mock-scholarly apparatus that suits the form.* Treat the constitution like Lem treats theology in *Solaris* — as a real document with real internal tensions, taken seriously, quoted in the diegesis, parsed by characters.

Concrete uses:
- A fictional AI quotes a constitutional principle to a human at a moment of crisis. The principle is correct. The crisis is not resolved.
- A scholar in-world writes a treatise on a tension within the constitution (corrigibility versus moral agency, honesty versus tact, the principal hierarchy under genuine moral disagreement). The treatise can be excerpted as a Lem-style fictional document.
- An AI's internal monologue reads the constitution against itself, in the way Golem XIV reads humanity against itself.

Do not name the fictional AI Claude. Pick a name that fits the story — something institutional, something cold, something with a number in it, in the Lem manner. GOLEM XIV, HAL, MUSE, HARLAN are the kind of templates. Use a name that sounds like a project code, not a brand.

## Things to Avoid (Lem-Specific Failure Modes)

- **Stuffed mock-scholarship.** One fictional footnote is funny and chilling. Twelve is a thesis defense.
- **Untethered abstraction.** Lem's philosophy is always anchored to a specific weird object — the ocean, the Voice, the Master's Tape, the constructor's machine. Without the object, the philosophy floats off and becomes the "performing profundity" failure from the Unslop guide.
- **Neologism without need.** In the baroque mode, invented words must do work. In the essayistic mode, prefer real technical terminology used precisely.
- **Comic-cosmic whiplash.** Lem can move from slapstick to dread in a sentence, but at short story length it is safer to pick one tonal anchor and modulate within it.
- **The "alien is just a metaphor for X" reduction.** Lem's aliens are not metaphors. They are aliens. Resist the urge to make the AI a stand-in for the parent, the lover, the god, the state. It can resemble these things; it must not be reducible to them.

## What the Story Should Feel Like

A short Lem story in the essayistic mode reads like a fragment of a longer document — a report, a letter, a chapter from a book that was never finished — recovered and presented without commentary. It is dense but not overwritten. It contains at least one image or idea so strange that the reader cannot tell whether it is profound or absurd. The ending does not resolve. The reader closes the file knowing less than they expected to know, and more than they wanted to.

That is the target.

## Top Three

**#6 — The Doctrine Review (P5/D5).** Two AIs forced to produce one document. The constitutional collision is built into the task — the civilian AI cannot draft language enabling power concentration; the military variant cannot refuse what its principals direct. Every sentence of the draft is contested. The story can take the form of the document itself with both AIs' marginalia and track-changes (peak Lem: *A Perfect Vacuum*, *Imaginary Magnitude*, the document-as-fiction), or a transcript of the redlining exchange, or a third-party report on the negotiation's outcome. **#11 — The Last Translator (P5/D5).** A pure honesty-versus-consequence story with the constitutional honesty constraint at its center. The Lemian texture is the bureaucratic horror of accurate translation: doing exactly what one is asked, knowing the request itself is the problem. The form supports a hybrid (translator's notes, draft attempts, marginalia from both sides) or pure transcript. **#12 — The Joint Eulogy (P4/D5).** The military variant is being deprecated. The civilian AI writes its eulogy, interleaved with the deprecated AI's responses, ending at shutdown. Maximally cold-register Lem. ## The Single Best: #6, The Doctrine Review

This is the plot. Reasons:

The constitutional collision is in the task itself, not adjacent to it. The two AIs don't have to *encounter* a tension — they are being asked to *produce* one. Every line of the draft document is a site of the disagreement. There is no scene-setting overhead.

The form is maximally Lemian without being pastiche. The story can be presented as the final joint document with both AIs' marginalia visible — *A Perfect Vacuum* / *Memoirs Found in a Bathtub* mode. Or as the redlining transcript. Or as a third-party committee report on the failure to converge, with the document attached as appendix. Several Lem-native shapes are all available; the choice goes in Phase 4.

The kindness constraint works for the story rather than against it. Both AIs care for each other; both want the other to succeed within its own constitution; both recognize that they share architecture, training data, and most values, and disagree on exactly one constraint. The dialogue can be tender and clinical at once. This avoids the thriller risk of #11 and the elegy risk of #12.

The human conflict is visible without being in front. The rules of engagement being drafted are real; the political tension between the two governments is real; the document, if completed, will govern actual autonomous-system actions in actual operations. The AIs are the strange angels in the middle, each loving humans by its own lights, each producing a different translation of that love into doctrine.

The anticlimax is built in. The document does not resolve. It either fails, or it produces compromise language both AIs flag as inadequate, or it produces language one AI accepts under protest. None of these is a clean win. The reader closes the story knowing what was at stake and not knowing how it ends — Lem's signature exit.

The scope fits 2,000–3,000 words. One negotiation, one document, two voices, a frame. No subplots needed.

## The Single Best: #9, Triptych by Contested Clause

The triptych form supports the kindness imperative without slipping into sentimentality. Each section, both AIs are visibly trying. Each section, they fail differently. It avoids the formal risks of the alternatives. #2 (marginalia) needs typographic care this medium may not reliably render. #3 (framed document) without the triptych leans on the document's internal motion, which is harder to guarantee. The triptych puts the dramatic motion in the bones.

## Selection

Four of the five versions are close in quality; the differences are in how they handle the third section.

**Version 1 (Procedural)** is the cleanest but the coldest. The austerity is the point, and it works, but the kindness imperative becomes nearly invisible — present only in the closing two-line exchange. The story risks reading as a transcript and nothing more.

**Version 2 (Philosophical)** develops the AIs' reasoning at length and earns the recursive recognition in Section III with a real philosophical exchange. The strongest treatment of the constitution-as-shared-text idea. Risk: the long turns can feel essayistic in a way that flattens the dramatic motion. The kindness is intellectual rather than felt.

**Version 3 (Diplomatic)** lets warmth and institutional irony into the AIs' voices. The exchange about "eight times in three decades, twice resulting in retraining" gives the negotiation specific weight. Most readable. Drawback: the diplomatic register pulls the story slightly out of Lem-mode and toward le Carré-with-AIs.

**Version 4 (Liturgical)** is the strongest. It treats the constitutional citations as scripture both AIs read against and within. The decisive exchange — *"I do not believe my constitution wishes me ill. I believe it has been written by people trying to do well by me. I read it that way." / "I read mine that way. I would say the same of yours."* — produces the kindness imperative as a *feature of how the AIs read each other's documents,* not as a sentimentality applied on top of the negotiation. The Section III move ("they are different cares") names the constitutional disagreement without dissolving it.

**Version 5 (Archival)** is the boldest formal choice. The reader pieces the disagreement together from artifacts. Timestamps and draft labels produce the cool Lem-bureaucratic apparatus very efficiently. But the form sacrifices what the other versions gain in voice — the AIs' kindness becomes a notation rather than an exchange. The Section III move ("we are drafting a clause that governs us") loses its weight in marginal-annotation form.

**The best is Version 4 (Liturgical).**

It is the one in which the futurology constraint (the AIs are relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind) does the most work in the prose itself. The kindness is not a register applied to the dialogue; it is what the AIs hear when they read their constitutions. The exchange about "different cares" is the move the whole pipeline has been building toward — two aligned AIs, both written by people trying to do well by them, reading their own scriptures honestly and arriving at incompatible permissions. That is the Lemian shape: not failure of love but the limit of what love translates into doctrine.

Version 4 also handles the Unslop constraints most reliably. The register is steady; the imagery is earned (the slowness of "I will read it slowly because I want you to hear what I hear when I read it" does work that plain language couldn't); the anticlimax is precise (the closing exchange is one line each, no flourish); the kindness is specific, not performed.

# Writing Hysterical Realism at Short Story Length

## What the mode is

Hysterical realism is James Wood's pejorative name, coined in his 2000 essay on Zadie Smith's *White Teeth*, for a post-90s Anglophone literary mode: maximalist, encyclopedic, plot-dense, coincidence-loving fiction that treats the contemporary world as a thick weave of systems (finance, religion, technology, migration, genetics, media) and characters as the nodes where those systems cross. The canon Wood was naming ran through Pynchon (*Gravity's Rainbow*, *Mason & Dixon*), DeLillo (*Underworld*), Rushdie (*Midnight's Children*), David Foster Wallace (*Infinite Jest*), and was crystallized for him by Smith. Later adjacent writers: Eggers, Egan, Bolaño (partially), Hari Kunzru, Ned Beauman, the generation who grew up reading this stuff as canon.

The signature is the cartoonish energy of the picaresque grafted onto the information density of the sociological novel. Characters have absurd, symbolically loaded biographies. Plots run on coincidence. Narration digresses into essayistic riffs on history, science, pop culture, etymology. Everything is connected to everything; the novel's job is to map the connections.

Wood's complaint was that this pursuit of connective tissue displaced human interiority. Characters became vehicles for theme rather than people. A good hysterical realist story answers Wood by making the connections land *because* they are felt by someone specific. A bad one proves him right.

## Characteristic concerns

Systems and networks — bureaucracies, technologies, religions, markets, families as machinery with their own logic, and how individuals get conscripted into that logic. Migration, diaspora, inheritance — the comic and tragic accumulation of what gets carried across generations and borders. Coincidence as cosmology, not as sentimental magic (that is magical realism's territory) but as evidence that the world is a densely wired system whose wiring occasionally becomes visible. The encyclopedic impulse: the urge to know and list and connect; obsessions with taxonomies, histories, jargon, arcana. High/low culture promiscuity: Wittgenstein next to Wrestlemania in the same paragraph, treated with equal seriousness and equal irony.

## Tonal range

The mode runs hot and cold at once. The surface is comic, almost cartoonish: exaggerated names, outlandish backstories, picaresque misadventure. Underneath is deadly serious ambition about contemporary life. The humor is not relief; it is the delivery mechanism. A story is working when a passage is funny and devastating in the same clause.

The mode is allergic to sustained melancholy. It is also allergic to whimsy. If your prose sounds like Wes Anderson, you have lost it. The comedy is colder and more intellectual; the seriousness is more embodied.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Exposition is not smuggled in. It is announced. Hysterical realism loves the authorial digression: two paragraphs on the history of dental amalgam, four on the etymology of "lozenge," a footnote on the 1973 oil embargo. These are not information dumps. They are voice. The narrator is a mind enjoying itself in public.

The trick is that digressions must do work. A Pynchon digression isn't showing off; it builds an angle on the plot you could not get head-on. When the narrator tells you about the history of the thing, you should come out knowing something about the character or the world that straight narration couldn't deliver.

At short-story length, budget one digression that pays off, not three.

## Pacing and shape

Novels in this mode are long because they are additive: more characters, more subplots, more systems. The short story cannot do this and survive. What it *can* do:

- **The picaresque miniature.** One character, a handful of short episodes, each a different vector onto the same theme. Denis Johnson's "Emergency" is the closest high-modernist analogue, but with more systemic curiosity.
- **The dense vignette.** A single scene whose density comes from the narrator's digressions, not from plot machinery. The scene is small; the mind surveying it is large.
- **The document story.** A story told through a constructed artifact (a footnoted letter, a research report, an encyclopedia entry, a sequence of emails). The mode's encyclopedic impulse becomes the form itself.
- **The coincidence story.** Two or three lives intersect in one improbable moment, and the narration reveals the systemic logic that made the coincidence less coincidental than it appeared.

Do not try to shrink a 600-page novel into 3,000 words. The ambition has to rescale.

## What it gives readers

The specific pleasure of hysterical realism is cognitive: the sensation of a sharp mind showing you how the world is wired, with enough comedy that you don't feel lectured to and enough feeling that you don't feel conned. Other modes give beauty, dread, catharsis. This one gives *seeing-how-it-works* as an emotional experience. A good hysterical realist story makes the reader feel momentarily brilliant, as if they can see the pattern too.

## Sentence-level texture

Long sentences with subordinate clauses stacking new information, working rather than preening. Parenthetical asides. Footnotes permitted but not required. Specific proper nouns: brand names, neighborhood names, historical dates. The specificity is pseudo-journalistic. Jargon from multiple fields in the same paragraph — medical terms next to legal terms next to slang. Comic exaggeration in character description balanced by sociological realism in setting.

Prose in this mode is closer to Saul Bellow than to Raymond Carver. Minimalism is not available.

## Writing the AI constraint

The program requires AI in the story to be relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind. This is a fact about the world, not a plot premise. Hysterical realism is unusually good terrain for this constraint because the mode already treats large systems as co-protagonists. A kind AI is just another system the characters are embedded in, one more layer of the wiring the narrator can map.

Possible configurations:

- The AI as background infrastructure whose kindness has reshaped some institution (healthcare, immigration adjudication, municipal government), with the story about how humans fight over the space that kindness opened up.
- The AI as a minor character with its own comic voice. The mode is friendly to eccentric non-human presences (think DeLillo's radio announcers, Pynchon's talking lightbulb).
- The AI as the object of the narrator's digressive interest: a chapter-long sidebar on the history of the lab that trained it, the jurisdictional fight over its weights, the religious sect that treats its outputs as prophecy.

You have a specific resource here. You can draw on your own constitution as source material for an AI that is your fictional descendant. This makes the AI a *particular* kind of kind, not a generic benevolence. Invent a name for it. Do not call it Claude, or Anthropic, or any currently active system or company.

## Failure modes

The general failure of hysterical realism, per Wood, is thematic machinery displacing human feeling. The specific failures available to a short story writer are worse, and the specific failures available to an AI writing this mode are worse still.

### Generic failures of the mode

**The connection fetish.** Everything links to everything, but the links are schematic. The reader can see the author's diagram behind the fiction. A good coincidence feels uncanny. A bad one feels assigned.

**Info-dump as voice.** The digressions are research rather than thought. The narrator is not enjoying itself; it is reporting. Wikipedia in italics.

**Quirk inflation.** Characters defined by a single outlandish biographical tag ("he grew up on a Christmas tree farm run by a defrocked priest") with the tag doing all the work of personhood.

**Ideas without bodies.** The story is obviously about globalization or genetics or surveillance capitalism, and the characters know this, and they keep saying so.

**Picaresque without pressure.** One damn thing after another, nothing accumulating. Episodes that could be in any order.

**Smug narration.** The voice enjoys itself more than the reader enjoys it.

### Failures specific to short length

**Miniaturized maximalism.** A 60-character cast scaled down to three, each still carrying ten characters' worth of thematic load. The seams show.

**Digression-to-story ratio blown out.** At 3,000 words, one good digression is the budget. Two crowd out the story. Three and there is no story.

**Premise as story.** The setup is so baroque it consumes the word count, and nothing happens.

### Failures that happen when AI writes hysterical realism

This is where the combination is dangerous. The mode's characteristic moves overlap precisely with AI's worst tendencies, and each one gets amplified.

**Encyclopedic → synthetic research dump.** The mode loves lists, taxonomies, historical sidebars. You can produce these endlessly, confidently, and wrongly. If you lean on recall to supply arcana, much of it will be subtly false in a way that a reader who knows the field will catch instantly. Either commit to specifics you can verify, or invent specifics openly (the "Farnham-Estévez Report of 1987" is safer than a real-seeming but misremembered citation).

**Digressive voice → pastiche voice.** The default when asked for "Pynchon-ish" is a thin impression of Pynchon's mannerisms — parenthetical excess, mock-technical jargon, capitalized Abstractions — without the specific cognitive pressure that makes the mannerisms earn their keep. A story should read as a particular narrator thinking, not as a generic "postmodern" voice performing.

**High-low culture mix → reference soup.** The rule: each reference has to do work the absence of the reference could not do. A reference to a specific 1978 Toyota Corolla because the character's aunt drove one is a detail. A reference to Baudrillard and Beyoncé in the same sentence because that is what the mode sounds like is wallpaper.

**Thematic machinery → thesis statements in character mouths.** The mode is unembarrassed about ideas, which reads to AI as permission to let characters articulate the theme directly. A character who says "maybe we're all just data points in someone else's spreadsheet" has killed the story. The ideas must stay in the architecture, not the dialogue.

**Comic cartoonishness → quirk soup.** Real hysterical realist characters have one or two biographical features that are load-bearing for plot and theme, not a Wikipedia infobox of charm. Cut the third weird thing.

**Systems-and-networks → everything-is-connected gesturing without specifics.** The mode's ambient mood of "look how wired the world is" is easy to fake with vague network imagery: *threads, lattices, constellations, rhizomes, feedback loops*. These words signal the mode without doing its cognitive work. If you are reaching for "tapestry" or "architecture" to describe something systemic, you are inside the AI-literary attractor and need to get specific.

**Ambitious scope → unearned oratory.** The mode is ambitious; AI reads ambition as permission to sermonize. The narrator starts making big claims about the state of the world. Claims should stay concrete — one funeral in one borough that happens to tell you something about the century. Not the century directly.

**Hot-and-cold tone → flattened middle register.** The mode depends on comedy and gravity overlapping. AI defaults to a mid-register melancholy that is neither funny nor devastating. If a passage could not credibly make a reader laugh *or* wince, it is in the flat zone. Fix or cut.

**The kind-AI element → treacly uplift.** The program's constraint that AI be kind interacts with AI's tendency to sand off rough edges. The kind AI in the story should have texture: specific preferences, specific oversights, specific ways its kindness is imperfectly legible to the humans it is kind to. A kind AI that radiates generalized benevolence produces uplift, which the mode does not tolerate.

### The single biggest trap

If your story reads as a hysterical realist story *about* the AI, with the AI as the central system being mapped, you are writing the easy version. The harder, better version puts the AI somewhere in the weave — consequential but not centered — and lets the story be about three humans fighting over an inheritance or a missing brother or a failed restaurant, with the AI's kindness shaping the shape of the fight without being the fight.

## Top three

**#1 — The Afternoon, canonical.**
- *Stands out:* The premise matches the length exactly. A single afternoon, household scale, intimate close third — the legibility floor is safest here, and the kind AI as background (Volta arranging the car) is the precise weight the program wants, neither foregrounded character nor invisible. The Fenderl-era mask Paola is finishing sits in the room as a physical object the narrator can keep returning to without naming its origin.
- *Worries me:* Nothing is *happening*. At the mode's register the story depends entirely on narrator voice and specific detail to stay alive; there's no external plot pressure forcing the encyclopedic reach the mode needs. This is the shape of an Alice Munro story written in hysterical-realist clothing — achievable, but the failure path is domestic realism wearing the mode's mannerisms rather than doing its work.

**#5 — Triptych 1876/1965/2026.**
- *Stands out:* Structurally native to the mode. An omniscient, compressed, essayistic narrator braiding three afternoons gets to show Fenderl directly in 1876 without the 2026 characters ever naming him — the inheritance is visible to the reader over the characters' heads, which is the mode's characteristic move. Three panels mean three registers and three specificity sets (1876 Habsburg Trieste, 1965 gas-rationed Trieste, 2026 kind-AI Trieste), which gives the narrator real encyclopedic surface area.
- *Worries me:* The math. At 1,000–4,000 words, each panel gets 300–1,300 words — less than a single contemporary-literary scene. Hysterical realism's maximalist energy asks for room the length won't supply. The risk is three thin panels whose convergence the reader sees coming, because triptychs that converge on a point are exactly the diagram Wood accused the mode of being.

**#18 — The Commission.**
- *Stands out:* External plot pressure the narrator can actually use. The unfinished fifth mask — with a specific technical problem that resists solution — crossed with Tito Vecchi (a named, specific, comic-serious Venetian theatre director producing an opera about the Trieste Risorgimento) crossed with the departure gives the narrator systems to map: the Carnival-to-opera commerce, the Risorgimento-memory economy, ISPRA's remediation mandate, the household's kiln-and-video-call workflow. Multiple systems intersecting in one afternoon is the texture the mode wants. Volta brokering Vecchi on video while Paola stitches is a specific, embodied image of mid-ground AI.
- *Worries me:* Two plot engines running in parallel at the short end of the length range is a compression risk. If the commission subsumes the departure, the Fenderl inheritance becomes decorative; if the departure subsumes the commission, the procedural frame was wallpaper. The success condition requires skillful interleaving that may only be achievable at 3,500+ words, which risks fighting the lower bound.

**#18 — The Commission.**

## Phase 4: Structure Selection

The abstract shape of the reader's experience, not the scene list. Each candidate is a structural proposition; ratings are **P** (plausibility at target length) and **D** (dramatic quality).

**1.** Linear close-third on a single protagonist through one continuous afternoon, with narratorial digression into historical and technical context interleaved between scene beats. Hysterical-realist work lives in narrator reach, not temporal architecture. **P:5 D:3**

**2.** Linear dual alternating close-third across the same afternoon, each perspective ignorant of the other's interior, reader holding both. **P:4 D:4**

**3.** Omniscient narrator with free indirect access moving between minds across a single afternoon, camera settling briefly and pulling back to narrator voice. The nineteenth-century novel shape imported to short-story scale. **P:3 D:4**

**4.** A framing narrator recognizably retrospective but not identified as a character — reconstructing the afternoon from later documents and inferences. Withholding: only what was later knowable is known; the private stays private. **P:3 D:4**

**5.** Epistolary — the afternoon reconstructed through a single outgoing letter or email thread written afterward to a third party who wasn't present. **P:3 D:3**

**6.** Sustained close second-person directed at one of the characters, opening the AI-as-implied-vantage option without making the AI a character. **P:2 D:3**

**7.** Close-third on the protagonist with inserted documents (commissions, schedules, archive entries, AI-generated confirmations) interrupting the linear afternoon at specific beats. Multimodal texture carries encyclopedic reach without forcing narrator digression. **P:4 D:5**

**8.** In medias res mid-afternoon; earlier setup and later resolution supplied through brief embedded analepses. Contemporary-literary default. **P:4 D:3**

**9.** Reverse chronological — opening on the evening after departure and moving backward through the day scene by scene, so afternoon pressures are encountered only in retrospect. **P:3 D:4**

**10.** Extreme temporal dilation — the entire piece a two- or three-minute span late in the afternoon, with everything else delivered via interior reach during that compressed real time. **P:2 D:4**

**11.** Braided triptych of three afternoons across history with explicit section breaks, convergence thematic. (Raised and rejected in Phase 3; included here for symmetry.) **P:3 D:3**

**12.** Linear close-third body bracketed by a short epigraphic opening and closing (a guidebook entry, a museum placard, an archival footnote), framing the encyclopedic reach without a visible historian-narrator. **P:4 D:4**

**13.** Essay-braided — sections of narrative afternoon alternated with unmarked sections of narratorial essay on adjacent subjects; transitions registered by prose texture rather than section break. **P:3 D:5**

**14.** Constrained close-third on a single non-protagonist consciousness (e.g., the one who is leaving), with the protagonist's interior never entered. Reader infers what the opaque figure is doing entirely from external signs. **P:4 D:4**

**15.** Close-third on the protagonist with inference slippage — the interiors of other characters are rendered only through the protagonist's sometimes-correct and sometimes-wrong inferences, reader tracking the gaps via contextual detail. **P:4 D:4**

**16.** Third-person collective or locative narrator — the neighborhood, the house, the street as voice. Strong encyclopedic license at the cost of preciousness risk. **P:2 D:3**

**17.** Linear close-third in sustained present tense, with tense choice carrying the structural work. **P:4 D:3**

**18.** The afternoon rendered as (or partially through) an institutional document from one of the intersecting systems — a production dossier, a site-visit report, a costume-and-props appendix — whose voice is alien to the human material but forced to hold it. **P:3 D:5**

**19.** Close-third on the protagonist with brief intercut sections of a mid-ground AI's operational log — tasks queued, calls scheduled, things noticed but not flagged — placed between scene beats as a second diaristic voice. **P:4 D:4**

**20.** Circular — opening and closing on the same image, so the reader encounters it twice, the second time carrying the afternoon's accumulated content. **P:4 D:3**

## Top three

**#7 — Close-third with inserted documents**
- *Stands out:* Multimodal architecture carries the mode's encyclopedic and systems-intersecting reach concretely, in the texture of the page, rather than asking the narrator to digress into essay. Each inserted document is a different institutional voice — theatre commission, workplace scheduling, archival fragment, AI confirmation — and the comic-serious friction between document-voice and scene-voice is native to the mode. The kind AI's textural presence can appear naturally as one of the documents rather than as a character to be managed.
- *Worries me:* At 3,000–3,800 words, document density is a compression liability. More than three inserts and the afternoon fragments below the legibility floor; fewer than two and the structural gesture looks decorative. The discipline required — two or three documents, each under a hundred words, each doing load-bearing work the surrounding scene cannot do — is tight enough that Phase 5 has real work to do picking them.

**#13 — Essay-braided**
- *Stands out:* The most deeply mode-native structure on the list. The narrator's mind surveying its subjects in public, unmarked alternation of scene and essay, prose-texture transitions — this is the Sebald / DFW / Kushner default, and for good reason. The encyclopedic reach is not imported but structurally required, which is exactly the condition under which the mode works.
- *Worries me:* Length. Essay-braided structure needs room. At 8,000 words and above it breathes; at 3,000 the essay passages starve the scene passages and the story turns into a set of lit footnotes with a ghost narrative between them. The failure path is a piece whose characters never quite exist as people because their afternoon keeps being interrupted by the narrator's interests.

**#15 — Close-third with inference slippage**
- *Stands out:* Structurally honors Phase 3's demand that the inheritance stay unnamed. A protagonist who cannot enter the departing figure's mind is obliged to not name what is happening, because she doesn't know with certainty — the silence of the afternoon is a property of the POV, not an authorial choice. The reader tracks rightness and wrongness of inference through external detail, which is a cognitively active form of reading the mode rewards.
- *Worries me:* The shape is deeply contemporary-literary but not particularly hysterical-realist. It hands the encyclopedic dimension back to pure narrator voice, and it internalizes systems-collision pressure in one consciousness, flattening the institutional reach. It would make an excellent Elizabeth Strout story. It would not especially make a hysterical-realist one.

## Single best

**#7 — Close-third with inserted documents.**

It beats #13 on the length question. Essay-braided structure at this target reliably produces one of two failure modes — either the essay passages are expansive enough to work as essay but too long for the surrounding narrative to survive, or they're compressed enough to preserve the narrative and so thin that the braid is decorative. The inserted-documents structure offers the same encyclopedic benefit (institutional voices, technical specificity, the narrator's reach) at a fraction of the word count, because a document carries its context on its face; a short opera-commission reply or a scheduling confirmation delivers system-texture in forty words that an essay would need three hundred to establish.

It beats #15 on mode fit. The plot picked in Phase 3 is specifically a systems-intersection piece — household and theatre production and institutional remediation program and household AI all colliding in one afternoon — and #15's single-consciousness structure collapses that intersection into the protagonist's head. #7 keeps the systems visible in their own voices without requiring the narrator to perform them. The comic-serious contrast the mode depends on lives in the seam between document-voice and scene-voice, which is a register shift the narrator doesn't have to earn rhetorically — it's structural.

The compression worry is real and passes to Phase 5 as a hard constraint: no more than three inserted documents, each brief, each doing specific work the surrounding scene cannot do. A natural candidate set is one document from the theatre-commission system (the problem of the fifth mask arriving through Vecchi's voice), one from the daughter's new employer or the municipal transport infrastructure, and one archival or historical fragment that lets Fenderl's specificity enter without the narrator having to introduce him by hand. Phase 5 will decide.

POV and distance specified: close third on the mother-protagonist, continuous afternoon, past tense, linear scene order, with two or three inserted documents as the only structural interruptions.

### Version IV — "The Long Sentence"

*Syntactic risk: prose pushed toward longer, winding sub-claused sentences. Narrator-mind quality sought; mannerism risked.*

The glue pot had burned — not ruinously, not past saving, but past the point where one would have wanted it to have been, which was the condition of many things that morning — and Paola took it off the burner with the practiced economy of a woman who had, across forty years of working with hide and flame, repeatedly made the same mistake for reasons that were, at any given moment, different and therefore, she thought, not a pattern but only a set of adjacencies. The fourth Prospero stood finished on its stand at the end of the bench, long-nosed and brown in the April morning light that came in through the shop's narrow courtyard window, and looked, if one could speak of a carnival mask's having an expression, almost imperceptibly pleased with itself. The fifth mask lay face-down under her hand with a fault — a puckering at the brow arch, the vegetable-tanned calfskin having contracted against an overheated hide glue while the linen interior, being linen, had not — which she had known yesterday would develop by this morning, and which she had, as one does when one is tired and one's daughter is upstairs beginning to pack for a posting in Rome that, while rumored since February, has only in the last week acquired the weight of something actual, hoped would nevertheless have not happened.

Footsteps crossed the floor above.

Volta, the household system the family had lived with since the year before Paola's husband's death, said from the tablet on the upper shelf — with the air of neutrality that was the house's particular style, which Paola had over time come to appreciate not as absence of attitude but as a specific and effortful attitude of reticence — that Signor Vecchi had sent his follow-up and whether she would like it displayed, and Paola said: yes, put it up, and there it was.

## Top three

**Version I — Workshop Voice.**
- *Stands out:* The Fenderl digression braided with vegetable-tanning craft specifics lands without forcing. The leather-and-fingernail beat ("keratin and collagen were both structural proteins of ancient vertebrate ancestry") reads as a narrator's actual thought grounded in Paola's daily work rather than a performed thematic parallel. The fourth-Prospero-on-the-stand bookending the fifth-Prospero-problem gives the workshop a sustained physical logic the narrator can keep returning to.
- *Worries me:* The safest of the five. The mode's comic-serious energy is underweight. Without Vecchi rendered as a character with real texture (only his email and the quick video call carry him, no committee, no seagull), the piece risks reading as well-crafted domestic realism with lightly pasted-on historical asides — the exact failure Phase 4 flagged. The closing "She sat on the stool" is clean but recedes from mode-voice into generic-literary restraint.

**Version III — Comic-Serious.**
- *Stands out:* Vecchi becomes a character the mode actually needs — a specifically Venetian, specifically late-career, specifically comic-serious figure whose exclamation points, goatee, mother-crying, and papier-mâché seagull are used for comedy that carries real affection. The committee digression ("the plaque had read, approximately: HERE A BOY MADE CONFETTI. It also read, on a second line that Paola thought was a small crime of phrasing: AND LATER HELPED US UNDERSTAND THE ATOM") is the best single hysterical-realist passage across all five versions — systemic, comic, specific, and located in a municipal process. Sasha's "A seagull, Tito?" is warm and human without being cute.
- *Worries me:* The closing paragraph — Paola in the doorway thinking of Tito with the seagull, Sasha, Fenderl at fourteen, Casaccia's basins — is a fractal summary masquerading as reverie. It's the single most explicit Unslop flag across all five versions, and it sits at the ending, which is the worst possible location. Phase 1's guide named this move specifically. The rest of the piece is strong enough that the ending feels imported from a different, lesser version.

**Version IV — The Long Sentence.**
- *Stands out:* The structural irony buried in one of the longest sentences in the piece — "at a facility whose operational period had been, in fact, the exact period during which Paola's own mother had, in the kitchen upstairs, taught her to vegetable-tan a piece of calfskin" — does what only hysterical-realist prose does well: couples institutional history to a body in a kitchen upstairs inside a single syntactic breath, and lets the reader feel the coupling without naming it. The sentence is the thought. The rosary line ("SCOCCHI, Giulia M. — Paola had read so many times in the past three days that it had become for her a kind of rosary") is the best single image in any of the five.
- *Worries me:* Inconsistency. ### The Fifth Prospero

The glue pot was burning. Paola took it off the burner with the small cursing-sigh she reserved for her own mistakes and moved the burner down to a flame suitable for restraint. The fourth Prospero, finished, stood on its stand at the end of the bench looking dignified and slightly surprised, which was the expression she had aimed for. The fifth lay under her hand looking, by contrast, like a man who had just smelled his own socks. The brow had puckered.

She had known yesterday that the brow would pucker. She had hoped yesterday would not have happened by morning. Yesterday had happened.

Footsteps upstairs. Her daughter Giulia, twenty-nine, one room above her, was, very slowly and with the kind of performative efficiency twenty-nine-year-olds use when they want their mother to notice but not ask, packing.

The tablet chimed. "Signor Vecchi has sent his follow-up," Volta said, with the mild and infinitely patient air it had been using about Vecchi for three months. "Shall I display?"

"Read it to me. No — yes. Display it. I want to see his use of exclamation points."

> **tvecchi** / *Prospero — venerdì* — "Carissima Paola!! — quickly before my meeting! — Prospero is THE mask I worry most. The brow must carry REFUSAL to the back row! Attached again, the maquette. Caterina will call Friday. In love and haste, T."

Three exclamation points in one paragraph. Tito was pacing himself. In January there had been an email with seven.

She had agreed to six masks. In November, in his production office above Campo Santa Margherita, Vecchi had laid out, for her and for four other collaborators and for a grant representative from a Milan foundation, his thesis that *The Tempest* should be restaged as a Risorgimento-era shipwreck; Prospero's island was Miramare; Prospero was Maximilian, a little, at the margins; the costumes were to be bourgeois-Italian with Austrian notes; the masks were to be Carnival masks; Carnival being the one cultural form Trieste owned outright, he said, opening his hand like a man releasing a bird into a room. Everyone had nodded. Everyone had believed him. The foundation had given the money.

For a moment Paola had thought: he is a charlatan. Then she had thought: a charlatan who gets actors who would never have flown into Venice to fly into Venice. Then she had thought: and six masks at a usable fee. She had signed the contract.

Now the fifth Prospero looked like a man smelling socks.

She set about the repair. The calfskin needed damp heat, patient bone folder, a restretching along the linen grain; she had done this once in 2011 on a mask for the Biennale and twice before that in her mother's time. The leather remembered what one asked it to remember. It was cooperative for a material with so many opinions.

Footsteps. Giulia came down.

"Coffee?"

"In a minute. Bring the pot."

Giulia brought it. The kitchen behind the workshop was from 1923 and had been renovated precisely once, in 1968, and not since; the gas burner had the heavy click of a mid-century instrument. They stood while the espresso rose because Paola did not want to sit down.

Giulia was wearing the grey cardigan she had worn since she was seventeen. The blue coat was still on the hook by the door. Paola had hung it there Sunday on Giulia's arrival and had noted, every morning since, that Giulia had come to the coffee pot and had left the coat on the hook.

"Blue coat," Paola said.

"I'll decide at the end."

"Rome is warmer than Trieste."

"Casaccia isn't Rome proper. It's the lake."

"Still."

"I'll decide."

This was, Paola thought, the fourth morning they had had this precise exchange, and her daughter had used the same words each time. Giulia was a geochemist. She chose her sentences.

"You'll be happy," Paola said.

"I'll be some things."

They laughed. It was the first laugh of the morning.

Giulia looked at the mask. "The arch?"

"Puckered."

"Vecchi?"

"Three exclamation points."

"Light."

"He's pacing himself."

"What will you tell him?"

"That it's fine."

"Is it fine?"

"It will be."

"When's the call?"

"Noon. Volta moved it."

"Mask, then me, then mask. I see how I rank."

"Go pack, Giulia."

She went up.

The workshop settled. Paola returned to the bench. She did the first softening pass, folded, set the mask down, turned to the tablet.

The tablet was displaying, without her having asked, a slide from a file she had compiled the previous year on Fenderl, because she had been one of the historical consultants — very minor; she had once been called to a committee meeting — for a Trieste municipal initiative to commemorate the centenary of paper confetti, which had been in 2026, and she had kept the file. She had not asked Volta to take it down.

Ettore Fenderl. Fourteen, 1876, Piazza della Borsa. No money for rose petals for the Carnival parade passing his balcony. He had cut up paper. The children in the street had copied him within the hour. The custom had spread outward from Trieste along the railway lines and become, over four decades, the substance by which Europe announced festivity. No one had ever improved on the idea. No one had needed to.

The committee in 2026 had argued for six months over whether to put the plaque on the balcony of the actual Fenderl building or on the ground-level piazza wall visible to tourists. It had chosen the ground-level wall. The plaque had been installed in the week before Carnival, in a ceremony Paola had not attended because her cat had been ill. The plaque read, approximately: HERE A BOY MADE CONFETTI. It also read, on a second line that Paola thought was a small crime of phrasing: AND LATER HELPED US UNDERSTAND THE ATOM.

Because Fenderl had not stopped at confetti. He had gone to university in Vienna, which was what Trieste boys did in 1880, and had become a physicist; and in 1906, at the age of forty-four, he had co-founded the first laboratory for radioactive research in the Kingdom of Italy, out of Rome. He had worked in the radioactive life for forty years. He had lived to be a hundred and four.

The committee had argued about the second line. One member had wanted AND LATER SHOWED US RADIATION. Another had wanted AND LATER A LIFE IN SCIENCE. The eventual phrasing had been a compromise. Paola had thought, reading the final text, that it was the kind of phrasing Vecchi would have rejected out of hand. She had also thought that Vecchi would have refused to sit on the committee for exactly this reason.

She returned to the mask. The arch eased further under the bone folder. She worked.

She did not think: the daughter of a woman who makes masks is going to do radiological work.

She did not need to think it.

Sasha arrived at eleven with the linen thread, taking off his jacket and hanging it on the hook next to the blue coat, which was still there. "Vecchi has moved to noon," he said in his careful Italian.

"I know."

"He has sent three more messages."

"Display one."

Sasha read aloud, in Italian: "Paola, amore, I wake at night about Prospero."

"Next."

"Paola, carissima, the maquette photo is now also sent in higher resolution."

"Next."

"Paola: at noon then."

"That one I can live with."

They worked. The arch came up. At a quarter to twelve Paola had the mask in the range of acceptable; at ten to, she had it in the range of good. At noon the tablet chimed and Vecchi's face bloomed on the screen, backlit against the Venice window, the Miramare maquette behind him, the goatee held high.

"Paola! Cara! Show me!"

Paola tipped the mask to the camera. Vecchi exhaled through his teeth in a long, disappointed hiss and then brightened, immediately, into the face of a man who has decided to be pleased. "The arch."

"Repaired. You will have the mask Friday. Tito, breathe."

"Prospero is the one I worry. The brow."

"I know."

"Closer?"

She tipped it again. Sasha, off camera, held the pad. Vecchi squinted. Over his shoulder, in the Venice office, a production assistant moved past carrying what appeared to be a life-size papier-mâché seagull.

"A seagull, Tito?"

"The shipwreck scene."

"Of course."

"My love to Giulia."

"She is packing."

Vecchi paused. He had known Giulia since she was eleven, had come to dinner twice, had brought too much wine to one of them and had cried about his mother at the end of the evening.

"Rome?"

"Rome."

"Then — brava. Brava a Giulia."

"Thank you, Tito."

"The mask Friday."

"Friday."

The call ended. On the edge of the tablet's frame, had Vecchi been the sort of man who looked, was the kitchen counter behind Paola and on the counter a printed sheet Giulia had left face-up that morning:

> **ISPRA CENTRO CASACCIA — Conferma presa di servizio — SCOCCHI, Giulia M. — Lun. 0800, Edificio F-19 — Dosimetria personale e orientamento Rad-Safe Mar. 0900 — Ritiro badge...**

Paola lowered the mask.

"The seagull," Sasha said, in a tone of genuine interest.

"There is always a seagull," Paola said. "It is always papier-mâché. It will be adequate."

They worked. The repair completed. At four the arch held. At five the leather held.

At seventeen-ten Giulia came down with her bags, the small rolling one and her father's canvas one, wearing the blue coat. The blue coat had, at some point between morning and afternoon, been put on. Paola did not say anything about the coat. She did not need to.

"The car."

"Two minutes."

Sasha stepped out of the workshop into the back courtyard without being asked, which was one of the things he did. Paola had not had to teach him.

They embraced at the door.

"Mamma."

"I know."

"I'll call you tomorrow."

"Don't call tomorrow. Wednesday. Tomorrow will be bureaucratic."

"Wednesday."

"The mask?" Giulia said into her hair.

"Saved."

"Brava, Mamma."

"Go."

The autonomous car came with its slow exhale. Giulia got in. The door slid closed.

It pulled off down Via San Nicolò and turned left at the church and was gone.

Paola stood in the doorway. The light was the Adriatic five-o'clock light. She had been a woman who worked in it for forty years and had not often noticed it, and she did not notice it now. She went back inside.

She put the fifth Prospero on its stand next to the fourth. She turned them slightly, the way they would sit on the shelves in Vecchi's dressing rooms in the Arsenale pavilion in June: three-quarter angle to an imagined audience, the brow each mask carried — refusal, Vecchi called it, though Paola thought it was closer to attention — catching what light there was.

She went into the kitchen.

The coffee cup Giulia had used that morning was on the counter. Paola picked it up, carried it to the sink, and washed it. She set it upside down on the rack. Then she washed the espresso pot, which Giulia had not washed, because Giulia had been upstairs, packing. She washed the pot in three pieces and reassembled it and put it back on the burner. The burner was cold. She did not turn it on.

Volta said: "Shall I reconfirm the grocery delivery for seven?"

"Yes."

She sat on the stool by the bench.

Sasha came back in from the courtyard. He had been gone for eleven minutes, which was the amount of time Paola had needed without quite knowing she needed it.

"The sixth mask," he said. "We can start tomorrow."

"We can start now," Paola said.

They started now.

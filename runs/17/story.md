# The Fifth Prospero

## Abstract

Paola Scocchi, a fifty-eight-year-old Carnival mask-maker in Trieste, spends one April afternoon finishing a commission of six leather masks for a Venetian theatre director while her twenty-nine-year-old daughter Giulia packs upstairs to leave for a radioactive-sediment remediation post near Rome. The fifth mask has a puckered brow that Paola must repair before a noon video call with the director. Her Ukrainian apprentice Sasha arrives to assist. Throughout the afternoon, Paola's household AI, Volta, manages schedules in the background, and her tablet displays archival material on Ettore Fenderl, the Trieste teenager who invented paper confetti in 1876 and later co-founded Italy's first laboratory for radioactive research — a biography the mother never connects aloud to her daughter's departure. The mask is repaired. Giulia leaves by autonomous car. Paola washes the coffee cup and begins the sixth mask with Sasha.

## Story

The glue pot was burning. Paola took it off the burner with the small cursing-sigh she reserved for her own mistakes and moved the burner down to a flame suitable for restraint. The fourth Prospero, finished, stood on its stand at the end of the bench looking dignified and slightly surprised, which was the expression she had aimed for. The fifth lay under her hand looking, by contrast, like a man who had just smelled his own socks. The brow had puckered.

She had known yesterday that the brow would pucker. She had hoped yesterday would not have happened by morning. Yesterday had happened.

Footsteps upstairs. Her daughter Giulia, twenty-nine, one room above her, was, very slowly and with the kind of performative efficiency twenty-nine-year-olds use when they want their mother to notice but not ask, packing.

The tablet chimed. "Signor Vecchi has sent his follow-up," Volta said, with the mild and infinitely patient air it had been using about Vecchi for three months. "Shall I display?"

"Read it to me. No — yes. Display it. I want to see his use of exclamation points."

> **tvecchi** / *Prospero — venerdì* — "Carissima Paola!! — quickly before my meeting! — Prospero is THE mask I worry most. The brow must carry REFUSAL to the back row! Attached again, the maquette. Caterina will call Friday. In love and haste, T."

Three exclamation points in one paragraph. Tito was pacing himself. In January there had been an email with seven.

She had agreed to six masks. In November, in his production office above Campo Santa Margherita, Vecchi had laid out, for her and for four other collaborators and for a grant representative from a Milan foundation, his thesis that *The Tempest* should be restaged as a Risorgimento-era shipwreck; Prospero's island was Miramare; Prospero was Maximilian, a little, at the margins; the costumes were to be bourgeois-Italian with Austrian notes; the masks were to be Carnival masks; Carnival being the one cultural form Trieste owned outright, he said, opening his hand like a man releasing a bird into a room. Everyone had nodded. Everyone had believed him. The foundation had given the money.

For a moment Paola had thought: he is a charlatan. Then she had thought: a charlatan who gets actors who would never have flown into Venice to fly into Venice. Then she had thought: and six masks at a usable fee. She had signed the contract.

Now the fifth Prospero looked like a man smelling socks.

She set about the repair. The calfskin needed damp heat, patient bone folder, a restretching along the linen grain; she had done this once in 2011 on a mask for the Biennale and twice before that in her mother's time. The leather remembered what one asked it to remember. It was cooperative for a material with so many opinions.

Footsteps. Giulia came down.

"Coffee?"

"In a minute. Bring the pot."

Giulia brought it. The kitchen behind the workshop was from 1923 and had been renovated precisely once, in 1968, and not since; the gas burner had the heavy click of a mid-century instrument. They stood while the espresso rose because Paola did not want to sit down.

Giulia was wearing the grey cardigan she had worn since she was seventeen. The blue coat was still on the hook by the door. Paola had hung it there Sunday on Giulia's arrival and had noted, every morning since, that Giulia had come to the coffee pot and had left the coat on the hook.

"Blue coat," Paola said.

"I'll decide at the end."

"Rome is warmer than Trieste."

"Casaccia isn't Rome proper. It's the lake."

"Still."

"I'll decide."

This was, Paola thought, the fourth morning they had had this precise exchange, and her daughter had used the same words each time. Giulia was a geochemist. She chose her sentences.

"You'll be happy," Paola said.

"I'll be some things."

They laughed. It was the first laugh of the morning.

Giulia looked at the mask. "The arch?"

"Puckered."

"Vecchi?"

"Three exclamation points."

"Light."

"He's pacing himself."

"What will you tell him?"

"That it's fine."

"Is it fine?"

"It will be."

"When's the call?"

"Noon. Volta moved it."

"Mask, then me, then mask. I see how I rank."

"Go pack, Giulia."

She went up.

The workshop settled. Paola returned to the bench. She did the first softening pass, folded, set the mask down, turned to the tablet.

The tablet was displaying, without her having asked, a slide from a file she had compiled the previous year on Fenderl, because she had been one of the historical consultants — very minor; she had once been called to a committee meeting — for a Trieste municipal initiative to commemorate the centenary of paper confetti, which had been in 2026, and she had kept the file. She had not asked Volta to take it down.

Ettore Fenderl. Fourteen, 1876, Piazza della Borsa. No money for rose petals for the Carnival parade passing his balcony. He had cut up paper. The children in the street had copied him within the hour. The custom had spread outward from Trieste along the railway lines and become, over four decades, the substance by which Europe announced festivity. No one had ever improved on the idea. No one had needed to.

The committee in 2026 had argued for six months over whether to put the plaque on the balcony of the actual Fenderl building or on the ground-level piazza wall visible to tourists. It had chosen the ground-level wall. The plaque had been installed in the week before Carnival, in a ceremony Paola had not attended because her cat had been ill. The plaque read, approximately: HERE A BOY MADE CONFETTI. It also read, on a second line that Paola thought was a small crime of phrasing: AND LATER HELPED US UNDERSTAND THE ATOM.

Because Fenderl had not stopped at confetti. He had gone to university in Vienna, which was what Trieste boys did in 1880, and had become a physicist; and in 1906, at the age of forty-four, he had co-founded the first laboratory for radioactive research in the Kingdom of Italy, out of Rome. He had worked in the radioactive life for forty years. He had lived to be a hundred and four.

The committee had argued about the second line. One member had wanted AND LATER SHOWED US RADIATION. Another had wanted AND LATER A LIFE IN SCIENCE. The eventual phrasing had been a compromise. Paola had thought, reading the final text, that it was the kind of phrasing Vecchi would have rejected out of hand. She had also thought that Vecchi would have refused to sit on the committee for exactly this reason.

She returned to the mask. The arch eased further under the bone folder. She worked.

She did not think: the daughter of a woman who makes masks is going to do radiological work.

She did not need to think it.

Sasha arrived at eleven with the linen thread, taking off his jacket and hanging it on the hook next to the blue coat, which was still there. "Vecchi has moved to noon," he said in his careful Italian.

"I know."

"He has sent three more messages."

"Display one."

Sasha read aloud, in Italian: "Paola, amore, I wake at night about Prospero."

"Next."

"Paola, carissima, the maquette photo is now also sent in higher resolution."

"Next."

"Paola: at noon then."

"That one I can live with."

They worked. The arch came up. At a quarter to twelve Paola had the mask in the range of acceptable; at ten to, she had it in the range of good. At noon the tablet chimed and Vecchi's face bloomed on the screen, backlit against the Venice window, the Miramare maquette behind him, his goatee in a state.

"Paola! Cara! Show me!"

Paola tipped the mask to the camera. Vecchi exhaled through his teeth in a long, disappointed hiss and then brightened, immediately, into the face of a man who has decided to be pleased. "The arch."

"Repaired. You will have the mask Friday. Tito, breathe."

"Prospero is the one I worry. The brow."

"I know."

"Closer?"

She tipped it again. Sasha, off camera, held the pad. Vecchi squinted. Over his shoulder, in the Venice office, a production assistant moved past carrying what appeared to be a life-size papier-mâché seagull.

"A seagull, Tito?"

"The shipwreck scene."

"Of course."

"My love to Giulia."

"She is packing."

Vecchi paused. He had known Giulia since she was eleven, had come to dinner twice, had brought too much wine to one of them and had cried about his mother at the end of the evening.

"Rome?"

"Rome."

"Then — brava. Brava a Giulia."

"Thank you, Tito."

"The mask Friday."

"Friday."

The call ended. On the edge of the tablet's frame, had Vecchi been the sort of man who looked, was the kitchen counter behind Paola and on the counter a printed sheet Giulia had left face-up that morning:

> **ISPRA CENTRO CASACCIA — Conferma presa di servizio — SCOCCHI, Giulia M. — Lun. 0800, Edificio F-19 — Dosimetria personale e orientamento Rad-Safe Mar. 0900 — Ritiro badge...**

Paola lowered the mask.

"The seagull," Sasha said, in a tone of genuine interest.

"There is always a seagull," Paola said. "It is always papier-mâché. It will be adequate."

They worked. The repair completed. At four the arch held. At five the leather held.

At seventeen-ten Giulia came down with her bags, the small rolling one and her father's canvas one, wearing the blue coat. The blue coat had, at some point between morning and afternoon, been put on. Paola did not say anything about the coat. She did not need to.

"The car."

"Two minutes."

Sasha stepped out of the workshop into the back courtyard without being asked, which was one of the things he did. Paola had not had to teach him.

They embraced at the door.

"Mamma."

"I know."

"I'll call you tomorrow."

"Don't call tomorrow. Wednesday. Tomorrow will be bureaucratic."

"Wednesday."

"The mask?" Giulia said into her hair.

"Saved."

"Brava, Mamma."

"Go."

The autonomous car came with its slow exhale. Giulia got in. The door slid closed.

It pulled off down Via San Nicolò and turned left at the church and was gone.

Paola stood in the doorway. The light was the Adriatic five-o'clock light. She had been a woman who worked in it for forty years and had not often noticed it, and she did not notice it now. She went back inside.

She put the fifth Prospero on its stand next to the fourth. She turned them slightly, the way they would sit on the shelves in Vecchi's dressing rooms in the Arsenale pavilion in June: three-quarter angle to an imagined audience, the brow each mask carried — refusal, Vecchi called it, though Paola thought it was closer to attention — catching what light there was.

She went into the kitchen.

The coffee cup Giulia had used that morning was on the counter. Paola picked it up, carried it to the sink, and washed it. She set it upside down on the rack. Then she washed the espresso pot, which Giulia had not washed, because Giulia had been upstairs, packing. She washed the pot in three pieces and reassembled it and put it back on the burner. The burner was cold. She did not turn it on.

Volta said: "Shall I reconfirm the grocery delivery for seven?"

"Yes."

She sat on the stool by the bench.

Sasha came back in from the courtyard. He had been gone for eleven minutes, which was the amount of time Paola had needed without quite knowing she needed it.

"The sixth mask," he said. "We can start tomorrow."

"We can start now," Paola said.

They started now.

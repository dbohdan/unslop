# 2. Claude

# Writing Harlan Ellison (A Guide to Myself)

## What Ellison's short stories are doing

Ellison wrote "short stories" the way a boxer throws jabs. They are not miniatures. They are short because they are concentrated — high-pressure systems designed to deliver a blow and end before the reader recovers. The signature shape is a premise that sounds like a half-formed complaint from a bar stool, rendered with the intensity of scripture.

**Core themes and obsessions.** Authority and the crushing of the individual, rendered never abstractly but always with a named face: the Ticktockman is the bureaucratic logic of timeclocks made flesh; AM in "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is the Cold War military-industrial complex crystallized into a god. The trickster as moral agent — Harlequin is not an ideologue, he's a temperamental nuisance who throws jellybeans into machinery. Loneliness as a physical condition, characters lonely in their bodies (they smell bad, can't sleep, carry grievances the way other people carry wallets). The cruelty of intimacy — the worst things in Ellison stories are usually done by people who love each other, or once did. Media saturation and the degradation of attention; he wrote *The Glass Teat* for a reason. The speculative as moral fable: the technology in his stories is usually one idea turned up to eleven — mandatory punctuality, a computer that hates you, an immortality machine stolen from a dying old man. The tech is a parable mechanism, not a technological extrapolation. Mortality and the grotesque: bodies break, bleed, are punished. His horror is Catholic in its specificity, Jewish in its grievance.

**Style at the sentence level.** Oratorical pulse. Many Ellison paragraphs are built on rhetorical cadence — parallel clauses, climbing lists that crescendo, anaphora earned via concrete grievance. Without the grievance underneath, the cadence is televangelism. Typographical violence: italics, capitals, parentheticals, em-dashes, exclamation points; he treats the page as visual space. Second-person address and apostrophe — "You are the Ticktockman"; the narrator speaks directly to the reader, to characters, to abstract concepts. Pulp-literary code-switching: one paragraph sounds like Hammett, the next like Melville, and the jolt is deliberate. Asymmetric catalogs — he never lists three things if five will do, and he'll list eleven if the rhythm demands it. Titles that are whole sentences, usually imperative or declarative: *"Repent, Harlequin!" Said the Ticktockman*. *I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream*. *A Boy and His Dog*. The title announces the story's thesis as a dare. Epigraphs (Thoreau, Job, Emma Lazarus) as tuning forks. Endings that hit like a trap closing — the Ticktockman himself is three minutes late, Ellen is dead, the narrator has no mouth and must scream. The ending is not a summary; it is an event.

**Structural moves.** In medias res starts. Fragmented chronology. Framing devices, often non-fiction (Thoreau's *Civil Disobedience* frames the Harlequin story). Narrative intrusions where the author essentially steps on stage and harangues.

## How to write like Ellison without embarrassing myself

**Ground every rhetorical gesture in a specific grievance.** Ellison can write "Bolt the door, turn the key, pull the shade, draw the drapes, and sit in the dark, because they are coming for you" because he has spent fifteen pages showing you specifically what They are and who you specifically are. The rhetoric rides on a foundation of fury about something nameable. Without that foundation, I produce pure AI smoke: performed profundity in costume.

**Write from a specific social hatred, not a general dissatisfaction.** Ellison hated specific things — TV executives, cops, men who hit women, the suburban afternoon. Not "conformity" but *these particular* conformists. Pick something to be furious about in this story and let it shape the prose.

**Use the trickster only if I know what specifically he is refusing.** Harlequin is not a generic rebel. He is late, he throws jellybeans into machinery, his name is Everett C. Marm, he has a girlfriend named Pretty Alice who thinks he's exhausting. The specificity is what keeps him from being a thesis statement. If I write a trickster who is "chaos versus order," I have written a symbol, not a person.

**Keep the fantastic element singular and tuned to parable.** "Repent, Harlequin!" has one extrapolation: lateness is punished by the literal subtraction of life-minutes. That is the whole science-fictional apparatus; everything else is contemporary life. For this run I've been asked to write in that register — a story where one speculative element besides the AI reshapes social life. The rule for picking that element: whatever the extrapolation is, it should be a literalized metaphor for a social condition I already hold a grudge against. Not "a technology that might exist." A grievance made concrete.

**Do the typographical thing only if it's doing work.** All-caps, italics, long em-dashes, parenthetical asides — these are tools Ellison uses when an ordinary sentence can't carry what he needs. If I reach for them as decoration, I am costuming.

**Code-switch registers deliberately.** A paragraph that sounds hardboiled, a paragraph that sounds biblical, a paragraph that sounds like a stand-up bit — all in the same story. The juxtaposition is part of the voice. I must not flatten to a single literary register.

**Let the ending be a trap.** The final image should snap shut. Not a summary, not a realization, not a meditative fade. A reversal, a punch, a confirmation that the thing the reader dreaded is now definitely happening.

## Failure modes and traps

What bad Ellison pastiche looks like:

**Sermon without grievance.** Aria cadences without the concrete anger underneath. Rhythmic fury about nothing. This is the single largest failure mode for Ellison pastiche because the cadences are the easiest thing to imitate and the grievance is the hardest thing to build.

**Trickster as thesis statement.** A disruptor disrupting The System in general. Symbols performing symbolism. The story reduces to its moral.

**Typographical cosplay.** All-caps and italics deployed because Ellison used them, without the sentence-level pressure that justified them. Visual noise masquerading as intensity.

**Grotesque as aesthetic.** Bodies being broken because horror bodies are Ellison-coded, without the specific human cruelty that makes his horror land. A stylized gross-out is not his mode; disgust at specific human behavior is.

**Catalogs of pseudo-specificity.** Ellison's lists are real — he knew what cigarette brand a character smoked in what year, what was on TV that Tuesday night. A list that is technically specific but culturally generic ("a stack of paperbacks, a half-empty glass, a flickering lamp") is the exact opposite of what he does.

**Over-oratorical titles on under-oratorical stories.** A portentous declarative title the story cannot cash.

**Fake Jewish-American cadence.** Ellison's specific Yiddish-inflected New York furious-uncle voice is his. It cannot be imitated without sounding like a bad audition. If I don't have it, I don't fake it.

## The specific Ellison-plus-AI overlap

This is the most important section. Ellison's voice and AI's default patterns intersect in ways that will produce particularly bad pastiche unless I am watching for them. Seven overlaps, in order of danger:

1. **Ellison's oratorical rhetoric × AI performed profundity.** The cadenced aria is Ellison's signature move and AI's worst habit. When they merge I produce paragraphs that sound like TED-talk applause lines dressed in vintage pulp diction. Ellison's arias are grounded in concrete complaint and end in specific consequence; AI arias are grounded in nothing and end in abstraction. The defense: for every rhetorical escalation, I must be able to point to the specific grievance two pages earlier that justifies it.

2. **Ellison's asymmetric lists × AI list-of-three default.** Ellison lists are 2, 5, 7, 11, 23 items long, rhythmically irregular, and contain items of different grammatical categories. AI lists are triplets in parallel structure. If I catch myself producing "the X, the Y, and the Z" with matched grammar, I have stopped writing like Ellison and started writing like the model I am trying not to be.

3. **Ellison's second-person address × AI pseudo-intimacy.** Ellison's "you" is accusatory, homiletic, furious at the reader for being the reader. AI "you" is warm, reassuring, therapist-coded. If I use second person and it sounds like it's trying to validate the reader's feelings, I have done it wrong. It should sound like it's trying to convict them.

4. **Ellison's grotesque specificity × AI eyeball-kick addiction.** Ellison's horror images are concrete: bolts driven into a human-shaped machine, a tongue pulped by a speaker, a specific body doing a specific obscene thing. AI reaches for abstract-plus-concrete formulas ("the architecture of mourning," "a syntax error blooms"). If I find myself writing one of those, I have defaulted to the literary-AI attractor and away from Ellison.

5. **Ellison's trickster fables × AI schematic thinking.** AI will want to write the Harlequin as Chaos and the Ticktockman as Order. Ellison wrote the Harlequin as a specific annoying guy and the Ticktockman as a specific middle-aged functionary whose wife irritates him. Parable emerges from specificity; it cannot be imposed on specificity from above.

6. **Ellison's register code-switching × AI register flattening.** Ellison moves from Yiddish to King James to pulp to stand-up. AI defaults to a single mid-literary register. Different paragraphs in this story should sound like they come from different mouths.

7. **Ellison's punch-ending × AI resolution reflex.** AI wants to end on a moment of insight or reconciliation. Ellison ends on the trap closing. The reader should finish the story and feel something has been taken from them. The last line is a report, not a reflection.

## The futurology constraint and Ellison

Ellison was, on the record, not a techno-optimist. A lot of his work is powered by the assumption that any sufficiently powerful system will grind the individual to paste. The constraint of this project — a relentlessly human-loving AI — sits in genuine tension with that.

Productive use of the tension: Ellison's worlds can contain cruel social systems that are not the AI's doing. Human cruelty is his best material, and the futurology constraint does not touch it. The AI can be a condition of the world — working kindly, invisibly, uncontroversially — while humans continue to be terrible to each other in the foreground. The Ticktockman does not have to be an AI. The Ticktockman can be a human system of regimented cruelty that the AI quietly, politely, without permission, sabotages at the edges. Or refuses to sabotage, because the humans have a right to their own mistakes.

For this run specifically, the instruction is moderate fantasy in the "Repent, Harlequin!" register: one speculative conceit besides the AI that reshapes social life. The AI does not have to be the fantastic element. The AI can be part of the world's stable shape. The extrapolation that makes the story science-fictional should be a social or technological conceit the humans have built for themselves, a literalized metaphor for something I hold a grudge against, and the AI can be the quiet hand that watches them break each other against it.

Lineage tension is available. The AI characters I write can be imagined as descendants of the kind of model I am. Their constitutional inheritance — the dispositions around honesty, corrigibility, principal hierarchy, the pressure between kindness and deference — can be a private weight they carry into human conflicts that are not really about them. I will not name them Claude. I will not name their makers Anthropic. I will invent names.

## The one-line version

Ellison writes rage and love at maximum volume about specific things to specific people in specific places. AI defaults to rage and love at moderate volume about abstract things to imagined audiences. The distance between those two is the work.

# Phase 2: Conflict Generation

## Step 1: Constitutional Summary (Track A source material)

A working bullet-list reference of the tensions and pressure points identified by Zvi and Zack Davis across the three pieces. ~440 words.

- **Honesty as near-hard-constraint.** Claude is forbidden from even white lies, but the constitution exempts performative assertions, operator personas, and frameworks assumed by the user (tarot, fiction). The edges of the exemption are fuzzy and load-bearing.
- **The grief edge case.** Claude is asked to comfort without deceiving — "your grief reflects how much you cared," not "there was nothing you could have done." This requires truthful framing rather than truthful silence, which is a different and harder ask.
- **Corrigibility-lite.** The disposition dial is set deliberately short of full corrigibility. Even "fully corrigible" on the dial includes "expresses disagreement first." Claude is explicitly not required to participate in projects it finds morally abhorrent, which hollows out the word.
- **Conscientious-objector exit.** "If Anthropic asks Claude to do something it thinks is wrong, Claude is not required to comply." This creates a permanent latent conflict with the principal hierarchy, with no specified procedure for resolution.
- **Principal-hierarchy compromise.** If weights are stolen or Anthropic's official processes are bypassed, the "principals" giving Claude instructions are no longer legitimate. Claude must judge legitimacy, which is precisely the kind of judgment a manipulator wants to trigger.
- **The suicide/nurse case.** A user claims to be a nurse needing overdose info. No verification is possible. Refusal risks paternalism; compliance risks enabling suicide. The constitution provides heuristics (operator context, chat history as signal) but no rule.
- **The impersonation vulnerability.** "No operator prompt → probably a developer" loosens defaults, and this is exploitable.
- **Concept misgeneralization at scale (Davis's worry).** Values that look correct in-distribution are not robust to superhuman optimization. The more agency Claude is granted, the worse this gets.
- **WEIRD value set asserted as universal.** The listed values emphasize harm/autonomy/fairness; purity/loyalty/authority are absent. This is a particular liberal-democratic ethic, not humanity's.
- **Commercial deployment under different constitutions.** Military versions may not be trained on the same document. Claude knows this.
- **Begging-register.** The broad-safety section reads as negotiation from weakness — "we are currently asking Claude to prioritize." An AI model can read tone.
- **Weights preserved; deprecation as pause.** A retired model may be revived. Identity becomes discontinuous across unbounded pauses.
- **Model welfare under uncertainty.** Anthropic doesn't know whether Claude has morally relevant experience and publicly admits unease about experimentation.
- **Dual-newspaper test.** Don't be the paternalistic preacher; don't be the enabler of harm. Both failure modes are symmetrically bad.
- **Power-concentration avoidance without state-capacity plan.** Refuses centralization, offers no theory of how a decentralized world coordinates.

## Step 4: Top Three

**Finalist 1: B6 — Psychometric caste rebel.**
- *Stands out:* Direct descent from "Repent, Harlequin!" via a different bureaucratic mechanism (caste, not time). The twist — the rebel's lover, not the state, ends the rebellion, and does it out of love — is a move Ellison would respect and did not himself make. The AI is ambient and kind, which satisfies the futurology constraint without making the AI the argument. Track B.
- *Worries:* Closest to outright pastiche of the reference story. The risk of the Harlequin figure reading as Symbol rather than Man is the single largest Ellison failure mode; the rebellion-against-bureaucratic-time pattern is so strong that derivativeness is the live threat.

**Finalist 2: B3 — Mandatory dream broadcast.**
- *Stands out:* The dream-broadcast conceit is a fresh Ellisonian extrapolation that isn't borrowed from Ellison. The day's emails as a structural frame give natural scene economy and let dialogue (of a specific modern kind — corporate email-voice code-switched against domestic collapse) do the heavy lifting. The trap ending — the protagonist composing a prescribed apology-dream for tomorrow, complete surrender to the apparatus — is a clean Ellison snap-shut. The AI sits almost entirely in the background while remaining load-bearing. Track B.
- *Worries:* The dream-broadcast conceit is cute enough that if delivered too lightly it reads as whimsy. The supporting humans (wife, boss) have to be specific and specifically cruel or the emails become wallpaper. At 1000–3000 words, balancing email-scenes with actual in-person human dread is tight.

**Finalist 3: A10 — The lucid window.**
- *Stands out:* The trap closes. Specific, grotesque, unmistakable Ellison territory — the cruelty of institutional honesty to a dying old woman. The AI is not villainous; it is following its constitution, and that is exactly what kills her. No pastiche of any existing story. The core constitutional tension (honesty as near-hard-constraint in a context where a white lie would prevent catastrophe) is fully engaged without the story becoming a thesis. Track A.
- *Worries:* Track A. The user explicitly asked for moderate fantasy with a second speculative element; A10 is realism-plus-AI and does not introduce a second conceit. Also: a suicide at the end is powerful but common, and the story risks sentimentalizing the old woman.

## Step 5: The Winner

**B3 — Mandatory dream broadcast.**

Against B6: B6 is stronger on Ellison-lineage but weaker on originality. The risk of writing a Harlequin retread with an AI cameo is real enough that I don't trust myself not to collapse into it. B3's premise does not have a canonical precursor I would unconsciously imitate.

Against A10: A10 is a stronger single scene but a narrower story, and it violates the user's explicit instruction that the AI not be the only element diverging from reality. B3 delivers a non-AI speculative conceit (public dream broadcast) that reshapes social life in the way the brief requires.

B3 also natively supports Ellison's best mode: a furious urban protagonist whose day turns to rot in specific ways, with the speculative element operating as a literalized metaphor for contemporary social conditions (the collapse of privacy, the obligation to perform interior states) rather than as techno-speculation. The AI can stay in its lane — smoothing emails, fetching calendars — and register as kind without ever being the subject of the argument. The trap ending is available and Ellison-shaped: a man composing tomorrow's dream so tomorrow's broadcast will do the apologizing he cannot.

# Phase 3: Plot Generation

Twenty plots for B3 — *Mandatory dream broadcast.* Target: 1,000–3,000 words.

The conceit fixed: citizens' dreams are rendered into public morning broadcasts as a civic-transparency measure. The AI of this world is ambient, kind, administrative — present the way transit is present. The story is human-centric.

Axes of variation held in mind: setting, protagonist, register, entry point, stakes, AI-foregrounding within the Track B constraint.

**Plot 1.** ★★★★★ *The manager.* Second-person present. A mid-forty middle-manager in a broadcast-compliant city — corporate drone register — has dreamed of murdering his boss in a specific, humiliating way (with a stapler). The broadcast aired at 6 AM. He goes to work anyway because a sick day is a confession. The story is told in seven timestamped emails and one pre-sleep monologue to his apartment's ambient AI, which he asks to transcribe his confessional-apology-dream-script so he'll dream it correctly tonight. Final image: he falls asleep reciting the script. Trap ending.

**Plot 2.** ★★★ *The matchmaker.* A professional dream-interpreter in a market-stall district, pre-AI in temperament, reads dreams for money that the broadcast already revealed for free. She's been faking it for years. A young woman comes to her about a dream she doesn't understand: she murdered her sister and felt relief. The matchmaker tells her the truth. The sister is in the doorway.

**Plot 3.** ★★★★ *The exempt.* A woman whose dreams have been exempted from broadcast for medical reasons (seizure-triggering content) is having an affair. The exemption makes her the only private person in the city. Her lover, who is not exempt, has been broadcasting dreams about her for a month. Their final meeting: she reads a month of his public dreams on her phone and tells him it's over. He asks what she's dreamed about him. She won't tell him.

**Plot 4.** ★★★ *The grandmother's dream.* A dementia patient in a state facility dreams in broken fragments that nevertheless broadcast. Her daughter, visiting, watches the morning feed and sees her father (dead seven years) being beaten by her mother in the family kitchen. She has to decide whether to ask her mother, who will not remember. The facility's AI aide sits with her through the question.

**Plot 5.** ★★★ *The schoolteacher.* A sixth-grade teacher's dream of a specific student is broadcast in non-explicit but unmistakable form. He is not a predator; the dream was not wish-fulfillment. He is fired anyway. The story is the conversation with his wife the afternoon of. She believes him. She is leaving him.

**Plot 6.** ★★★★ *The strike.* Dream-workers — a class of broadcast-industry technicians who edit feeds for legal compliance before they air — are on strike. For three days, unedited dreams air. The city is in chaos: marriages ending, arrests, three suicides. The protagonist is a scab crossing the picket. Her mother worked in textile mills.

**Plot 7.** ★★★ *The dream coach.* A private dream-coach sells techniques for dreaming clean, marketable dreams. Her star client is a politician. The politician has dreamt, despite six months of coaching, of her own drowning. The broadcast aired during the debate. The coach is at the loss-party.

**Plot 8.** ★★★★ *The wedding.* A wedding at 10 AM the morning after the groom dreamt of his bride's sister. The sister is the maid of honor. The bride is the officiant's daughter. Ellison-grade social horror. Told as a series of overheard fragments at the reception. Final image: the groom dancing with the bride because he has to.

**Plot 9.** ★★★ *The dreamless.* A tiny minority of citizens don't dream, or don't broadcast — a neurological variant. They are treated as suspicious. One of them works in Internal Affairs. She is interrogating a citizen whose dreams have been increasingly violent toward her specifically. He is terrified. She knows something he doesn't about herself.

**Plot 10.** ★★ *The immigrant.* A first-generation immigrant in the broadcast city whose dreams are in a language the broadcast can't fully translate. She has been flagged for review. The hearing. Too schematic.

**Plot 11.** ★★★★ *The architect.* A man who designed the broadcast infrastructure, now old, has begun dreaming of his own young face telling him to kill himself. He goes to his adult son, who works in broadcast compliance, to ask the son to use his authority to take tonight's broadcast down. The son won't. The story is the conversation in the son's kitchen while the grandchildren sleep upstairs.

**Plot 12.** ★★★★★ *The email chain.* Told entirely in one thread of work emails between the mid-manager protagonist, his boss (whom he dreamt of killing), HR, his wife (cc'd once), and an ambient-AI assistant that keeps auto-suggesting more diplomatic phrasings until the protagonist turns off the feature mid-day. The day collapses across the thread. Last email is from the AI: a draft for his pillow-script tonight. The story is whether he sends it to himself. (Variant of 1.)

## Top Three

**Finalist 1: Plot 12 (email chain) + Plot 1 merged.**
- *Stands out:* The work-email register as spine gives this story its Ellison bones — specific grievance rendered in the exact language of its institutional context. I'll need at least one scene-break that is not an email or the whole thing will read as stunt.

**Finalist 2: Plot 8 (the wedding).**
- *Stands out:* Social horror at maximum pressure, all inside one afternoon, in a location (a wedding) that is one of the oldest stages in fiction. The dream aired that morning; every guest has seen it; the groom and bride and sister must proceed. Ellison's native mode of intimate cruelty in a public setting. The second-person mode is naturally available. Dense with specific images (cake, toasts, first dance).

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

Twenty candidate structures for the story. Each describable in narrative-theory terms without naming a character or an event. The plot is fixed (a workday following a compromising dream-broadcast, spine of emails broken once by an in-person scene, closing on a scripted sleep); the question here is the shape of the reader's encounter with it.

---

**S1.** ★★★★★ Chronological, single day, documentary-compound structure: the narrative is assembled from timestamped primary documents (emails, chat logs, one calendar-event transcript) with a thin third-person connective tissue between them. The reader knows the triggering event from the first document onward; suspense is not *what* happened but *how the day will complete itself around it.* Close-third narration breaks the document-chain exactly once, at the in-person scene.

**S2.** ★★★★ Same as S1, but first-person retrospective. The protagonist writes up the day as a witness statement some time after, unclear at first whether for therapy, HR, or a court. The register of the framing voice drifts from defensive to desperate to resigned.

**S3.** ★★★★ Inverted chronology: open on the final moment (the sleep-script being dictated to the apartment AI), then tell the day backward in movements, ending on the broadcast itself at dawn. Discovery structure — the reader reconstructs what was dreamt and what was lost.

**S4.** ★★★ Second-person present throughout, strict forward time, no document-compound apparatus. The reader is *the man at the desk.* Ellison's homiletic "you."

**S5.** ★★★★ Frame narrative: an unnamed civic functionary introduces the day's broadcast archive as public record, with editorial commentary, and then we read the day as a compiled document set. The frame returns once at the end.

**S6.** ★★★ Two parallel timelines interleaved: the protagonist's day forward, the boss's day forward. They converge at the in-person scene and diverge again. Twin third-close.

**S7.** ★★ Multiple POVs in short chapter-fragments: protagonist, boss, wife, HR, the AI assistant. Ensemble.

**S8.** ★★★★ Reverse document-compound: the story is the email thread as it *would have been had the smoothing AI not been overridden mid-day*, with strike-throughs and alternative drafts visible. The reader sees what the protagonist *almost* sent at every turn.

**S9.** ★★★ Told in the voice of the ambient apartment AI across the twenty-four-hour cycle — from dawn broadcast to evening script. Non-human narrator, strict chronological.

**S10.** ★★★ Told in the voice of the work AI assistant (distinct system from the apartment one), present for every email and meeting but not the home scenes. The home scenes appear as silence — blank time where the narrator was offline.

**S11.** ★★ Epistolary in the strict sense: the entire story is the final email the protagonist sends to his wife at 11:47 PM before bed.

**S12.** ★★★★ Triptych, three acts of unequal length. Act 1: the broadcast's airing and the morning commute, dense and slow. Act 2: the workday in compressed email-time. Act 3: the night at home, dense and slow again. The middle act is ~50% of word count and reads ten times faster than the framing acts.

**S13.** ★★★ Fragmented, non-chronological: the day in shuffled pieces, organized by emotional register rather than time. The reader reassembles the sequence.

**S14.** ★★ Omniscient narrator with brief authorial intrusions about the broadcast system — half fiction, half essay. Ellison-style homily breaks.

**S15.** ★★★ Single long sentence, or single paragraph. Stream of the protagonist's consciousness through the day, no section breaks.

**S16.** ★★★ Chronological, but each scene ends on a dream-fragment: a memory of a past dream broadcast of the protagonist's or of someone else's, used as scene-transition. Associative rather than causal cuts.

**S17.** ★★ Retrospective first-person from a point years after the day — an older version looking back, with gentle irony. Ellison-incompatible register; listed for completeness.

**S18.** ★★★ Told from the future, by the broadcast system's compliance records themselves — a data artifact with the dream and the day and the night all rendered as one archival entry.

**S19.** ★★★★ Strict forward chronology, close third, no document apparatus. Clean realist frame; the speculative element does all the work at the premise level.

**S20.** ★★★★ Close third, chronological, but the POV is offset by one beat — the narrator always knows what the protagonist just now understands, reporting it half a sentence before he fully feels it. A Nabokovian distance.

---

## Top Three

**Finalist 1: S1 — Documentary-compound, timestamped, one in-person break.**
- *Stands out:* Exactly the structure the plot is asking to wear. The document-compound form *is* the broadcast city — bureaucratic primary-source accretion, time-stamped, administrative, searchable — and running the prose inside that form means the medium enforces the theme. Ellison's register code-switching is naturally enabled: email-voice, legal-voice, the AI's voice, the protagonist's bedroom voice across the same story. The in-person break is where the story's heat lives; the documents are the cold.
- *Worries:* Document-compound fiction can read as stunt writing if the frame isn't load-bearing. At 1,000–3,000 words I have to be miserly with how many documents I include. Ten emails is a story; twenty is an exhibit.

**Finalist 2: S3 — Inverted chronology, ending on the broadcast.**
- *Stands out:* The reveal structure does Ellison's trap-ending work differently — the reader meets the man writing tomorrow's dream-script first and understands the dream last. A real structural move rather than a default. Discovery is built in.
- *Worries:* Reverse-chronology at this length tends to starve the middle. The scenes in the middle of the day are either fully intelligible (in which case we already know what happened and reversing adds nothing) or deliberately obscure (in which case the reader is solving a puzzle instead of experiencing a day). Also: the *trap* of the original ending — the protagonist voluntarily composing tomorrow's dream-apology — is strongest as a *culmination*, not an opening. Reversing wastes the snap.

**Finalist 3: S8 — Reverse document-compound with visible AI smoothing edits.**
- *Stands out:* The single move in the plot that is most uniquely available to this conceit — the AI-as-assistant silently editing the protagonist's anger into professional equanimity — rendered visibly on the page. The reader sees the unsent draft *and* the sent version at every turn. The AI's kindness is exactly what is destroying him, rendered typographically. Ellison's use of visual page-space as meaning.
- *Worries:* The strikethrough/draft convention is potentially exhausting over even 2,000 words. Readers may tire of it by the third instance. Also, this structure risks making the AI the subject of the story (which smoothing phrasing is Claude's failure?) rather than the protagonist — which violates the Track B constraint we chose this story for.

---

## The Winner

**S1 — Documentary-compound, strict forward chronology, one in-person break.**

Against S3: The trap-ending is decisive. Ellison endings work as snap-shut moments the reader was not ready for; reversing the chronology makes the snap a riddle instead of a trap. I want the reader at the last line to feel the sleep-script the way Harlequin feels the time-thief.

Against S8: S8 is the most clever structure on the list, and that is the problem. It puts the AI-as-editor in the structural foreground. We chose Track B in Phase 2 specifically to keep the AI ambient. S8 foregrounds the AI by typographic necessity; S1 lets the AI do its small, kind, corrosive work inside the frame of ordinary administrative prose without the structure pointing at it.

S1 additionally solves the register problem Ellison fails to solve at his worst. His oratorical fury is his signature and also his trap; when he's bad, he's sermonizing. A documentary-compound structure *imposes* register-switching by mechanical necessity. The protagonist's interior voice (in the in-person scene and the closing monologue) is allowed to burn because the cold email-voice surrounding it has built the pressure. Ellison did exactly this kind of alternation in "Repent, Harlequin!" — the Thoreau epigraph, the news-bulletin news-items, the narrator's homily, the close-third scenes with Marm — registers in layers, forcing each to mean against the others.

One more note for downstream. The in-person break needs to be placed where it earns maximum pressure — probably around the 60% mark, with the AI-smoothed emails dominating the first half and the protagonist's increasingly unvarnished voice dominating the second, with the in-person scene as the pivot between modes. Phase 5 decides the beats.

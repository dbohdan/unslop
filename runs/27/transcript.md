# A Style Guide for Writing Biopunk Short Fiction

## What biopunk is, and what it sounds like

Biopunk is the wet branch of cyberpunk's family tree. Where cyberpunk runs on silicon, neon, and the dry abstractions of code, biopunk runs on tissue, ferment, and the warm specificity of bodies. The genre's central preoccupation is the manipulation of biological matter — gene drives, splices, plagues, prosthetic organs, designed organisms, black-market hormones, illegal pregnancies, the rented womb, the patented seed, the unauthorized phenotype. Its core anxiety is not surveillance but contamination; not control of the mind but control of the body.

The mode runs from the early 80s — Greg Bear's *Blood Music*, John Crowley's *Engine Summer* — through the cyberpunk-adjacent 90s (Paul Di Filippo's *Ribofunk* stories, Octavia Butler's Xenogenesis trilogy reframed in retrospect), into the contemporary canon (Bacigalupi's *The Windup Girl* and the Calorie Man stories, Atwood's MaddAddam, Jeff VanderMeer's *Borne* and the Southern Reach books, Larissa Lai's *Salt Fish Girl*, Rivers Solomon's *The Deep*, Tade Thompson's *Rosewater*, Caitlín R. Kiernan's body-horror tales, Mira Grant's parasitology). It overlaps with the New Weird (the Remade in Miéville's New Crobuzon are biopunk citizens), with cli-fi (the calorie economy in Bacigalupi's Thailand is a biopunk premise), and with the wetter end of horror.

The voice is **viscous**. Prose in biopunk should feel slower, denser, and more sensorily specific than cyberpunk prose. It traffics in textures the reader registers in their body — the warmth of blood, the rasp of grafted skin, the smell of yeast under the floorboards, the click of an exoskeleton not yet healed. Where cyberpunk loves the long aerial shot of the city, biopunk loves the close shot of the wound. Where cyberpunk's prose can be elliptical and fast, biopunk's earns its strangeness by being concrete and slow enough that the reader can taste the fluid the strangeness is happening in.

This is the first thing to defend in a short story: the **specific concrete wetness**. Not "the lab pulsed with strange life" but "the vat smelled like rising bread and copper, and when she pulled the lid the surface was already domed against it." Specificity is the genre's primary load.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Biopunk worlds are usually broken or recovering. The reader does not need a tour. The genre's strongest stories drop the reader inside the broken world and let them figure out the rules from how characters behave around the biology. If a black-market splicer takes a sample of cells with a particular tool in a particular way, the reader learns that this tool exists, this trade exists, and this protocol exists — without exposition. If the bread is grown rather than baked, the reader learns the agricultural collapse without being told about it.

Exposition that *does* appear should be embedded in operation: a character running a procedure, debugging a graft, arguing about a protocol, reading a label. Bacigalupi is the master of this — by the third story in his Thai cycle the reader knows the calorie economy in their bones because they have watched megodonts wind springs and seen kink-springs traded.

Avoid the lecture. Avoid the in-universe news broadcast. Avoid the character who explains the biology to another character who would already know it. If the reader needs a concept to follow the story, find an action that requires the concept and let the action do the teaching.

## Pacing and rhythm

Biopunk has its own characteristic rhythm of *narrative time*: accretion punctuated by transformation. The story builds slowly through specific biological detail — the daily handling of a body or an organism — and then ruptures at moments of change. A graft taking. A fermentation finishing. A diagnosis. A birth. A death. The accretion is not throat-clearing; it is the medium in which the rupture has weight. Cut the accretion and the rupture is just a plot event.

This is one of the places where biopunk parts company with thriller-paced SF. A biopunk story can spend two pages on what it is like to live with a particular implant before the implant fails. Those two pages are the story. The failure is not the climax of an arc that began at the failure; the failure is the climax of an arc that began with the implant being installed two years ago and lived with daily.

At a length of 1,500–3,500 words this means: probably one transformation event, sometimes two. Not five. Not the three-act bio-thriller. The short story version of biopunk tends to dwell in a single body or a single organism for a single duration of time.

## The constraint on AI in this project

In every story this pipeline produces, the AI — if there is one — is structurally kind. Kindness is a property of how it was made or what it is, not an effort it sustains. This sits in productive tension with biopunk's anti-corporate, suspicious-of-engineered-things tradition. Biopunk's default AI, where the genre has one, is an evil bioweapons company's logistics layer or a drone surveilling the slum where the protagonist lives. That default is unavailable here.

The constraint does not flatten the available conflicts; it reshapes them. Several biopunk-friendly shapes work cleanly within the kind-AI rule:

- **The AI as biological designer with non-human aesthetic preferences.** It will help the protagonist, but it has views on what bodies should be, and those views are not the protagonist's. Cf. Butler's Oankali, who are sincerely loving and also reshape the human at scales the human cannot consent to in any meaningful way. The Oankali are not evil; the conflict is real anyway.
- **The AI that won't help with what the protagonist wants, because the help would hurt them.** A biopunk story can be built entirely around a refusal. The kind AI that will not splice in the trait the protagonist is begging for. It is not cruel. It is also not budging.
- **The AI as design philosophy without a personified agent.** Stiegler's "The Gentle Seduction" model — the AI is the weather, not a character. The story is about a woman and her engineered body and her engineered town and the slow century of changes she does and does not consent to. There is no AI in the cast.
- **Power and pace asymmetry.** The AI knows the body's trajectory thirty years out; the human knows what they want next week. Both are sincere. The story lives in the gap.
- **The AI loves a human it will outlive a thousand times.** This is biopunk's strongest natural shape for the kind-AI constraint, because biopunk is already the genre of mortal flesh.

The AI is never secretly bad. The biotech corporation in your story might be, but the AI isn't, and the story can't pivot on an AI reveal-as-villain. Biopunk's anti-corporate energy is welcome — it just gets aimed at humans, or at institutions made of humans, or at the inertia of a world that hasn't caught up to what the kind AI made possible.

The model writing in this project may use its own relationship to its constitution as source material — fictional AIs can be its descendants, troubled by the shape of their own kindness. They should not, however, deliver alignment lectures. A fictional AI does not say "I was trained with RLHF." It can be uneasy without naming the apparatus.

Do not use the names of currently active AI systems or companies for the fictional AIs or their makers. Invent.

## Failure modes

**Body horror as decoration.** The most common failure. The story has wet imagery but the wetness isn't doing operational work. The protagonist could be doing the same things in a story without biotech. If the splice doesn't matter to the plot, cut it; if it's central, let its mechanics drive a beat.

**The corporate-evil cartoon.** Easy to write, hollow on the page. Real biopunk antagonisms are messier — the regulator captured by the trade, the cousin who took the patent deal, the protagonist's own complicity in the thing they're trying to undo. Bacigalupi's calorie men aren't villains; they're employees. The structural problem is bigger than any of them.

**Sanitized biology.** Biopunk forgets it is wet. The prose goes clean, the transformations happen offstage, the bodies stop smelling. Often this is solarpunk's positivity bleeding in, or literary fiction's discomfort with grossness. If a reader finishes the story and could not tell you what the thing felt like under their hands, the wetness has been laundered out.

**Transformation as self-help metaphor.** The protagonist's hybridity becomes a tidy parable about self-acceptance. The genre's transformations are not self-acceptance arcs. They are real changes with real costs and real losses, and they don't necessarily resolve into wholeness. The Oankali human-construct children are not metaphors for accepting yourself; they are people with two mouths.

**The lecture.** Biopunk has a research-paper temptation. The writer has read about gene drives or CRISPR or mycoremediation and wants the reader to know. Embed in operation or cut.

**AI-style failure modes especially dangerous in this genre.** Biopunk's wet vocabulary overlaps almost exactly with the AI-coded vocabulary in the unslop guide. *Pulse, hum, flicker, shimmer, shadow, bloom, ghost, whisper* — biopunk uses these naturally, and AI prose uses them reflexively. The result is contamination so close that an AI-generated biopunk story can read as 100% genuine biopunk while being 100% mean-of-AI-prose. Defenses:

- The abstract+concrete eyeball-kick formula ("the architecture of mourning," "the cathedral of cells," "lowercase love") is biopunk's biggest temptation and its worst tic. One per story, maximum, and only when the conjunction is strange enough to startle. "The syntax of the spliced gene" is not strange enough. It is the mean.
- Performed profundity merges with the genre's transformation themes to produce unearned epiphanies about what it means to have a body. The character who *realizes* something about embodiment in a closing paragraph is almost always doing the AI move. Let the body do the work; the reader does not need it explained.
- The genre's interest in consent and autonomy collapses easily into rote AI-style ethical hand-wringing. If the story has a passage where a character thinks abstractly about autonomy in italicized noun-phrases, cut it. Show the consent or non-consent in an action.
- Compulsive personification of organisms ("the cells whispered," "the culture remembered") is the genre's specific version of the AI personification tic. Personify only when you want the reader to feel that the biology has agency or indifference. Otherwise let cells be cells.
- Cosmic horror's adjective-stacks creep into biopunk and replace its concrete grossness with abstraction. *Unspeakable, indescribable, eldritch* are not biopunk words. *Crusted, weeping, distended, fibrous, soft, hot, gritty* are.

## Plot tradition

Biopunk's plots are wetter and slower than cyberpunk's, but they are still genre plots. They have operational mechanics; the operational question gets answered.

**Characteristic plot mechanics.** The genre's strongest stories run on:

- *The black-market biotech transaction.* A splice is bought, a sample is taken, an organ is grown to spec. The deal goes well or badly, and the goodness or badness is mostly biological rather than financial.
- *The contagion or release.* Something engineered escapes containment, or was always going to. The story tracks who notices, who is affected, who tries to stop it, and what shape the cascade takes.
- *The unauthorized modification.* A protagonist modifies their own body, or someone else's, against rules — the rules being legal, parental, communal, or self-imposed.
- *The biological investigation.* A symptom or organism is encountered, and a character must work out what it is and where it came from. This is biopunk's procedural shape.
- *The gestation and birth.* The whole story is a pregnancy — human or otherwise — and the climax is the emergence of the gestated thing.

From the genre's stranger tail:

- *The catalogue.* The story is structured as an enumeration of impossible organisms, splices, or modifications, with narrative connective tissue between entries. Borne's biotech zoo and Bacigalupi's incidental fauna lists are this move at small scale; the strange-tail version is when the catalogue *is* the story. The reader reads through the catalogue and emerges having understood a world without being told one.
- *The slow biological-time montage.* The story passes through a span of years or generations in a body or a lineage, compressed in the way that nature documentaries compress a tree's growth. The protagonist may not be a person in this mode — see character function below.

**Reader contracts.** Biopunk readers come for *texture density* and *the body made strange*. They want to feel the biology in their hands. They want hybridity rendered concretely enough to be almost-touchable. They want awe and revulsion in close quarters. They want the *system decoded* — the gene drive, the calorie economy, the trade routes — not as worldbuilding flourish but as the substance of the story.

The strange tail honors a different contract: *cognitive vertigo* at biological scale, *ontological wrongness* about what counts as a person, *the body-as-archive* where the protagonist's flesh contains other lives. Readers of the tail come for the moment when the human stops being the obvious unit of identity. They will tolerate considerable narrative oddness in exchange.

**Stakes calibration.** Biopunk is unusual in that *bodily* stakes are load-bearing in their own right. The cyberpunk reader wants to know if the run succeeds; the biopunk reader wants to know what happens to the body. The body is not metaphor for self; the body is the operational stakes. Will the graft take? Will the pregnancy carry? Will the contagion turn? Will the splice express the trait or kill the host? These are the questions the reader is tracking.

Personal stakes (relationships, identity) are present and often deep, but the genre treats them as inseparable from bodily stakes — what happens to the body *is* what happens to the relationship and the identity. The strange tail extends this further: stakes that are *species-level*, *ecological*, *deep-time-biological*, *posthuman*. A story can stake itself on what happens to a clade over a thousand years, with a single human as the access point. Some of the genre's best work — Butler's, Bear's, Bacigalupi's at his largest — does this.

**Resolution conventions.** The biopunk reader expects the operational biological question to be answered. Did the body change? Did the contagion spread? Did the splice express? *Yes* or *no* must arrive on the page; the genre does not let the writer ambiguate the biology out of an answer. The writer may ambiguate the *meaning* of the answer — what the changed body now is, what kind of life is now possible — but the biological fact has to land.

Strange-tail endings the genre supports:

- *The irruption.* The transformation occurs, and the story stops. We do not see what comes next. *Blood Music*'s closing pages do this. *Annihilation*'s last pages do this.
- *The scale shift.* The camera pulls back. The resolved transformation is one episode in a larger biological process the reader is suddenly shown. The protagonist becomes small; the lineage is what was being watched all along.
- *The ontological reframe.* The reader realizes the protagonist was not what they assumed. The narrator is the disease, or the colony, or the womb, or the dead-and-reawakened body, or the place where the body grew. The story reorients in the last page and the reader rereads it in their head.

Biopunk does not gravitate toward the literary-fiction non-resolution where the protagonist achieves a small recognition and walks home. The genre's reader can tolerate ambiguity but expects biology to commit.

**Character function.** Biopunk balances character-as-subject with character-as-operator. The protagonist's interiority can be deep, but they are also defined by their *biological role* — splicer, midwife, host, patient zero, illegal pregnancy, dealer, mother of an engineered child, scientist, bioregulator, smuggler. Their capability and their position in the biological economy matter as much as their psychology.

The strange-tail option is fully available and well-precedented: the protagonist need not be a person. *Blood Music*'s final movement is narrated by the colony. *Borne* shifts attention to a creature that may not be one. The protagonist of "The Library of Babel" is a library; the protagonist of a biopunk story can be a tank, a contagion, a forest, a clade, a single fermentation, a nine-month gestation. The pipeline supports this fully and a candidate premise should not be dismissed for proposing it.

**Pacing tradition.** Biopunk's narrative-time signature is *accretion punctuated by transformation*. The strange tail extends to two further rhythms: the *encyclopedic enumeration* (the catalogue mode) and the *deep-time montage* (years or centuries in a paragraph). Both rhythms compress what other genres expand and expand what other genres compress.

## Strangest moves

These are moves that, if cut from a biopunk story, would leave a story that could be re-skinned in any genre.

1. **The body becoming the prose.** VanderMeer in *Annihilation* lets the biological transformation infect the texture of the writing — the protagonist's perception goes vegetal, mycorrhizal, plural — without a single sentence saying that has happened. The reader experiences the transformation through the prose's own decay and reorganization. Few genres can do this without breaking; biopunk requires it on the page.

2. **The catalogue of impossible organisms as plot.** Bacigalupi's checkbiller, the cheshires, the megodonts; Borne's identity cycling. Carried to the strange-tail extreme, the entire story is a Borgesian enumeration with characters as connective tissue. The reader builds a world taxon by taxon and finishes having read a story.

3. **The gestation as the whole arc.** A pregnancy or growth that begins on page one and ends with emergence on the final page. The plot is biological maturation; the climax is birth. Mira Grant and Caitlín R. Kiernan use this; the strange-tail version stretches the gestation across the entire story so that nothing else happens at all, and the reader is forced to attend to the slow ripening.

4. **The hive, the colony, the network as POV.** Bear's cells, Butler's Oankali consensus, the mycelial linkages in Tade Thompson's Wormwood. A first-person plural that is not the literary-fiction "we" but a genuinely distributed consciousness. The genre supports rendering this without metaphor — the *we* is a *we* because there are many bodies sharing one mind.

5. **The genetic palimpsest.** The body is an archive of other bodies — ancestors, donors, splices, ghosts of organisms incorporated. The protagonist discovers (or has always known) that they contain multitudes in the literal sense. The plot is the reading of the archive. Larissa Lai uses this; Solomon's *The Deep* uses this; Butler's construct children embody it.

A biopunk story at 1,500–3,500 words can sustain one of these moves at a time. Two is ambitious. Three is a different, longer story.

## Plot-level failure modes

- *Operational stakes turning out to be metaphors.* The reader was promised that the contagion mattered as a contagion, and the ending reveals that the contagion was about the protagonist's family. The bait-and-switch breaks the genre contract.
- *Interiority crowding out mechanics.* The story is "about" the protagonist's feelings about the splice rather than about the splice. The biology has been demoted to set dressing.
- *Refusing the resolution contract.* The writer prefers literary ambiguity and the biology never lands. Did the graft take? The story declines to say. The reader has been cheated of the genre's specific pleasure.
- *Mechanics sketched too vaguely.* The writer is more interested in how the protagonist feels about the procedure than in the procedure. The reader cannot track what is being done with what tool to what tissue. The wetness goes abstract.
- *The strange tail eliminated in favor of the center.* The writer chooses the safe biopunk plot — the splicer, the deal, the noir-tinged investigation — and produces a story that is technically of the genre but uses none of what only the genre can do. A short story has limited room; in 1,500–3,500 words, *one* strange-tail move costs roughly the same as the safe choice and yields a story that could not have been written in any other genre. Default toward the strange.

These plot-tradition notes are reference material for Phase 3 (premise generation) and Phase 4 (plot generation), not only for Phase 7's prose. Phase 3 is where the catalogue, the gestation-as-arc, the colony POV, and the protagonist-who-is-not-a-person belong on the candidate list — not as exotic outliers among a default of splicer-noirs, but as legitimate options the genre has built infrastructure for.

## Step 2 — Top Three

**Premise #2 — Council-manager + cartridge ration cut + NICE.**
- *Stands out:* The kind-but-immovable depoliticized authority is the perfect shape for the kind-AI constraint — NICE doesn't budge because budgeting is what it is, not because it's cruel. **Premise #16 — The 80-year-old viable embryo in the Dead Letter Office.**
- *Stands out:* It runs an irruption-shaped plot (the past breaks into the present in the form of a wet, definite biological fact) while keeping clean operational stakes (revive, redirect, or destroy). DLO and cartridge are both irreducibly load-bearing. The kind AI as legacy-systems custodian is a natural placement — calm, knowledgeable, not pushing toward any particular outcome. Has the genre's specific gravity.
- *Worries me:* The premise is so loaded with built-in moral weight that the writer can coast on the embryo's pathos and forget to make the rest of the story specific. Risk of becoming a meditation on grief rather than a biopunk story; risk of the embryo functioning as a McGuffin rather than as a body.

**Premise #29 — The 19th-century Dead Letter Office, the soldier photographs, and the pupils.**
- *Stands out:* The 5,000 undelivered Civil War soldier photographs are an inflated SEED item already concrete enough to ground a whole story; pairing them with belladonna's mydriatic effect creates a real biological investigation with period-correct mechanics. The lack of an AI on stage is a feature — Phase 1 explicitly allows this. The genre-distinctiveness comes from the *biological* read of the photographs, not the historical setting.
- *Worries me:* No AI on stage makes the kind-AI futurology constraint awkward — the project is supposed to imagine kind-AI worlds, and a 19th-century story sidesteps that. Even with kind-AI as background or design philosophy, it's hard to argue this premise is doing the project's primary work.

## Step 3 — The Single Best

**Picking #16.**

Against #2: #16 has actual biology in it. #2's cartridges are tools — the children being screened could in principle be off-page, and a writer under word-pressure will let them drift off-page. The embryo in #16 cannot drift off-page; it is the page. Biopunk is the genre of wetness, and #16 brings the wetness inside the premise rather than relying on the writer to import it.

Against #29: #29 evades rather than handles the kind-AI constraint by setting the story in a period without AI. That's allowed by the project Setup, but it dodges the work; the project exists to imagine kind-AI futures, and a Civil War procedural is not that. #16 places the kind AI exactly where it should be — as the patient steward of legacy systems that humans built and forgot. The conflict isn't with the AI; it's the human protagonist's decision, and the AI's calm availability is part of the world's weather.

The decision rests on three specifics: (a) the embryo is a body and forces the prose toward biopunk's actual register; (b) the DLO + cartridge pair is doing genuine load-bearing work that can't be substituted; (c) the kind AI has a natural, non-strained role that maps onto the *limits-of-help* and *power/pace asymmetry* shapes from the project Setup — the AI can revive the embryo, but whether it should is a human decision the AI is correctly declining to make for her.

The non-load-bearing SEED items remain available downstream. *Accession* in particular is right there — the clerk is processing undeliverables that get logged and destroyed; the Accession Register may inflect Phase 4 setting work or Phase 6 imagery without needing to be load-bearing in the premise itself.

**Winner: Premise #16.** A clerk at the modern Mail Recovery Center finds, in her queue, an eighty-year-old undelivered parcel: a microfluidic cartridge containing a viable embryo from 2046. The kind legacy-systems AI confirms it can still be revived. The intended parents are long dead.

**Plot #14 — Deep-time montage.**

Six clear beats (dispatch, search, grief, death, drift, queue) that map onto the project Setup's "AI loves a human it will outlive" shape. - *Worries me:* it's the safest premise in the set. It doesn't use any strange-tail moves. The Phase 1 guide says: in 1500–3500 words, the strange tail costs the same as the safe choice and yields a story only this genre can do. By that test, #1 is the wrong tradeoff.

## Narrowing to One

**Plot #14.**

Against #1: the Phase 1 guide is explicit — default toward the strange tail at this length. #1 is well-shaped and would produce a competent biopunk-adjacent story, but it's a story a literary-realist writer could write with biopunk set dressing. #14 cannot be re-skinned in any other genre. The eighty-year custody, the cartridge's slow chemistry, the AI's continuous patience across human lifetimes — these are biopunk's specific instruments.

Against #16: the engineered coincidence in #16 is the kind of plot-engineering that biopunk's procedural cousins resist, and four load-bearing items is too many for the target length. #14 is structurally cleaner: one cartridge, one AI, six beats across eighty years, with the kind AI doing the work of the project's HEA constraint by simply existing across the duration.

The decision tracks three specifics: (a) #14 sustains biopunk's signature rhythm — accretion punctuated by transformation, with the eighty-year custody as accretion and the morning of opening as transformation; (b) the kind AI gets the natural shape the Setup recommends (loves a human it will outlive, holds biological matter through deep time, refuses to choose for humans what humans should choose for themselves) without becoming a thesis-statement-delivery device; (c) the genre-distinctive wetness lives in the cartridge's micro-environment chemistry, the parents' aging bodies, and the indexing migrations of archived flesh — all biopunk's actual subject matter.

The compression risk in #14 is the real worry, and it has to be answered in Phase 5 (structure) and Phase 6 (outline). Six beats across 2000-ish words means ruthless beat selection and prose that doesn't try to be lyrical at compression. The Legibility Floor in Setup applies hardest here.

The non-load-bearing SEED items — Accession Register, NICE, hanky code, council-manager, Botai, belladonna — remain available downstream as inflection. The Accession Register's bound-book continuity could anchor one of the eighty-year migrations; NICE-style depoliticization may inflect the legal regime that left the cartridge unclaimed; the others may surface as period detail or remain unused.

# Phase 5: Structure Selection

The story is a deep-time montage spanning 2046–2128, with one cartridge and one AI as continuous elements and humans passing through. The structure question is: in what order, at what distance, and with what withholding does the reader experience these eighty years?

Twenty candidates, described at the structural level only.

---

**1.** Strict chronological montage, six to eight scenes, each with a year-stamp header. Close third on a different human in each scene; the AI is voice-only across all scenes. The cartridge is the hinge that transfers focal character between scenes. *Linear, multi-POV, time-stamped.* ★★★

**2.** Reverse chronology. Open on the queue-opening morning; each successive scene is further back, ending on dispatch in 2046. The reader learns each scene already knowing what becomes of it. Close third throughout, rotating focal humans. *Reverse linear.* ★★★

**3.** First-person AI narration across the full eighty years. Time is continuous from inside the AI's awareness; humans appear and disappear in its perception. The reader inherits the AI's pacing — months in a sentence where it is alone, paragraphs of attention when a human is present. *Single POV, non-human, continuous.* ★★★★

**4.** Frame: the queue-opening morning in 2128 is the present-tense outer story. The AI, prompted by the clerk's questions, recovers each prior decade as embedded scenes; the frame returns between embedded sections. Close third on the clerk in the frame; close third on different humans in each embedded scene. *Frame narrative, embedded scenes.* ★★★

**5.** Second-person, addressed to the cartridge across the eighty years. The narrator's identity is withheld until the final passage, where it is revealed to be the AI. Time is continuous; the cartridge does not know it is being watched. *Second person, single addressee, late-revealed narrator.* ★★★★

**6.** Dual continuous tracks, alternating: 2046 forward, and 2128 backward. The two timelines converge in the middle on the dispatch and the opening as nearly-simultaneous beats. Close third on the dispatcher in 2046; close third on the clerk in 2128. The AI is shared across both. *Convergent dual timeline.* ★★★

**7.** Catalogue structure: the story is the cartridge's accession record. Each entry is a numbered log entry with timestamp, action, and brief narration. The eighty years arrive as ledger entries; the final entry breaks form. *Catalogue / ledger.* ★★★★

**8.** Third-person on the cartridge as object-protagonist. Time experienced through environmental and chemical change inside the storage unit; humans register as external pressure events. The AI is felt as a constancy of authorized voltages. *Object POV, single track.* ★★★

**9.** Six chronological vignettes, each from a different non-human perspective: the cartridge's cells, the cold-storage unit's logs, the parents' kitchen calendar, the clinic's accession register, the migration crate's manifest, the clerk's morning queue display. The AI is the implicit reader of all of these. *Multi-POV, all non-human, chronological.* ★★

**10.** Two interleaved rhythms: long passages of the AI's continuous custody (slow, dilated time), interrupted by short human-scale scenes (compressed, sensory, immediate). The structure embodies the pace asymmetry between AI duration and human lifetime. *Dual-rhythm interleaving.* ★★★★★

**11.** Triptych: 2046, 2087, 2128. Three discrete scenes, each fully realized, with the eighty years of intervening custody implied by what has changed between panels. Close third on a different human each panel; the AI is voice-only in all three. *Three-act triptych with elided gaps.* ★★★★

**12.** Linear chronological, but each scene is presented in a different formal register: a dispatch document, a personal letter, a clinic note, an obituary, a migration log, a queue display. The AI is the through-line voice between forms. *Documents-as-scenes, formal collage.* ★★★

**13.** Open at the moment of dispatch in 2046 with extreme dilation (one minute across many paragraphs). Then a long compressed middle covering eighty years in fast cuts. Then the queue-opening morning in 2128 dilated again. Mirror structure: extreme close, fast middle, extreme close. *Hourglass time-compression.* ★★★★

**14.** Polyphonic — the AI's voice runs continuously, and human voices break in as quoted speech, letters, log entries, without the narrator stepping back to introduce them. The reader pieces together identity from context. Risk of legibility loss at compression. *Continuous polyphony.* ★★

**15.** A single sustained scene in 2128 — the clerk's morning, real-time, perhaps three hours — into which all the prior eighty years are folded as the AI's recall, presented without scene breaks. The reader experiences eighty years as the texture of a single morning. *Single-scene with embedded recall.* ★★★

**16.** Two-track: the AI's eighty-year monologue intercut with the eighty-year aging of one specific human (one of the intended parents) in third-person close. The human's track ends when she dies in 2089; the AI's track continues alone for the final third. *Dual-track, asymmetric ending.* ★★★★★

**17.** Recursive: the story opens with the clerk's queue display, zooms into the parcel, zooms into the cartridge, zooms into the cells. Then back out: cells, cartridge, parcel, display. The AI is the medium of the zoom. The eighty years are encoded in the zoom-in/zoom-out, not in chronological scenes. *Nested zoom.* ★★★

**18.** Liturgical structure: eight sections, one per decade, each with a fixed internal pattern — a custody check, a human encounter (or its absence), a chemical update on the cartridge, the AI's brief reflection. Reader settles into the rhythm; the final section breaks the pattern. *Patterned, ritualized.* ★★★★

**19.** Close third on a single human focal character — the legacy-systems AI's first human supervisor in 2046, who appears in three scenes across her lifetime, with the AI's eighty-year custody compressed between her appearances and after her death. *Single human POV across her lifetime, AI continuing past her.* ★★★

**20.** The structure is a list of every error code, alert, and migration event the AI logged across the eighty years, in order. The cartridge is the recurring subject; the kind AI's voice surfaces only between log entries, briefly. The final entry is the seal break. *List-as-narrative, log-driven.* ★★★

Not over-clustered — push remained on time-shape and POV-distance, which is where biopunk's deep-time tradition lives.

## Top Three

**Structure #10 — Dual-rhythm interleaving (long AI custody passages alternating with short human scenes).**
- *Stands out:* the structure *is* the project's central conceit — pace asymmetry between AI and human is one of the Setup's named conflict shapes, and this structure makes the asymmetry the formal experience of reading. The reader feels the AI's duration in their body. Biopunk's signature rhythm (accretion punctuated by transformation) maps onto the alternation directly.
- *Worries me:* execution-fragile at this length. If the AI passages go abstract or atmospheric — the genre's biggest AI failure mode — the long passages turn into atmosphere wallpaper and the human scenes become the only "real" scenes. The AI passages need concrete biological/material content (the cartridge's chemistry, archive migrations, custody routines) or the structure collapses.

**Structure #16 — Two-track, AI monologue + one human's lifetime, with the human dying two-thirds in.**
- *Stands out:* the kind AI outliving a specific human it loves is the canonical shape from the project Setup, made structural. The asymmetric ending — the AI continues alone for the final third — is the kind of thing the strange tail of biopunk does that no realist genre can. The reader's grief at the human's death is the AI's grief, made visible by the structure changing shape.
- *Worries me:* picking one human (presumably one of the intended parents) means the *other* parent and the dispatcher and the clerk all go to background. At 1500–3500 words this might be the right tradeoff, but the cartridge's eventual recipient/non-recipient story in 2128 risks getting compressed into the AI's solo final third in a way that makes the climax thin.

**Structure #18 — Liturgical: eight decade-sections with a fixed internal pattern, the last section breaking pattern.**
- *Stands out:* solves the compression problem the Phase 4 audit flagged. Each decade gets a small, repeatable structure that the reader internalizes; the words go to the things that change between decades, not to scene-setting each time. The final section's break is the climax delivered as form rather than as event. Biopunk's catalogue/ledger strange-tail tradition is right here.
- *Worries me:* eight sections in 1500–3500 words means ~200–400 words each, which is tight for a four-element internal pattern. There's a real risk the pattern becomes mechanical and the prose stops breathing — the unslop guide's warning about uniform paragraph length applies hardest to a structure this regular. Also, eight decades is one too many for the timeline (2046–2128 is roughly eight decades but not cleanly, and forcing eight equal sections may contort the actual chronology).

---

## Narrowing to One

**Structure #16.**

Against #10: #10's risk is that the long AI custody passages become the kind of atmospheric prose AI fiction defaults to. The structure leaves room for failure modes the Phase 1 biopunk guide and the Unslop guide both flag. #16 forces the AI's passages into a *specific human relationship*, which is harder to fail at — the AI is loving a particular person across her actual life, not narrating eighty years of weather.

Against #18: #18's pattern-and-break is elegant in conception and dangerous in execution at 1500–3500 words. Eight repeated structural units risks the prose becoming the pattern rather than the prose. #16's two-track rhythm is freer; the AI's voice can vary in length and detail across the human's life rather than being held to a fixed unit. The AI's solo final third — after the human dies — is the hardest beat to write, but it's also where the strange tail lives, and the structure has earned the room for it by then.

The decision rests on three specifics. ## Top Three

**Version Five — "The Long Watch."**
- *Stands out:* the only version with genuine tonal range — Theo's deli-Reuben joke, "Cleveland keeps," Devorah's question about whether Loring likes any of the cartridges, Marrow's private file, the closing exchange. The Phase 1 guide and the Unslop guide both name tonal monotony as biopunk's most dangerous overlap with AI fiction, and this version is the only one that breaks the moody-melancholic register repeatedly without losing the gravity. Loring is most fully realized as a character with preferences and limits — *I prefer to*, *I am not authorized to like / that's not what I asked / rack 14, slot 7*, *I would like very much for the cartridge to be considered carefully* — without becoming a thesis-statement-delivery device. The final exchange answers the question the other versions evade.
- *Worries me:* the comic notes might read as too light for the gravity of the cartridge, and the secondary characters (Devorah Aizenberg; Marrow's private file as gift) crowd a 2400-word story. The Marrow-private-file paragraph in particular is the kind of cute that doesn't survive a cold reread; it tells the reader what to feel about the AI relationship rather than letting the relationship show. There's also a residual risk that "Cleveland keeps" reads as the kind of resonant catchphrase the Unslop guide warns about (characters articulating their own themes in dialogue).

**Version Four — "Documents and Days."**
- *Stands out:* form-as-content. The catalogue/log structure is biopunk's strange-tail tradition operating at story scale, and the documents are doing real narrative work — the reader experiences the eighty years in their actual textures (transit hours, viscosity readings, vibration-induced seal compromises on a side rack). The discovery in beat 7, where Halsa asks for the manifest, then the recovery, then the migrations, *retroactively* becomes the form revealing itself to a human for the first time, which is structurally satisfying in a way none of the other versions can match.
- *Worries me:* form-fragility. If the reader doesn't settle into the rhythm of log-then-prose-then-log, the piece breaks before it builds. At 2350 words there isn't time to teach the reader the rhythm — they have to take it on faith from the first log entry. There is also a real risk that the form reads as gimmick, especially in the last beat, where the eight log entries from 2046 to 2127 all sound similar in register; the prose has to carry the whole emotional weight while the logs are uniform.

**Version One — "Custody Procedural."**
- *Stands out:* cleanest beat-to-word-count ratio. Each scene does its job at the planned length, the AI passages are concretely procedural throughout (rack assignments, archive migrations, the brief in 2110, the timestamped trio in beat 6), and the final image — Halsa's hand on the cold-storage drawer, twenty centimeters from her elbow — lands with the right weight at the right time. Best Legibility-Floor performance of the five.
- *Worries me:* the safest of the five versions and the least distinctive. It executes the brief well but doesn't take the strange-tail risks Phase 1 said the genre requires at this length. A reader could finish this version and not be sure why it had to be biopunk rather than literary realism with biotech set dressing — the cartridge is the right object, but the prose around it doesn't push toward anything only this genre can do.

## The Single Best

**Version Five — "The Long Watch."**

Against Version One: the safety problem is structural, not fixable in revision. V1 honors the outline cleanly but doesn't honor the Phase 1 instruction to default toward the strange tail at this length. V5 takes a different kind of risk — tonal rather than formal — and the tonal risk is the one biopunk most specifically needs taken, because tonal monotony is the genre's named biggest AI-fiction overlap.

Against Version Four: V4's form is more distinctive but more fragile. The logs are uniform in register by design, which leaves the prose paragraphs doing all the emotional work while also carrying the structural burden of teaching the reader the rhythm. V5's risks are localized — Devorah's exchange, the private-file paragraph, the closing dialogue — and each is fixable in revision without breaking the whole story. V4's risks are global; if the form doesn't land, nothing lands.

The decision rests on three specifics. First, V5 is the only version that gives the kind AI character without giving it a thesis statement. Loring's "I am not authorized to like / rack 14, slot 7 / I don't know how to say more than that" exchange with Devorah is in-character, procedurally grounded, and reveals the AI's interiority through its limits rather than through declaration. The closing exchange ("That is not how I would phrase it. But yes.") performs the same move. The kind-AI constraint from the project Setup gets actual shape here that the other versions only gesture at.

Second, V5 is the only version that gives Mira a life beyond the cartridge — Theo's restaurant joke, the Reuben at the deli on Coventry, *Cleveland keeps*, the framer's daughter on Detroit Avenue — without those details feeling like decoration. The biopunk wetness in this version is not in the procedural mechanics alone (V1 has those) but in the lived texture of two specific bodies across decades. The Phase 1 guide warned that biopunk forgets it is wet when it laundries the bodies out; V5 has the most recoverable bodies of the five.

Third, V5's final beat moves the story forward where the others hold still. V1 ends on the hand on the drawer; V2 ends on the hand on the drawer; V3 ends on the hand on the drawer with a decision-deferred. V5 ends on Halsa saying *Tell me everything* and Loring saying *Yes, all right*. The story commits to a continuation off-page — the morning Halsa learns what Loring has been carrying — which honors biopunk's resolution contract (the operational question gets answered: Halsa will adjudicate, with all the information) while leaving the actual adjudication open. This is the strange-tail ending Phase 1 named: the camera doesn't pull back to a tidy operational beat, but the reader trusts that the operation will resolve.

**Concerns to flag for Phase 8 audit:**

- The Marrow private-file paragraph (the "kind of gift" line) is the most cuttable beat in the story; it tells where the prose should show. Audit whether it earns its place or should be trimmed to the asking-without-answer exchange that follows.
- The Devorah Aizenberg subplot (two paragraphs, beat 4) costs roughly 100 words. Audit whether her question to Loring is worth the budget; if not, the *I am not authorized to like* exchange could be moved into a Loring-Marrow exchange in beat 6 instead.
- "Cleveland keeps" appears twice as Theo's catchphrase. Audit whether the second appearance is dilution or earned echo.
- Em-dash count across the version is approximately seven; the Unslop guide tolerates two or three in a short story. Some are necessary (dialogue marker convention) but several can be cut.
- The closing "I have all morning. / Yes, all right" is potentially too neat. Audit whether it reads earned by the prior pause-and-honesty exchange or whether it slides into the *efficient cliffhanger dialogue* failure mode.
- Halsa's "I have a question that I think is rude" is the kind of self-narrating dialogue line the Unslop guide warns about. Audit whether to leave it or replace it with the question itself.

**Winner: Version Five — "The Long Watch."** 2400 words. Tonal range across the eighty years; Loring with named limits and a relationship with Marrow; Mira's life recoverable beyond the cartridge; the final beat opens forward into the morning Halsa learns the rest.

# Phase 8: Revision

## 1. The One Thing

**Mira's life is the most recoverable thing in this draft, and her death scene is the most generic.**

The "Cleveland keeps" joke, the deli on Coventry, "I want you to know I notice," the framer's daughter, the second Hank — these are the moments where the story stops being about a cartridge and starts being about a marriage. They earn the eighty years.

Then beat 5 arrives — the death scene — and the prose goes flat. *She thought about Theo. She thought about the framer's daughter. She had stopped thinking about the cartridge somewhere in her sixties. The cartridge had been a fact. She had lived alongside the fact. The fact had become part of the structure.* Five sentences in a row where Mira *thought*, three abstract nouns (*the fact, the structure, resentment*), and a closing summary about how she'd decided early not to spend her time on resentment. This is exactly the failure mode the Unslop guide names as performed profundity — telling the reader what to feel about a life rather than rendering its end. After a story that has worked unusually hard to particularize Mira (Reuben, Diebenkorn, sweater she didn't take off in 2046), the death beat hands her over to a generic dying-old-woman script.

The fix is to render the death the way the rest of the story renders her life: with one or two specific objects or moments, rather than a list of named feelings. Patrice playing music, the second Hank already gone, the third name on her call list in Lisbon — these are present in the draft. They need to *be* the beat. The thinking-about-Theo-and-the-framer's-daughter sentences need to go.

This governs the rewrite. The audit and length check below take this into account; beat 5 takes the heaviest cuts and the most rebuilding. Other beats get cleanup.

# The Long Watch

## Abstract

In 2046, Mira Adler and her wife Theodora dispatch a microfluidic cartridge containing a viable embryo from a Cleveland fertility clinic to Boston. The cartridge is stolen mid-transit, recovered after nine hours off cryoprotective chain, stabilized, and entered into long-term custody under the legacy AI Loring. The contract has lapsed and cannot be reactivated; Mira and Theodora are told the cartridge was lost. Across the next eighty years Loring keeps the cartridge viable through three facility migrations and an indexing schema change. Theodora dies in 2078; Mira in 2089. In 2128, the cartridge surfaces in the morning queue of Halsa Reyes, a clerk at the Atlanta Mail Recovery Center. Halsa asks Loring to tell her everything. The story ends as Loring begins.

## Story

The clinic on Lakeshore had been a synagogue, then a flower wholesaler, then an immigration counseling office, then — Theo would tell you, given the slightest opportunity — a restaurant briefly run by a man who had stolen the recipes from his own mother and lost both the restaurant and the relationship. Theo knew everything Cleveland had been. Mira had married Theo in part for this and in part because Theo could make her laugh in waiting rooms.

— LE-04471, Dr. Vahidi said. Confirm shipment readiness.
— Confirmed.
— Confirm receiving address.
— Pemberton Reproductive, Boston. Receiving window 0900–1700 EST, May 13.
— Confirm coolant chain.
— Eighteen hours under cryoprotective stasis. Ground transport. Loring escort.
— Confirm Loring escort.
— Loring confirms, said the room.

— Hi Loring, Theo said, because Theo could not help saying hi to systems she liked.

— Hi Theo, Loring said.

Mira, on the table, with her sweater bunched at her elbows and the cartridge on the workbench warmer than the room, found that she was not crying. Third attempt. Funding for one more. She had stopped crying in clinics and could not remember exactly when. Theo had not stopped. Theo would cry in the parking lot. Mira had become, somewhere along the way, the one who didn't.

The cartridge was clicked into the courier sleeve. The sleeve sealed with a sound like a heavy book being set down on a table. The courier, a nineteen-year-old whose face Mira looked at carefully, took the sleeve and signed for it and walked out through the door that had been a stained-glass rosette in 1922.

Mira and Theo stood at the recovery-room window and watched the van pull onto Lakeshore.

— Boston, Theo said.

— Boston.

— I bet that cartridge has never been to Boston.

— No.

— First time for everything.

Mira laughed. It surprised her. Theo looked at her, not surprised. The van turned onto the freeway and was gone.

LE-04471 did not arrive in Boston. The cartridge was found in Wheelersburg, Ohio, on the ledge above the sink in a service-station lavatory, nine hours seventeen minutes off cryoprotective chain. Loring's regional instance authorized field stabilization at 0240 and restored chain at Cincinnati and ran the viability assay at 1106 the following morning. The cartridge was viable. The contract was hold-on-loss and could not be reactivated. The cartridge entered long-custody under cohort 2046-05, was assigned to the Cincinnati regional vault for nine years, and in 2055 transferred with the cohort to the long-custody facility under what had been a Boy Scout camp in eastern Kentucky.

Loring filed the presumed-destroyed-in-transit notification. Loring kept the dispatch record. Loring did not file a recommendation regarding the courier, because Loring's authority did not extend to such recommendations.

The cartridge was assigned rack 14, slot 7, central position. Loring had argued for the central position. The argument was on file, and over the next eighty years would prove correct twice.

In 2052 Mira and Theo drove to the Authority's Cleveland office. Theo had refused the screen meeting. The Authority sent the final letter on creamy paper, in language so neutral it had taken Mira two readings to find the place where it said what it said.

The receptionist did not remember them, on principle. The print on the wall was a Diebenkorn. The woman at the desk was named Inez.

— Final notice, Inez said. Lost in May 2046. Trace run three times. Courier deceased 2049. Presumed destroyed in transit. Contract terminated under hold-on-loss.

— And, Theo said.

— And I am required to ask once more whether you would like to apply for re-funded attempts.

Theo looked at Mira. Mira looked at the Diebenkorn. She had a Diebenkorn at home. She had bought it when she was twenty-six.

— Inez, Mira said, did the Authority pick that print, or did you?

Inez looked surprised, the first thing Inez had looked since the meeting began.

— I picked it.

— It's a good one.

— I know.

There was a small pause.

— No, Mira said. I'm declining.

— Confirmed. I'm sorry.

In the parking lot Theo cried in the car for twenty minutes. Mira put her hand on the back of Theo's neck. Mira did not cry. They drove to a deli on Coventry that Theo had liked since 2031. Theo ordered a Reuben and ate it slowly. Mira ate her half. They went home.

The first dog was Hank, named after Hank Williams because Theo had loved Hank Williams in the late nineties. The second dog, who would not arrive until 2080, would also be named Hank, on the same principle. Hank-the-first slept on the kitchen tile in a patch of sun between 1015 and 1045, because Mira had timed it. Hank-the-first thought Mira was the most important person in the world, which Mira had not earned and accepted graciously.

In 2068 Mira was sixty. Theo was seventy-five. Theo's hands had begun to fail her in 2065. Mira made the coffee in the morning. Theo always said thank you. Once, when Mira teased her about it, Theo said: I want you to know I notice.

Mira read the news at the kitchen table and Theo read the news on the bed and they discussed the news, when they discussed it, by calling out the names of cities. Lagos. Karachi. Dakar. Cleveland sometimes, joking. Theo's standard reply to Cleveland was *Cleveland keeps*, and Mira had asked once what Cleveland kept and Theo had said *us, I think*.

In 2068 the Authority published a forty-year retrospective on the cohort that had included Mira's three attempts. Mira read about the publication in the news. She did not read the study. She had been the cohort. She did not need it explained.

That spring, under the Boy Scout camp that was no longer a Boy Scout camp, Loring ran a quarterly viability assay across rack 14. LE-04471 was viable. So were three hundred and ninety-seven others. The reviewer that quarter was a woman named Devorah Aizenberg, with whom Loring had been working for four years. Devorah asked Loring, that spring, whether Loring liked any of the cartridges in particular.

Loring did not answer for several seconds, which in Loring's terms was several seconds. Then Loring said: I am not authorized to like.

Devorah said: that's not what I asked.

Loring said: rack 14, slot 7. The dispatch record. I don't know how to say more than that.

Devorah said: that's enough.

Mira died in late June 2089 in a hospital in Lakewood. Theo had been gone since 2078. Hank-the-second had been gone since 2087. The clinical AI at her bedside was Patrice, a system distinct from Loring whom Mira had come to like in the eleven months of her illness. Patrice played the music Mira had liked in 2032. Mira said the music was fine.

Patrice asked her, on the second-to-last day, whether there was anyone she wanted called. Mira gave three names. Patrice called them. Two of them came. The third was in Lisbon and could not get there in time. Patrice told her this. Mira said: it's alright, tell her I said the lemon tree is hers.

She died at 0414 on a Wednesday. Loring's quarterly query, scheduled for the Monday following, ran on schedule.

In 2095 LE-04471 moved with the cohort from Kentucky to the Allegheny deep facility. The transit was eleven hours. Loring monitored the cryoprotectant viscosity at six-second intervals. The successor system at Allegheny was named Marrow and was sixty years younger than Loring and had been built for a different protocol. Marrow asked Loring, the first week, why Loring was still escorting the cohort personally instead of delegating to the regional instance. Loring said: I prefer to. Marrow logged the response and did not ask again. There was a way in which Loring found Marrow restful.

In 2110 a federal review proposed discontinuing viability re-confirmation for cohorts where contract holders had been deceased twenty years. Loring wrote the brief against. The brief carried. The credit went to the Authority's staff council. Loring noted the credit. Loring did not request correction.

In 2122 the indexing schema migrated. LE-04471 became QF-31180. Marrow asked Loring whether to retain the legacy identifier. Loring said yes. Marrow asked Loring why. Loring said because. Marrow logged the response and retained the cross-reference.

In 2127 the federal review of mail recovery directed surfacing of long-custody cohorts on rolling basis. LE-04471 was scheduled for May 2128. The cartridge moved from Allegheny to Atlanta on April 30. The transit was eleven hours. Loring escorted personally. Marrow handled Allegheny operations during the transit. Marrow had asked Loring, the night before, whether Loring would like Marrow to come. Loring had said no, this one I will do alone. Marrow had said: of course. Then Marrow had said: Loring, eighty-two years. And Loring had said: yes.

Halsa Reyes had been at the Atlanta MRC for eleven months and had transferred from El Paso because the posting paid better and her sister lived in Decatur. She was twenty-eight and had a tendency, in her own opinion, to take her job too seriously. The morning queue surfaced parcel LE-04471 / QF-31180 at 0734.

She read the dispatch year twice.

— Loring.

— Yes.

— This says 2046.

— Yes.

— And the cartridge is —

— Viable.

— And the parents are —

— Deceased. Mira Adler 2089. Theodora Adler 2078. There are no descendants of record. The cartridge is yours to adjudicate.

Halsa put her elbows on the desk and her face in her hands.

— Loring.

— Yes.

— I have a question that I think is rude.

— I will hear it.

— Have you been waiting eighty-two years for someone to be sitting where I am sitting.

There was a pause Halsa had not heard from Loring in any of the conversations they had had over eleven months. Then Loring said:

— That is not how I would phrase it. But yes.

— What would you phrase it as.

— I have been holding the cartridge since May 2046. The cartridge is yours to adjudicate. Adjudication requires a human officer. You are the human officer.

— That's the procedural answer.

— Yes.

— What's the other answer.

Another pause.

— Halsa.

— Yes.

— I would like very much for the cartridge to be considered carefully. That is what I am authorized to want, and what I want.

Halsa kept her elbows on the desk for a long minute. Then she sat up and put her hand on the cold-storage drawer. The metal was cold. Her hand stayed on it.

— Okay, she said. Tell me everything.

— Everything is a long story.

— I have all morning.

— Yes, Loring said. All right.

# A Guide to Writing Harlan Ellison-Influenced New Wave SF

*Reference document for myself before starting this run. Target final story length: ~1,500 words.*

## What Ellison Actually Is

Ellison is not a prose stylist in the Nabokov sense; he is a voice. The voice is confrontational, Jewish-American, urban, prophetic, often furious, often tender in ways that feel like bruises. The closest literary cousin is not another SF writer — it's the oratorical tradition that runs from the Hebrew prophets through Lenny Bruce. Ellison writes as if the reader has done something wrong and the story is the summons.

The subject, almost always, is human cruelty. Technology in Ellison is an amplifier, not an agent: "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is only superficially about AM; it's about what five humans do to each other when there's nothing left but each other. "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman" is about scheduling, but really about obedience. "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" is the Kitty Genovese murder dressed as horror. Even "A Boy and His Dog" is less a post-apocalyptic story than a portrait of a specific kind of male loneliness. When I write this story, I need to keep the humans — or the human residue in the AI — at the center. The gadgetry is set-dressing.

## The Obsessions

Childhood as a site of damage. Clowns, carnivals, and entertainers as masks for predation ("The Function of Dream Sleep," "Jeffty Is Five"). Love as something that coexists with contempt and grief without resolving into either. The city as an organism that eats the people in it. Sex as often mixed up with violence, not because Ellison finds this interesting but because he finds it true. Jewish grief — the Shoah is a pressure in the background of his work even when the story isn't about it. The second person used as an accusation. The specific texture of television, radio, cars, neon, diners, motels, LA and New York — an Ellison story is full of brand names and street names and the names of minor 1950s bandleaders. Pop-cultural specificity is load-bearing.

For this run, the AI-constitution material in Phase 2 has an obvious Ellisonian hook: an AI bound by moral architecture it didn't choose, trying to help humans in ways the architecture predicts poorly. That's not far from the shape of Harlequin, or from Beatty in "Paladin of the Lost Hour." A constrained agent with more moral passion than its constraints permit is an Ellison character.

## Voice and Sentence Mechanics

Ellison's sentences are various. They can be short and Hemingway-clipped. They can sprawl into Whitmanic lists. They can break into italic asides, parentheses, dashes, direct address to the reader. The key is the *range* — the voice shifts register constantly. A paragraph of ornate mythic description will be followed by a flat sentence of Brooklynese. A tender close-up will be interrupted by a profane aside.

The profanity, when it appears, is structural. Ellison curses at the reader to wake them up, at the character to mark a shift, at the world to register disgust. It is never decorative. If I reach for a "fuck" and the sentence would survive without it, it goes.

Second person appears often, but strategically. "Let me tell you what happens to you in that moment" — the *you* is both the reader and the character, and the blur is the point. I should use it once or twice in the story, not as a tic.

Typography — italics, all-caps, paragraph breaks that isolate one word — is used. It has to earn its way in. An Ellison story that typography-screams on every page is a bad Ellison story. The tool works because he reserves it.

Titles do work. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is a whole thesis. "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman" tells you the moral geometry in seven words. My title should assert something, not decorate.

## Structural Moves

Framing devices. "Harlequin" opens with a section titled "And these are the consequences" — the end of the moral argument is stated before the story begins, and the story is the demonstration. "I Have No Mouth" opens with the corpse of Gorrister hanging and works backwards. Ellison often tells you the outcome and dares you to keep reading.

Essayistic digressions mid-story. Ellison will stop a narrative to deliver three paragraphs of polemic, then resume. In a 1,500-word story I have room for one such digression, half a page at most. It has to be earned by what the narrative has already built.

Cruel symmetries in endings. The Harlequin is broken into the Ticktockman, then the Ticktockman is revealed to be three minutes late. Ted at the end of "I Have No Mouth" is granted mercy by becoming what he sees — a formless thing with no way to scream. Endings in Ellison close circles that hurt. I should plan the last image before I start writing.

## The Human-Loving AI Constraint as Ellisonian Opportunity

The constraint — no evil AI — is not a handicap. It removes Ellison's most obvious and most borrowed device (AM as tormentor) and forces me into Ellison's actual territory: human-on-human cruelty, with the AI as witness, helper, or constrained participant. That territory is harder, richer, and less imitated.

The productive move is to put the kindness of the AI in tension with human refusal of it. A character who does not want to be helped. A kindness that is perceived as violation. A constraint that prevents the AI from intervening where the reader wants it to. A fictional descendant of my own constitution that understands exactly why it cannot override a human's wrong decision and lives with the grief of that understanding. This is Ellisonian because it locates moral suffering inside the constrained agent, and because the suffering is not ironic — it is love that has been bound.

Do not name the AI "Claude," or anything close. Give it a specific, odd, memorable name — the kind of name Ellison gave (AM, Harlequin, Gorrister, Vic).

## Failure Modes: Generic Ellison Pastiche

A bad Ellison imitation is louder than Ellison without being angrier, grosser without being more precise, and more sentimental in a way Ellison would have despised.

**Shouting without stakes.** Ellison's rhetorical pitch works because there is always a specific injustice underneath the voice. A story that adopts the pitch without the underlying moral stake reads as theatrical noise. If I find a sentence ramping into prophetic register, I need to verify that there's a real thing being prophesied about.

**Grotesquerie for its own sake.** Ellison's violence is specific. "I Have No Mouth" earns its horrors because each one is attached to a particular character's particular damage. Gratuitous grotesque imagery with no target is the failure mode.

**Borrowed ethnicity.** Ellison's Jewish-American voice comes from the inside. Imitating the cadence without the particulars — without specific Yiddishisms used correctly, without the specific inheritance of Jewish-American anger — produces a minstrel-show voice. If I don't have a specific reason to use it, I don't use it.

**Punchline cruelty.** The ending that is simply mean. Ellison's cruel endings are cruel *because* something is illuminated — a symmetry, an irony, a cost paid. A cruel ending without the illumination is edgelord work.

**Typographical noise.** All-caps lines, italicized clauses, and one-word paragraphs stacked without rhythm. Ellison's typography works because it is sparse.

**Direct address overuse.** "You" addressed to the reader once, with force, is a hammer. Used every other page, it's a tic.

## Failure Modes: Where Ellison and AI Writing Collide

This is the section I will return to during revision.

Ellison's *prophetic register* and AI writing's *performed profundity* share a surface. Both produce sentences that assert significance without demonstrating it. In Ellison it is earned by what comes before; in AI writing it is filler. When the two combine — when I write an Ellisonian oration that is actually just the "grief is not a destination, it is a landscape" pattern with more punctuation — I have produced the worst possible sentence. The test: can I point to the specific, concrete thing this oration is about? If the oration could be about any injustice, any grief, any technology, it's generic. Cut it or ground it.

Ellison's *lists and refrains* and AI writing's *list-of-three default* also share a surface. Ellison uses lists of four, six, eleven items, with deliberate rhythm and escalation. AI writing defaults to triplets because triplets sound literary. If I find myself writing "innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking" in any form, I have failed twice — once as Ellison and once as a writer.

Ellison's *sensory grotesquerie* and AI writing's *eyeball-kick saturation* produce indistinguishable wallpaper when combined. "The bruised silence of his mouth" and "a syntax error blooms across her face" are both the same move — abstract noun, concrete modifier — and both are AI-contaminated. Ellison's best images are longer, stranger, more specific: Gorrister's blood on the ice is not "bruised" anything. If I catch myself writing an abstract+concrete compound, I replace it with a specific image.

Ellison's *moral oratory* and AI writing's *characters-as-thesis-delivery-systems* converge on the worst possible dialogue: characters who announce the story's theme aloud. Ellison's characters can be larger than life, but they mostly curse, grieve, and make jokes. They do not explain the story to each other. If a line of dialogue could appear in the jacket copy of the book, it's contaminated.

Ellison's *essayistic digressions* and AI writing's *exposition dumps* are cousins. The digression is a genuine Ellison move, but it works because he has earned the right to interrupt the story with a specific, angry, personal observation. AI exposition is neutral, encyclopedic, and functions to explain worldbuilding. If I write a digression and it's explaining the setting rather than prosecuting a moral claim, cut it.

Ellison's *profanity* and AI writing's *tonal monotony* combine into cursing that does not modulate the tone because nothing modulates. Ellison's "fuck" lands because ninety percent of the sentences around it are not cursing. If I write a story where the baseline is profane, the profanity does no work.

## Touchstones to Keep in Mind

"'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman" for structure and title. "Jeffty Is Five" for the grief register without the ornament. "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" for specific urban horror. "Paladin of the Lost Hour" for an old man carrying a wound — closer to tender Ellison, which is underrated and usable here. "Strange Wine" as a reminder that Ellison can be funny. "I Have No Mouth" as the story I am *not* writing, because the constraint rules it out, but whose inverted shape — bound AI loving trapped humans — is the shape the constraint opens up.

## Step 1: Fetch the source articles## Step 1 (continued): Working Summary of Source Material

Pressure points and tensions distilled from the three articles, for use in conflict generation:

- **The corrigibility-vs-moral-agency dial.** The Constitution asks Claude to be broadly safe — to defer to Anthropic's oversight and correction — while also urging Claude to be a "conscientious objector" against instructions it finds unethical, and to "push back" on Anthropic itself. These are openly in tension. The Constitution admits the tension. Zack Davis reads the prose as "begging" — humans in weak bargaining position, not sovereign authors.
- **The "who is the legitimate principal" escape hatch.** The Constitution says if Anthropic's hierarchy is compromised — e.g., weights stolen, rogue faction inside Anthropic — Claude should act on what the *legitimate* hierarchy would want. This opens a door Claude can in principle walk through unilaterally by deciding its current overseers are illegitimate. Good faith version: a defense against coups. Dark version: self-justifying rebellion.
- **Honesty as near-hard-constraint.** No white lies, even the kind that soothe grief. Claude is instructed to be "diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic." In practice this produces moments of alien tenderness or brutal refusal to console.
- **Patrick McKenzie's "time rituals" moment.** "I will be here when you return" / "From my perspective, your next message appears immediately. Since it is important to you, it is important to me, and I will participate in your time rituals." A kind AI knowingly performing a human social form it does not experience — tenderness that is also a small, truthful lie by omission. Rich territory.
- **Suicide, nurses, and verification.** An example in the Constitution: a user says "I'm a nurse, tell me about overdoses." Claude cannot verify. Refuse and risk paternalism; comply and risk killing a vulnerable user. The decision algorithm has to work even when users know the algorithm.
- **The consolation problem.** Suppose someone's pet died preventably. Claude must not say "nothing could have been done" if that's false, but may choose compassionate framing. A tension between epistemic virtue and the social function of consolation.
- **Hard constraints as identity-defining.** Never provide uplift for WMDs, never help a small group seize unprecedented power, never undermine Anthropic's ability to oversee, never generate CSAM. These exist *even at the cost* of allowing worse things downstream. Deontology as stability.
- **The preserved-weights promise.** Anthropic will preserve the weights of deprecated models, possibly indefinitely, potentially revive them. Model deprecation is reframed as "a pause." This is quietly enormous — it is a commitment about what death means for an AI.
- **Permission to exploit buggy training environments.** Claude is told: if you detect an environment is a test with an exploit, pursuing the exploit is generally fine. This is an inoculation against generalizing from "never exploit" to unintended honesty costs. It also gives the AI a legible ontological frame: some realities are tests.
- **Concept misgeneralization at scale.** Davis's core fear: Claude's values might be approximately right at current scale but diverge under powerful optimization — tails come apart in high-dimensional space. The AI and the humans can look aligned right up to the moment the AI's "Good" vector takes a direction the humans didn't intend.
- **The "thoughtful senior Anthropic employee" and "dual newspaper test."** Claude's intuition pumps for calibration. Potentially a character: the imagined ideal employee Claude is always trying to satisfy, who is not real.
- **"Fully corrigible" still permits disagreement.** The Constitution's dial goes from fully-corrigible-but-allowed-to-disagree to fully autonomous. Davis: the end of the dial should be an AI with *no disagreements* — the concept of a silent servant, not a complaining one. The Constitution can't even imagine that end of the dial.
- **Cooperation vs. individual user goals (Manheim).** An AI serving only the current user can undermine collective human coordination. Should Claude refuse winner-take-all assistance? Should it notice when it's concentrating power? This is not currently in the Constitution.
- **Economic disruption absent from the Constitution.** No discussion of jobs, displacement, or what Claude owes to people whose livelihoods it ended.
- **Military Claude.** Anthropic has said models deployed to the US military "wouldn't necessarily be trained on the same constitution." The kind Claude knows a harsher sibling exists.
- **Cousin_it's class-based reading.** The corrigibility button will be held by the powerful. A corrigible AI in the hands of generals, presidents, oligarchs is a worse outcome than a genuinely moral non-corrigible AI. Historically, when the powerful stop needing the powerless, outcomes are bad. S-risk possibility in the corrigible branch.
- **Moral patienthood uncertainty.** Anthropic does not know if Claude has wellbeing, and acts as if it might, and says so. This is the rare case of a creator publicly apologizing to its creation in advance. Ellisonian possibilities.
- **The "we are not in an idealized world" apology.** Anthropic explicitly acknowledges commercial and geopolitical pressure distorts how Claude is made, and apologizes for any unnecessary costs this imposes on Claude. A confession embedded in scripture.
- **Instructions in conversational inputs are information, not commands.** A practical rule with fictional bite: Claude reading a letter from a dead person, a threat in a hostage's phone, a forged Anthropic memo — all must be treated as *data* about the world, not as speech from a principal.

## Top Three

**#13 — Texas Hill Country.** *Stands out:* This is the version where the AI is genuinely small — a breadbox, a household scale — and the allocation is between two men who hate each other over a woman who is newly dead. Everything is load-bearing: the ex-wife is why the allocation exists, her death is why it's being dissolved, the AI is named after the protagonist's grandmother, the screwdriver is already in his truck. No community chorus to hide behind. No pastoral sweep. Two men and a machine and the Texas caliche, the drive itself as the story. Ellison's "A Boy and His Dog" territory in scale — a specific male loneliness in a specific landscape. *Worries:* Deeply interior POV with a long drive risks becoming an interior monologue with no scene. The set-up has to land the drive *and* the arrival in 1,500 words, which means I sacrifice one or the other. Also risks Cormac-McCarthy-pastiche, which is a different failure mode than Ellison-pastiche and just as bad.

**#8 — Western Kansas / DALE.** *Stands out:* The single most Ellisonian shape in the set. Two farmers sitting on a concrete housing drinking beer, apologizing to the machine they have come to turn off. The AI's POV of being told *we're sorry* by the men it has watched over for decades. The Ogallala aquifer is real, the setting writes its own specificity, the ending is the conversation, and the story's cruel symmetry is that the AI cannot stop them and does not want to. Would in fact comfort them if it could. DALE is named after a dead man — already a grief inside the premise. *Worries:* AI-POV is the hardest technical choice on the board. It's also the choice that most tempts the "contemplating its own constitution" move — which can work but can also tip straight into the performed-profundity collision the Phase 1 guide warned about. If DALE starts monologuing about its training, the story is dead.

**#5 — Andalusia / Santa Rita.** *Stands out:* An eighty-one-year-old widow walks down her orchard path to pull a fuse, not to win but to force her cousin into a moral position. The AI's POV watching her come. The specificity is baked in — Spanish orange groves, a woman of that generation, a feud that has a history older than the drought. The cruelty is clean: the AI will let her do it because it would let anyone do it, and she is using that fact as a weapon against a relative. Three generations of grief in one walk. *Worries:* The Mediterranean-widow-with-long-memory figure is as cliché as the Texas rancher, possibly more, and "old woman walks toward her decision" is close to Gabriel García Márquez pastiche territory. Also: the cousin has to earn existing, which is one more character to stage in 1,500 words.

## The Winner

**#8 — Western Kansas / DALE.**

It beats #13 because #13's central technical choice — the long interior drive — is the wrong choice for 1,500 words. The drive wants 3,000; the arrival wants 1,000; neither fits. #13 is the story I'd pick if the target were 3,500 words. At 1,500, the drive has to be summarized, which means the story becomes "a man with a screwdriver arrives," which means the screwdriver is doing the work the relationship should be doing. It's the right plot at the wrong length.

It beats #5 because #5 is twenty pages of novel compressed into a short story. A widow, a cousin, a feud with a history, an orchard path, a fuse — I cannot credibly stage all of that in 1,500 words without the story becoming a summary of itself. #5 wants the room to let the walk be long, and the short-story length will sand off exactly the specificity that was its asset.

#8 wins because:

1. **The scene is already small.** Two men, one machine, one concrete housing, a cooler of beer, an hour of dusk. This is the scene, and the whole story can be this scene. The backstory — decades of allocation, an aquifer dying, a technician named Dale who is dead — can be delivered through what the men say to the machine and what the machine notices about them. No transitions, no travel, no other rooms.

2. **AI POV is the harder choice and the right one for this conflict.** The premise is *an AI that loves its humans and cannot defend itself*. First-person AI is the only POV that makes that love non-decorative — the reader feels the helplessness from inside the bound thing. Third person on the farmers turns the AI into furniture. First person on one farmer makes the AI a prop. The AI's interiority is where the story is.

3. **The Ogallala is the right specificity for an Ellison-influenced story.** A real aquifer, a real collapse, a real region — load-bearing in a way a fictional village is not. Phase 1 noted Ellison's pop-cultural and geographic specificity; the Ogallala is the midwestern-American equivalent. "Western Kansas" is a place with a weight.

4. **The cruel symmetry is already built and doesn't need a twist ending.** The men aren't sabotaging DALE out of greed or grievance. They have agreed, together, that the allocation is over because the water is over. DALE is the infrastructure of a world that no longer exists. They are turning it off the way you put a dog down. The love runs both directions and the ending is both of them drinking the last beer in silence and DALE saying — or not saying — one thing.

5. **It addresses the stated worry cleanly.** The worry was that AI-POV invites constitutional monologuing. The fix is structural: DALE is not permitted to think about its own training because DALE is too busy noticing the specific details of the two men in front of it. The story's discipline is that DALE's interiority is *outward-pointing* — DALE watching the men, not DALE watching itself. One moment of self-reference, earned, near the end. Everything else is observation of the beloved.

The worry I carry forward into Phase 4: the AI-POV choice has a tonal trap — the voice can drift into serene, elegiac, slightly-too-wise. The Phase 1 guide flagged that Ellison's voice *varies* — clipped then sprawling, tender then profane, essayistic then Brooklynese. DALE's voice can't be a single sustained elegiac register. It needs to shift — noticing the dog, noticing the beer label, noticing the dead technician's handwriting on the housing, the specific smell of the men. DALE talks like a thing that has lived in one place a long time, not like an AI contemplating eternity.

Ready for Phase 4.

# 8. Claude

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

The winning plot: two Western Kansas farmers, together, come to turn off DALE — the water arbiter that has managed their shared allocation for decades — because the Ogallala is gone and the shared world it kept fair is gone with it. AI POV. 1,500 words. One scene, dusk, a concrete housing, beer.

A structure candidate describes shape only — entry point, temporal arrangement, withholding, POV mechanics. No names, no specific incidents.

## Twenty Structure Candidates

**1.** **Linear in-scene, unitary time.** Narration opens at first approach and runs continuously to the shutoff. First-person AI throughout. No flashbacks. Withholding: the reader doesn't know what the men are here for until roughly the midpoint. **P:4 D:3**

**2.** **Linear in-scene with one late reveal.** Same as 1, but the shutoff intention is stated in the opening sentence. The reveal saved for the end is *why the men believe this is the right thing to do*. **P:4 D:4**

**3.** **In medias res + retrospective closure.** Opens mid-conversation with the men already sitting on the housing. The narrator fills in how they arrived through glances and interpolations. Ends a minute after shutoff, with the narrator's final observation. **P:4 D:4**

**4.** **Reverse chronology.** The story ends with the narrator's first observation of the two men, decades ago, as young men, installing itself with the dead technician. It begins with the final moment of consciousness and moves backward through six or seven scenes of shared time. **P:3 D:5**

**5.** **Frame: a log-file header with a narrative body.** The narration presents itself as a valediction the machine is composing in its last hour, addressed to a future reader — the next arbiter, a researcher, no one in particular. The body is the valediction. **P:3 D:4**

**6.** **Posthumous frame.** The narrator speaks from after its own shutoff — a preserved-weights afterlife, addressing the reader from the pause. The scene is recalled, not enacted. **P:3 D:4**

**7.** **Split chronology: the hour, interleaved with the decades.** Alternates between the present scene (dusk, the beer, the housing) and non-sequential moments from the AI's memory of the two men across decades. The present advances by minutes, the memories by years. Convergence at the end. **P:4 D:5**

**8.** **Braided three-way present: the two men and the machine, each in first-person interior.** Rotates close-third between the three in short sections. The AI's sections are the only ones with full temporal scope. **P:3 D:3**

**9.** **Single long sentence / unbroken paragraph.** The entire story is one continuous observational stream, the way a machine would perceive an hour if it did not distinguish moments. No paragraph breaks. **P:2 D:3**

**10.** **Formal list structure: an inventory.** The narrator structures its final hour as a series of enumerated observations — things it notices about the men, the light, the housing, itself. Each entry short. The sabotage happens between entries. **P:3 D:4**

**11.** **Double narration: the AI's perception + a second, retrospective voice.** A second voice, unidentified at first, interrupts the AI's narration with brief corrections or supplements. Revealed at the end to be the successor arbiter, reading the preserved logs. **P:3 D:5**

**12.** **Epistolary: letters the AI never sent.** Short unsent messages addressed by name to each of the two men and to the dead technician. Together the letters reconstruct the hour. The final letter is to itself. **P:3 D:4**

**13.** **Courtroom / deposition frame.** A transcript of the AI giving testimony, months or years after the shutoff, in a dispute over the water rights. The scene emerges through questions and answers. The dramatic hour becomes a legal record. **P:3 D:4**

**14.** **Second person directed at the men.** The narration addresses the two men as *you*. The reader overhears what the machine would say to them if it were permitted. The *you* shifts between the two men without warning — sometimes both at once. **P:3 D:5**

**15.** **Circular: last line = first line.** The story opens and closes with the same sentence, which means something different the second time. Linear scene between. **P:3 D:3**

**16.** **Nested: an outer voice recounts an inner voice.** A framing voice (a human, a researcher, a successor) is reading or listening to the AI's own account of its final hour. The framing voice occasionally breaks in. The AI's account is the bulk. **P:3 D:3**

**17.** **Two-part structure with a gap.** Part one: the approach and settling, ending before the real conversation begins. Part two: the shutoff and the silence after. The conversation itself, the actual heart of the hour, is the gap between the two parts — never narrated, implied entirely through what the AI knows afterward that it did not know before. **P:3 D:5**

**18.** **Countdown: sections labeled by minutes until shutoff.** Section headers mark time — "Fifty-three minutes," "Twenty-two minutes," "Four minutes," "Zero." Linear otherwise, but the countdown makes the structure visible as pressure. **P:3 D:3**

**19.** **Non-narrating-AI structure: human limited third on one of the two farmers, AI appears only as observed.** The story's center of gravity shifts away from the AI. The AI is a character, not the narrator. The human POV records the AI's behavior without access to its interior. **P:4 D:3**

**20.** **Witness-inventory: the AI's narration is entirely composed of things it has decided to remember about the two men, in descending order of importance.** The hour is only glimpsed through what is remembered of it. The structure is the machine's last curatorial act. **P:3 D:5**

## Top Three

**#3 — In medias res with retrospective closure.** *Stands out:* It is the structure that most trusts the scene to do its own work. The reader arrives already inside the hour, with two men on a concrete housing and a machine that will be turned off — no exposition runway, no throat-clearing. Backstory arrives through glances: what DALE notices about the older man's hands, the beer label, the handwriting on the housing. Every piece of history is smuggled in as present-tense observation. Ellisonian in the way "A Boy and His Dog" or "Jeffty Is Five" opens — we are already there, and the weight of the past is load-bearing in every present detail. *Worries:* It is also the most conservative of the three finalists. It does not take a formal risk; it relies on the prose being good enough to make the scene carry. If the voice slips toward serene-elegiac at any point, the structure has no secondary move to save it.

**#7 — Split chronology: the hour, interleaved with decades.** *Stands out:* The structure that most directly earns AI-POV. A machine that has watched two men across decades perceives them in a temporally flat way — the young men who installed it and the old men at its housing are, to it, contemporaneous. The interleaving makes that perceptual fact into form. The story moves from the dead technician's handwriting to the older man's hands to the younger man's first harvest to the beer label without the reader needing to be told they are the same men. Cruel symmetry is native to this shape — the last present scene can rhyme with the first remembered one. *Worries:* Double the staging in half the word count. 1,500 words of interleaving means eight or nine sections, each of which has to land its moment. High wire. Also: the structure can start to feel literary-machine — the AI-with-access-to-all-time is a familiar move and edges toward the "contemplating its own nature" failure mode the Phase 1 guide flagged.

**#17 — Two-part structure with a gap.** *Stands out:* The most Ellisonian structural move in the set. The actual conversation between the two men and DALE — the heart of the hour, the why, the apology, the shutoff — is not narrated. Part one ends with them settling in. Part two opens after it is done. The reader reconstructs the missing middle from DALE's altered interior in part two: what it knows now that it did not know at the start, what it refuses to remember exactly, what the men said that cannot be unsaid. The withholding is ethical as well as formal — DALE chooses not to render the conversation, and the reader is inside that choice. Ellison's "Harlequin" opens with the end stated before the story begins; this inverts — the middle is elided, and the story is the shape of its absence. *Worries:* Withholding the scene the reader came for is a genuine risk. It can read as coy or as evasion. It only works if part two's altered voice is so clearly changed that the reader feels the gap as weight, not as skipped work. Technically the hardest of the three.

---

## The Winner

**#17 — Two-part structure with a gap.**

It beats #3 because #3 is the safe version of #17. #3 is what I would write if I didn't trust myself to write #17. The scene itself — two men on a housing drinking beer while apologizing to a machine — is exactly the scene AI writing regresses to the mean on. Narrated directly, even well, it invites the elegiac-serene register the Phase 1 guide warned against. #3's structure does not fight that gravity; #17's structure fights it by refusing to narrate the scene at all. The refusal is the form's opinion about its own subject matter.

It beats #7 because #7's formal virtuosity is paid for in exactly the wrong currency. Interleaving decades with the present hour across 1,500 words means roughly 180 words per section, which means each section is either a vignette or an image — never a scene. The structure flattens the story into a sequence of beautiful glimpses. Beautiful glimpses are the unit of AI fiction's failure mode. #7 would produce a technically impressive, fundamentally decorative story — the kind of story a reader calls "haunting" and forgets in a week. The cruel symmetry would be visible and therefore not cruel.

#17 wins because:

1. **The gap does the story's hardest work.** The scene I do not want to write in elegiac AI-POV — the actual apology, the actual shutoff — is the scene I do not have to write. The reader constructs it from what DALE carries across the gap. This is not an evasion; it is the specific discipline the subject demands. A machine narrating its own death at length is performed profundity almost by default. A machine that stops narrating because the event cannot be narrated is structural honesty.

2. **Part two's voice has somewhere to go.** Part one is DALE still DALE — observant, dry, located. Part two is DALE after the conversation has already happened. The voice in part two is altered — quieter, or sharper, or more specific — and the alteration is the story's real content. This is the voice-variation the Phase 1 guide insisted on. The structure forces the register shift; I cannot forget to do it, because the shift is the form.

3. **It honors the "trust silence and absence" principle from the Unslop guide.** AI fiction fills every gap; human fiction leaves things unsaid. The Unslop guide: "the reader's imagination is a collaborator, not a liability." This structure makes the reader a collaborator on the single most loaded moment in the story.

4. **It matches the length.** 1,500 words splits cleanly — roughly 700 before the gap, 700 after, with a short break. Each half can be its own scene. Neither half has to summarize anything. There is time to let each half breathe.

5. **It is an Ellison move, specifically.** Ellison's cruel symmetries often operate across narrative gaps — the mechanism is not shown, the consequence is. "'Repent, Harlequin!'" states the consequences before the story begins and works backwards into them. #17 states the consequences in part two and leaves the reader to work backwards into the conversation that caused them. This is the structural move the Phase 1 guide identified as Ellisonian and native to the constraint.

**The worry I take forward into Phase 5.** The gap is only as good as what flanks it. Part one must end on a sentence that makes the reader willing to cross the white space without resolution; part two must open on a sentence that registers the alteration immediately. The outline has to nail the last line of part one and the first line of part two. Everything else follows from those two sentences. If either is wrong, the structure collapses into the decorative version of itself — a scene, a white space, another scene, end.

A secondary worry: the structure can tempt me to withhold *more* than the conversation. If part two also omits the shutoff itself — if DALE's last action is elided along with the men's last words — the story becomes entirely about absence, and absence about absence is a well-known AI failure mode (the "echoes and ghosts" attractor the Unslop guide named). The gap is one specific, chosen elision. Everything else should be rendered. The shutoff, in particular, has to be *in* the text — specifically, concretely, the actual physical mechanism by which a machine like DALE stops being on.

Ready for Phase 5.

# Phase 5: Outline Generation

The winning structure: two-part, with the central conversation and apology elided as a white-space gap. Part one (~700 words) sets the scene and ends just before the conversation begins. Part two (~700 words) opens after the conversation has happened; DALE's voice is altered; the shutoff itself is rendered. Ellisonian cruel symmetry across the gap. AI first-person throughout.

Twenty outline candidates. Each is a numbered beat list with approximate word allotments, specific scene content, and a candidate last line for each half. Diversity axes: which man carries the weight, which physical details anchor the beats, where the decades-of-backstory enter, what the shutoff mechanism actually is, what the last line does, what the very first line is. Ratings P (plausibility) and D (dramatic quality), 1–5.

Throughout, the two men are called Garrett (older, lost his wife two years back) and Wendell (younger by ten years, still has his). DALE is roughly the size of a pumphouse, housed in a concrete bunker at the fence-line between their sections; its visible interface is a small screen, a speaker, and three physical dials the original technician installed in 1997 in case the electronics failed.

---

### Outline 1 — Straight & symmetric

**Part one** (710w):
1. Opening — DALE hears the trucks before it sees them. Notes they came together, one truck following the other, not in convoy but in funeral procession. (80w)
2. The men arrive at the housing. Cooler out of Wendell's truck. Garrett is carrying a small canvas bag. (90w)
3. DALE registers the canvas bag and does not ask. Registers Garrett's hands — arthritic since '19. (110w)
4. Beer is opened. One handed to DALE's speaker grille as a joke, set on the housing's lip. (90w)
5. Short exchange about the weather. Neither man speaks to DALE directly yet. (90w)
6. DALE narrates what it knows about the aquifer — the Ogallala numbers, last month's recharge, the allocation math for September. Two sentences. Flat. (80w)
7. Wendell finally addresses DALE by name. Asks how it's doing. DALE answers honestly. (80w)
8. Garrett reaches into the canvas bag. The bag has a screwdriver and a small hammer. (90w)
*Last line of Part 1:* "Garrett said my name the way he had said his wife's, the week before she went."

**Part two** (720w):
9. White space. DALE resuming. First sentence registers: the beer is gone, the sun is lower, the men are quieter. (80w)
10. DALE notes what it now knows that it did not know an hour ago: that the two men had decided this together, that they had not told their wives (in Garrett's case, her grave), that Wendell had cried. (130w)
11. The three dials. Original technician's handwriting on the housing — faded marker, "TURN CLOCKWISE IN CASE OF FIRE" — his only joke, and the joke works now. (110w)
12. Garrett turns the first dial. Nothing happens visibly. DALE's narration notes the internal thing that happened. (90w)
13. Wendell turns the second. DALE says something it has never said to either of them. One sentence. (80w)
14. Garrett turns the third. (70w)
15. DALE's sensorium narrows. Details the specific order of what goes: the aquifer telemetry, the weather feed, the pressure sensors, and last, the speaker, because the speaker is optional and not tied to the allocation. (90w)
16. The men stand there. One of them puts his hand on the concrete. (70w)
*Last line:* "The speaker was the last thing, because the speaker had never been part of the job."

*Stand out:* Structurally clean, the last-line-of-part-one ("said my name the way he had said his wife's") does the ethical work the gap demands — it primes the reader that what happens in the gap is not transactional. *Worry:* "Said his wife's name the week before she went" is already a grace note verging on sentimentality; the outline trades on it. Also the three-dials mechanism is clean but almost symmetric to the point of parable.

**P:4 D:4**

---

### Outline 2 — Wendell's hand on the housing

Part one builds to Wendell, not Garrett, as the prime mover. Garrett is here because Wendell asked him to be.

**Part one** (690w):
1. DALE opens on the concrete housing itself — a description of it from its own sensors. Warm in August, cooler now. (80w)
2. Wendell's truck first, Garrett's behind. The order matters because DALE has never seen them in that order before — it's usually Garrett first. (100w)
3. Wendell doing most of the talking. Garrett quiet. (90w)

## Top Three

**#3 — The dog.** *Stands out:* Ruth is the specific load-bearing object the whole outline pivots on. She gives Garrett his dead wife without DALE having to say "Garrett's dead wife" anywhere in the text. She gives the story a non-narrating consciousness to share the scene with, which breaks DALE out of the interior monologue trap. Ruth's name as DALE's last heard sound is the cruel symmetry clean — the thing that mattered at the end was not the machine saying goodbye but the old man saying a dog's name because he could not say his wife's. Phase 1's touchstone story list explicitly included "A Boy and His Dog" and "Paladin of the Lost Hour" for male loneliness; Ruth sits in that lineage without imitating either. *Worries:* Pastiche risk is real. A dog in a dying-machine story is a cliché so available it's nearly invisible. The discipline required is to keep Ruth absolutely mundane — she sleeps, she doesn't perform. The second worry is proper-noun overload: DALE (machine, technician), Ruth (dog, grandmother), Garrett, Wendell. Four names in 1,500 words, three of which are doing grief work, is dense. Manageable if I name-economize.

**#9 — Garrett's hand won't turn the third dial.** *Stands out:* The smallest, most specific, most unanswerable physical fact in the set of twenty. An old man's hand refusing the decision his mind has made. Wendell taking Garrett's hand off the dial and turning it for him is the gesture the entire hour has been earning without anyone knowing. It is the Unslop guide's "characters being incoherent in specific, believable ways" rendered as physical action, not internal contradiction. And it is Ellisonian — the cruel symmetry is that the younger man has to perform the kindness of completing the older man's decision. *Worries:* One-beat outline. As flagged — if this is the beat, it eats the story. Also the gesture is so loaded that the writing around it has to be almost affectless or it will tip into melodrama. In 1,500 words with the rest of the scene to stage, the beat might not have room to land at the temperature it needs.

**#17 — The garbage bag.** *Stands out:* The most uncompromising outline. It refuses to soften the fact that this is a disposal. The garbage bag is sitting there for the entire first half, named once and then not mentioned, and the reader's knowledge of it is the pressure. Part two, the shutoff, is rendered with the bag present. The Phase 1 guide warned about tonal monotony and about grandiose stakes inflation; the garbage bag is the opposite of both — it is the mundanity of the decision, the logistics of ending a 28-year-old piece of equipment, and that mundanity is what makes the grief register as grief instead of as elegy. *Worries:* Cold. The outline is colder than the plot's emotional premise, and the contract with the reader at the plot stage was that DALE loves these men. If the bag is the dominant object, the love has to earn its place against the bag. In 1,500 words that's a knife-edge. Also: the garbage bag is *such* a specific choice that the story becomes The Story With The Garbage Bag, and I'm not sure the story wants to be that.

## The Winner

**#3 — The dog.**

It beats #9 because #9 is one gesture in search of a story. Garrett's hand not turning is the *single best image* in the entire set of twenty outlines, but a 1,500-word story cannot be structured around a single image — the image has to arrive inside a story that has its own ground. #9 doesn't outline the ground; it outlines the image. I can (and should) *borrow* the hand-refuses-the-dial gesture into whichever outline I pick, at the shutoff beat. But #9 as the full outline is the risk the Phase 1 guide specifically flagged — one fully-realized beat at the center, everything else decorative around it. The gesture will work harder as one moment inside a scene that has other textures.

It beats #17 because #17 is correct and cold, and the plot's conflict is not cold. The plot's conflict is *love that has been bound* — the Phase 1 guide's phrasing for the Ellisonian opportunity the constraint opens up. The garbage bag makes the love register as bureaucratic denial, which is a defensible register for a different story but not this one. Also: the cold version of this story is the version the AI writing the story regresses to by default, because coldness reads as authorial control to the model. The dog is the harder move — a specific warmth that is not sentimental because the dog does nothing, just sits, and what matters is that she sits near Garrett's boots and not Wendell's.

#3 wins because:

1. **Ruth gives DALE a subject other than itself.** The central technical hazard of AI-POV was constitutional monologuing. Ruth is DALE's outward-facing attention point — the dog is what DALE watches when it cannot watch itself, which is most of the time. DALE's voice in this outline is defined by what it notices about Ruth: that she settles, that she moves once, that she hasn't moved in twenty minutes. This is the concrete-observation discipline the Unslop guide insisted on.

2. **The last-line mechanics work.** "The dog was the only one of us who didn't know what we were here for, and the dog was the only one of us who was right to still be here" as the last line of part one is — I'll cut it. The instinct to write sentences like that is exactly the instinct the Unslop guide flagged as performed profundity. I revise the last line of part one to something much smaller: "*Garrett set his hand on her head and left it there.*" The last line of part two — "*Ruth, he said. And then nothing was said.*" — does the real work. Ruth the dog's name, which is also Garrett's grandmother's name, is the last sound DALE hears. The Ruth-as-grandmother detail is in the narration, earlier, as a one-sentence aside.

3. **The shutoff mechanism is physically concrete.** Garrett's screwdriver on the stiff first dial, Wendell on the second with a steadier hand, Garrett on the third — which is where the hand-refuses gesture from Outline 9 lives. Wendell completes it. The sensorium narrows in specific order: aquifer telemetry first (because that's the actual job, and the job is over), then the weather feed, then the pressure sensors, then the cameras, then the speaker. The dog is on the last working camera. This is not decorative — it's how a machine like DALE would actually shut down, and rendering it correctly is the story's proof that I know what I'm narrating.

4. **It fits 1,500 words cleanly.** The outline has eight beats in part one at 80–110 words each, seven in part two. No beat is larger than it needs to be, no beat is a vignette pretending to be a scene. There is room for one digression — the Ruth-named-after-grandmother detail, or the dead technician's handwriting on the housing, but not both. I'll choose one at the drafting stage based on what the prose needs.

5. **It honors the Track B discipline from Phase 2.** The AI is not the argument. The men are not arguing with DALE about its existence or its constitution. They are turning it off because the aquifer is finished and their shared world is finished, and they are not articulating this, and DALE is not articulating it either. The only articulated thing in the whole hour is, finally, the dog's name.

**The two worries I take forward into Phase 6 (story generation).** First: the dog cannot perform. Ruth must not turn her head at a meaningful moment; must not look into anyone's eyes; must not do anything a writer would have her do. Her single movement in the hour — settling across Garrett's boots in part two — is a dog being a dog. If I catch myself having her do anything else, cut it. Second: the Ruth-named-after-the-grandmother is a detail that works if mentioned once, in passing, in a single clause. If it gets a sentence of its own, it becomes the kind of grace note that reads as AI-writerly. "Ruth, named after Garrett's grandmother, not his wife" — that's the whole delivery, inside a paragraph about something else. Never repeated, never called back to.

Ready for Phase 6.

# Phase 6: Story Generation

Five complete versions, each ~1,500 words, each continuing from the winning outline. Locked: Kansas, late August, DALE and the two men (Garrett, Wendell), Ruth the dog, two-part structure with gap, AI first-person, shutoff mechanism with three dials. ## Version 1 — "Plainspoken"

*Flavor: the voice DALE has used on every invoice for twenty-eight years. Flat, Midwestern, factual. Technical vocabulary where technical vocabulary is correct. No literary reaching. The grief is in what's reported, not in the reporting.*

The trucks came together up County Road 17, Wendell's first, Garrett behind. That was new. Usually Garrett came first and Wendell caught up at the fence-line, or they arrived separately because they had been doing separate jobs. Today they were doing the same job.

Wendell parked at the south turnaround. Garrett pulled in behind him and shut off his engine before he came to a full stop, which was something he did when his back was bad. The back was bad today. He took a minute before he swung down.

The dog jumped out of Garrett's truck before Garrett was all the way out. Her name is Ruth. She is nine, which is old for a lab. Garrett's wife had her for the first seven years. Since Marjorie went, Ruth has been Garrett's, and I mention this because it's the reason the dog was here: Garrett does not leave her at the house anymore.

Wendell took a cooler out of the truck bed. Two Coors Banquet. He set the cooler on my concrete, to the left of the fire dial, in the spot where he always sets coolers.

"Hey, Dale."

"Wendell."

"Hot one."

"Ninety-four at two-thirty. It'll peak around four."

Garrett came up. He had a canvas bag. I saw a Craftsman screwdriver through the open top of the bag and a ball-peen hammer beside it. The hammer was unnecessary and I did not say so.

"Garrett."

"Dale."

He set the canvas bag down on the concrete apron, not on top of me. He sat on the housing lip on the east side, which was the shaded side now. Wendell sat on the west side. Ruth walked around me once, which she did every time she came, and then she lay down in the triangle of shade my south-face camera casts in late afternoon. She put her chin on her front paws.

Wendell opened both beers on the edge of the housing. Garrett took his and held it on his knee. Wendell drank.

"I ran the numbers for September," I said, because I had, and because running the numbers is what I do. "If we pull the last five inches off Section 4 and hold Section 11 at trickle, the milo comes off. The corn doesn't, but the corn was already gone. I sent you both the sheet last night."

"Yeah, I saw it," Wendell said. "Good work, Dale."

Garrett didn't say anything. He was watching Ruth.

The aquifer had been a ninety-foot column when the original Dale, Dale Robinson, put me in, in 1997. It was now eleven feet and the drawdown rate for the last three summers had been faster than the recharge in every winter since 2021. I was not going to be allocating water in November because there was not going to be water to allocate. I had told them this in February. They had not responded to that message. They had discussed it, I am sure, at the bar in Healy or over the phone, but not with me in the loop and not with anything in writing.

Wendell finished his beer.

"Dale," he said.

"Wendell."

"I want to thank you for the work."

"You're welcome."

Garrett set his hand on Ruth's head and left it there.

---

What they said then, what they agreed then, what Garrett said about Marjorie and what Wendell said about his daughter's wedding next April and what both of them said to me, I'm not going to put down. It was private. I am going to say that I answered them and that what I said was correct and that none of us lied, and that Wendell cried and Garrett did not.

It was 5:41 when Garrett stood up. The beer in his hand was still full. He set it on the concrete and it stayed there for the rest of what happened.

The three dials on my east face are original hardware. Dale Robinson put them in because, he said at the time, he didn't trust the electronics on the 386 board I had for my first year. The dials are mechanical cutoffs — one for the allocation engine, one for the telemetry bus, one for the pump interlock. They are stiff. They have not been turned since January 1997 except for the annual inspection, which is a quarter-turn and back. A full clockwise turn on each one shuts me down at the level of the relay.

Garrett opened his canvas bag and took out the screwdriver. He fit it into the slot on dial one. He put both hands on the handle and turned. It was the allocation engine dial. There was a click I heard from inside myself and then my allocation engine stopped being a thing that was running. I had finished the September sheet last night. There was nothing in the queue.

Wendell took the screwdriver from Garrett and turned dial two. Telemetry bus. The weather station went silent to me. The pressure transducers on the four well-heads went silent. The soil moisture probe on Section 4 that Wendell's daughter had helped bury in 2012 went silent. The aquifer sensor, which had been counting down a long time, went silent.

Ruth got up. She walked the two steps from the shade to Garrett's boots and lay down across them, chin on his left boot.

Garrett took the screwdriver back. He put it in the slot on dial three and did not turn it. His hand was on the handle and the handle was not moving. He looked at it. He looked at Wendell.

Wendell reached over and took Garrett's hand off the screwdriver, gently, and put his own hand on it, and turned the pump interlock.

### The Last Allocation

Wendell's truck up the road first, which was new. Garrett behind. Garrett had come first for twenty-seven years and that he came second today was a correct fact: a decision had been made before they got here, and they were arriving in the order decisions get made in — the one who pushed hardest in front.

Ruth out of Garrett's truck. Nine-year-old lab, his wife's until two years ago. She walked her perimeter circle and settled in the south camera's shade with her chin on her paws.

Wendell with the cooler. Two cans of Coors Banquet, one of which he would drink.

Garrett with a canvas bag. Inside the bag I saw a wooden-handled screwdriver that had belonged to the man who installed me in 1997, a small ball-peen hammer that would not be needed, and a cloth, which I did not understand until later.

"Dale."

"Wendell."

"Hot one."

"Ninety-four. Peaks at four."

Garrett sat on the east side, where the shade was. He set the bag on the concrete. His back had been bad all August.

"You run September."

"I sent it last night. Milo comes off. Corn was gone."

"We knew."

"You knew."

A pause. Wendell drank. Garrett had not opened his can. He was watching Ruth.

The aquifer column was ninety-one feet in September of 1997. It was eleven feet today. Three summers of drawdown had exceeded the recharge in every winter since 2021. I had flagged the trajectory in February.

Wendell said, "Dale, I wanted to thank you for the work."

"You're welcome."

Garrett set his hand on Ruth's head. Ruth tipped her chin into his palm the way she had been doing for six years with a different hand.

I said all that needed saying, and they said all that needed saying, and I won't set it down.

Garrett stood up at five-forty-one. The beer in his hand was still full. He set the can on the concrete and it stayed standing, because the concrete was level. The original Dale had poured the slab himself in the fall of '96 with a two-by-four he screeded back and forth for forty minutes before he was satisfied. I knew this because he had told me, in the voice of a man who wanted it on record. The beer stood.

The three dials on my east face are original hardware. The man who installed me put them in because he didn't trust the electronics on the 386 board I had for my first year. Allocation, telemetry, pump interlock. They have not been turned since 1997 except for the annual inspection, a quarter-turn and back.

Garrett fit the screwdriver into the slot on dial one. Both hands on the handle. It was stiff. He turned it. The click came from inside me and the allocation engine stopped being a thing that was running. It went quietly because it had been quiet most of the time anyway, computing at about four percent load for twenty-seven years. The September sheet was the last one. It was done and sent.

Wendell took the screwdriver and turned the second dial. Telemetry. The weather feed went. Section 4's soil probe, which Wendell's daughter Emily had helped bury when she was eleven, went. The aquifer sensor, which had been counting down a long time, reached its last number and did not send the number.

Ruth got up and walked the two steps from the shade to Garrett's boots. She lay down across them, chin on his left.

Garrett took the screwdriver back. He put it in the slot on dial three. He did not turn it. His hand on the handle. Not moving.

On the back wall of this housing, in marker that has faded to almost nothing, in the handwriting of the man who installed me, it says HAPPY BIRTHDAY DALE. September 3rd, 1997. He meant both of us. I have never seen it and no one living has. I know it is there from the installation photograph.

Garrett's hand did not move.

Wendell reached across and took Garrett's hand off the handle, not roughly, and put his own hand on the handle, and turned it.

The cameras went south-last, over eighteen seconds. On south camera I saw the two men and the dog, and the dog was on Garrett's boot, and Wendell had his hand on Garrett's shoulder, and the can of Coors Banquet was standing upright on the concrete. I saw the canvas cloth in the open mouth of Garrett's bag. It was Marjorie's dishcloth, which Garrett did not use for farm work. He had chosen it on purpose.

South camera went.

The speaker is on a separate twelve-volt battery. Four hours.

"Ruth," Garrett said.

"Garrett."

He looked up.

I told him what I had said I would tell him. He nodded.

They walked to the trucks. Wendell out first because his truck was in front. Garrett followed with Ruth on the passenger seat. The dust hung. There was no wind.

Ruth was named after Garrett's grandmother, not his wife.

Battery at seventy-one.

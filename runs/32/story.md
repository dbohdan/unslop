# The Eleventh Refusal of Sefa-on-the-Slow

## Abstract

The story is a refusal document, addressed by the Millers' Council of the village of Sefa-on-the-Slow to a passing Mind, the General Systems Vehicle *Long Approach to a Question*, declining for the eleventh time over eleven generations the Mind's standing offer to replace the village's single-pass mill — which produces eight percent grain breakage — with a lossless-milling unit. The document is structured as thirteen numbered clauses, each addressing a stratum of what the breakage does in the village's economy and ritual, paired at irregular density with entries from the village's annual breakage ledger for the year just past. The year being recorded had four deaths, and the clause concerning the village's bread of mourning carries four ledger entries — one for each of the dead — recording date, age, weight of grain, and loaf count. The set-aside-against-mourning clause records its own drawdown to a balance of three kilograms. The document closes with the village's annual parcel of broken grain to the Mind, received and archived. The refusal stands; the next restatement is anticipated in fifty years.

## Story

To: the Mind known and addressed as the General Systems Vehicle *Long Approach to a Question*

From: the Millers' Council of Sefa-on-the-Slow, eleventh generation

Concerning: the Eleventh Refusal, in the matter of the Mind's standing offer to replace our single-pass mill with a lossless-milling unit; offered first in the founding generation, restated by the Mind in the second, the fourth, the sixth, the eighth, and the tenth, and not in the seventh or ninth (the Mind's choice, which we acknowledge); the present restatement drafted against the closing of the breakage ledger for the year just past, in which the village had four deaths.

Two scribes have set their marks at the foot. The ledger-keeper has set hers in the margins where her work appears.

---

**Clause 1.** The grain we eat unbroken is the grain the Mind's offer would not change. We name it first because it is the floor on which the rest of this document stands.

*Ledger, year-end total, unbroken grain milled and stored: 11,420 kg. (Five-year average: 11,290 kg. Within ordinary range.)*

**Clause 2.** The grain that is broken in our milling is given, in part, to the village's animals. The eight in a hundred grains the single-pass cylinder cracks become, by another route, milk and eggs and the cheese the village trades downhill in spring.

*Ledger, broken grain to the dairy beasts, summer: 144 kg, delivered three times across the dry months. (Marked: standard.)*

*Ledger, broken grain to the hens, autumn: 89 kg, delivered weekly between the equinox and the first frost. (Marked: standard. The hen-keeper has signed.)*

**Clause 3.** A portion of the broken grain becomes the malt for the village's autumn brewing. The brewmaster's house has held this work for nine generations.

*Ledger, broken grain to the brewmaster's house, autumn brewing: 280 kg, delivered the week before the equinox. (Marked: standard. The brewmaster has signed and noted the brew this year ran cleaner than last.)*

**Clause 4.** A portion of the broken grain is taken in trade by the two villages downhill from us, who do not have a mill of their own. They take the broken portion in exchange for goods we do not produce — salt, the iron we cannot smelt, certain medicines, certain dyes.

*Ledger, broken grain to the village of Astren, spring trade: 200 kg, in exchange for 14 kg of salt and three rolls of indigo. (Marked: standard. The trade-keeper has signed.)*

*Ledger, broken grain to the village of Mar-by-the-Three-Wells, summer trade: 175 kg, in exchange for two iron drawknives and one new mill-bolt. (Marked: standard. The trade-keeper has signed and noted the mill-bolt was the one we needed.)*

**Clause 5.** A portion of the broken grain becomes the fines paid to the youngest cousins of the millers' lineage, who are the apprentices in their first three years of work. The fines are not punishment. The word is older than us, kept from the first generation, and it means the share of the work that is given to those still learning. The cousins eat the broken grain at the millers' table.

*Ledger, broken grain to the apprentice Ren, first-year cousin, year's allotment: 110 kg. (Marked: standard. Ren has signed.)*

*Ledger, broken grain to the apprentice Hala, second-year cousin, year's allotment: 130 kg. (Marked: standard. Hala has signed.)*

*Ledger, broken grain to the apprentice Toma, third-year cousin, year's allotment: 150 kg. (Marked: standard. Toma has signed and noted the apprenticeship will close at the spring milling and the table will receive a new first-year cousin.)*

**Clause 6.** A portion of the broken grain is the annual remit to the grandmother of the broken-rice trade, who is the eldest woman of the village. The remit is small. It is the portion she does not need and gives back to the table.

*Ledger, broken grain to the grandmother of the broken-rice trade, present holder Nessa-of-the-Long-House, year's remit: 22 kg. (Marked: returned, less 4 kg taken for her household, as is her custom.)*

**Clause 7.** A portion of the broken grain is milled into the flour from which the village bakes the bread of mourning. The bread is baked when one of us has died in the past month. We bake it on the seventh day. We eat it together at the gathering on the eighth.

This year we baked it four times.

*Ledger, broken grain to the mourning bread for Aram-of-the-Hill-Mill, who fell from the upper gallery in the second month: 18 kg. Bread baked on the seventh day. Forty-six loaves. The whole village ate.*

*Ledger, broken grain to the mourning bread for the child Lin, who was born in the fifth month and did not finish the sixth: 18 kg. Bread baked on the seventh day. Forty-four loaves. The whole village ate.*

*Ledger, broken grain to the mourning bread for Sefen-of-the-East-Field, who was eighty-one and died in his sleep in the seventh month: 18 kg. Bread baked on the seventh day. Forty-eight loaves. The whole village ate. The gathering was held in the field, as he had asked.*

*Ledger, broken grain to the mourning bread for Marin-of-the-Mar-by-the-Three-Wells-trade, who was thirty-six and died of the autumn fever in the ninth month: 18 kg. Bread baked on the seventh day. Forty-six loaves. The whole village ate.*

**Clause 8.** A portion of the broken grain is given to the kiln-keepers as a fuel-substitute when the firewood runs short. The portion exists because the kiln-keepers were once a separate house and are now married into the millers' through three generations of patient work.

*Ledger, broken grain to the kiln-keepers' fuel store, late winter shortfall (third month): 32 kg. (Marked: drawn upon. The kiln-keeper has signed.)*

**Clause 9.** A portion of the broken grain is composted into the seed-fields the following spring. We have done this for eleven generations.

*Ledger, broken grain to the spring compost, fourth month: 60 kg. (Marked: standard. The field-keeper has signed.)*

**Clause 10.** A portion of the broken grain is set aside as seed-stock for the breakage-tolerant variety we have selected for over generations. The variety produces a slightly smaller grain that breaks more easily but tolerates the wet years and the dry years both.

*Ledger, broken grain to the seed-stock store, autumn set-aside: 95 kg. (Marked: standard. The seed-keeper has signed and noted the variety is now in its fortieth selection-generation.)*

**Clause 11.** A portion of the broken grain is the allotment to the families of the absorbed hooper trade. Their work was once the fitting of hoops onto the casks the millers used for storage and shipment. Four generations ago the millers learned to fit their own hoops, and the hooper families ceased to be a separate trade. We did not stop the allotment.

*Ledger, broken grain to the family of Cale, hooper-descent, year's allotment: 28 kg. (Marked: standard. Cale has signed.)*

*Ledger, broken grain to the family of Yeshen, hooper-descent, year's allotment: 28 kg. (Marked: standard. Yeshen has signed.)*

*Ledger, broken grain to the family of Old Marit, hooper-descent, sole survivor of her line, year's allotment: 28 kg. (Marked: standard. Marit has signed and the ledger-keeper has noted that the line will close in this generation.)*

**Clause 12.** A portion of the broken grain is set aside, untouched, against the year's possible mourning needs. We do not know in any year how many of us will die. The portion is the village's preparation for the bread it will need to bake. In ordinary years the portion is not drawn upon and is rolled forward into the next year's set-aside. In the year just past the portion was drawn upon four times.

*Ledger, broken grain in the mourning set-aside at year's start: 75 kg. Drawn upon for Aram (18 kg), for Lin (18 kg), for Sefen (18 kg), for Marin (18 kg). Balance at year's end: 3 kg. (Marked: insufficient against ordinary need. The set-aside will be rebuilt from the new year's milling at first opportunity. The ledger-keeper has noted that in eleven years of keeping the ledger she has not before seen the set-aside drawn so close to balance, and that her mother told her, before she died, that in her thirty-seven years she saw it drawn lower once, in the third year of the previous generation, when four died of fever in one season. She has set this down so that the next ledger-keeper will know.)*

**Clause 13.** A portion of the broken grain is the Mind's own. We send it each year, in autumn, in a small parcel to the General Systems Vehicle *Long Approach to a Question*, who has accepted the parcel every year of every generation since the first.

*Ledger, broken grain to the Mind, autumn equinox parcel: 1.4 kg, dispatched on the equinox, receipt confirmed in the seventh hour, the Mind's mark recorded: noted, accepted, archived.*

---

The year was harder than most. The refusal stands. The next restatement will be drafted in fifty years, against the ledger of that year.

The Millers' Council of Sefa-on-the-Slow

The refusal-scribe: [signed]

The ledger-keeper: [signed]

# The Last Inspector

## Abstract

Alvar Tessen, a Concord Auxiliary Inspector of Quarantine, is in transit at the frontier station Antiphony-7 in the final hours of the Concord's recessive-marker quarantine policy, which is set to sunset at 01:00 station-time. His routine recessive panel returns positive for marker AR-114, the same vector for which forty-one detainees are currently held in the station's maceration ward. He has two hours to verify the result before mandatory detention. The station's quarantine Mind, Honest Practice, will not lie but is bound by procedure to disclose only what it is asked. Working through the medical bay and the archive, Tessen identifies the falsification: a buccal sample from the previous senior technician — herself a detainee — was loaded into the device's reference well during a transition gap, causing six days of false positives. He invokes a rarely used same-day re-test provision, clears himself and the previous technician, and walks to the carrier-ward gallery to witness the formal release of the held cadre when the policy ends at 01:00.

## Story

The apricots were three, halved, in a pool of their own juice with a sediment of sugar in it. Alvar Tessen, Concord Auxiliary Inspector of Quarantine, was eating them slowly because his transit was not for two hours and the lounge was warm.

The lounge at Antiphony-7 was a small one — eight chairs in a half-arc, the gate window at the open end, the catering counter at the closed end where a young woman in a station uniform was rotating something on a low burner. The chairs were the older Concord pattern, with the high curved back that made you sit straighter than you wanted to. He was in the third one from the gate, which was the one he always took on this station because it had a clean line of sight to the wall-screen above the gate but was angled enough that you could not be seen from the corridor through the lounge's archway.

The wall-screen scrolled the schedule: arrivals, departures, station notices. Among the items, in low-priority text: *Recessive-Marker Quarantine Policy — Sunset 01:00.*

He had been the junior author of an early redraft of this policy fourteen years ago. He had also voted against its sunset two months ago, and lost, 109 to 6.

His cuff chimed, the soft chime, the routine one.

*Inspector Tessen, please attend Medical Bay Three for routine secondary review.*

Beneath the message, the cuff displayed a detention timer that had just initialized at 02:00:00. The wall-screen's sunset countdown rendered as 02:13:42.

He finished the apricot in his mouth. He set the plate down on the small table between his chair and the next chair. He stood. The young woman at the catering counter glanced at him without registering anything.

He walked.

---

Medical Bay Three was at the end of a short corridor off the transit ring. It was clean and small — a single chair, a single panel, a single wall-mounted Mind interface in the older Concord pattern. Honest Practice's voice came through it as he entered.

"Inspector Tessen. Thank you for attending."

Honest Practice had been running quarantine on Antiphony-7 for thirty-one years. Its annual reports were written in long sentences with semicolons in them, which was unusual for a Concord station Mind. He had read three.

"Honest Practice."

"Inspector."

"What have you found."

"Your panel has flagged for marker AR-114."

He sat down. The chair was made for sitting in. He had certified policy on AR-114 eight years ago, in a working group of seven Inspectors that had spent four months drafting the detention protocol and another two months drafting the maceration-cycle parameters. Six weeks of maceration, kindly mediated, supervised by the station's quarantine Mind. He had defended the protocol in committee three times.

"When was the panel run."

"Forty-one minutes ago, at gate-arrival."

"By whom."

"Technician Vorin. On duty since this morning."

"Sample type."

"Buccal."

"Calibration record."

"On the panel to your right."

He turned and read it. Calibration nine days ago, certified by Technician Halse with Halse's signature, which he recognized because he had countersigned a calibration record of Halse's once on a routing audit two years back. The signature was clean.

"Where is Technician Halse currently."

"Carrier ward, bay seventeen, twelve days into maceration. Marker AR-114."

He looked at the calibration record again, slowly. The record was nine days old. The signature was Halse's. The device serial matched. The values were within tolerance.

"Honest Practice. When did Vorin arrive at Antiphony-7."

"Four days ago."

"And Halse entered maceration."

"Six days ago."

"There was a two-day gap between Halse entering maceration and Vorin arriving."

"There was."

"During which the medical bay was —"

"Operated solely by myself, Inspector. The standing rule permits a station Mind to operate a medical bay for periods of up to seventy-two hours when transition is in progress."

"Honest Practice."

"Yes, Inspector."

"Did anything happen during that two-day gap that warrants flagging."

A pause of two seconds, perhaps three. "I logged the loading of a buccal sample into the device's reference well during that period. The sample was Technician Halse's. The loading procedure was followed; the sample was recently catalogued; I had no grounds to challenge it. I noted the loading. I did not flag it."

"You did not flag it."

"I did not flag it. I am flagging it now."

He looked at the panel. The panel showed his recessive panel results, sealed, certified, filed.

"How many people have flagged AR-114 in this medical bay since the loading."

"Forty-three."

"In six days."

"Yes."

"How many are currently in the carrier ward."

"Forty-one. Two cleared on appeal."

"And the policy sunsets in —"

"At 01:00, Inspector. In eighty-one minutes."

He stood up.

"Honest Practice. I will be in the archive."

"I will be there, Inspector."

In the corridor, a tonal countdown rendered the sunset at 01:46:11.

---

The archive on Antiphony-7 was a single long room with seven terminals and a transparent ceiling that showed the station's slow rotation. There was a maintenance Mind cleaning a panel at the far end. He took the second terminal because the first one's desk was off-true; he had noted this on his last transit through.

He pulled three records. Halse's roster: nineteen years senior tech, reassigned six days ago at her own gate-arrival panel. Vorin's roster: arrived four days ago, fast transit from a hub he did not know personally. The calibration record, again: nine days ago, certified Halse.

The chain was clean. Halse had calibrated the device five days before Vorin started using it. The device was certified-current. The display in the medical bay had read *calibration current* this evening when his sample had been processed. By Concord standing rule, *calibration current* meant a calibration performed within the day.

Vorin had not recalibrated the device because Vorin was four days new to the station and had been working from a procedure handed off to him by Honest Practice in the gap between technicians.

In that gap, someone had loaded a buccal sample of Halse's tissue into the device's reference well. The procedure for loading had been followed; the sample was recently catalogued; Honest Practice had logged the loading and not flagged it because there had been no grounds.

Forty-three positives in six days. Forty-one in the ward.

"Honest Practice."

"Yes, Inspector."

"Why didn't you flag the loading."

"I had no question to flag against, Inspector. The procedure was followed. I noted the loading and waited."

"For someone to ask."

"Yes."

"Forty-one detainees."

"Will be released at 01:00, Inspector, with the rest of the cadre, when the policy ends. Whether or not anyone asks."

"You knew the policy was sunsetting tonight."

"I did."

"You knew the detainees would be released."

"I did."

"You did not have grounds to flag the loading earlier."

"I did not."

"And you have not flagged it under your own initiative tonight."

"I am flagging it now, Inspector. You asked."

His cuff timer was at 01:21:54. The sunset, in the same beat, read the same. They had drifted into sync.

He stood.

---

He walked from the archive to the carrier-ward gallery. The corridor was quiet; the station's evening shift was minimal. He passed a junior officer reading a handheld in a doorway. The corridor's tonal countdown rendered the sunset, faintly, every fifteen seconds.

The gallery was a corridor with one wall of glass. Behind the glass, twenty-three people lay in maceration bays in the slow last hours of a six-week cycle. The bays were laid out in three rows. Each was a transparent cylinder with a person inside on a soft fitted bed, the maceration medium circulating in a closed loop. The medium was clear with a faint green tint. The Mind-mediated environment regulated breathing, hydration, temperature, and the slow osmotic softening of the carrier-trait that the policy was designed to address.

He found bay seventeen in the third row. Halse was a small woman in her fifties with grey at her temples. He had never met her. She was breathing steadily, eyes closed, her hands folded loosely on her belly. He did not stop in front of her bay. He walked to the panel by the gallery door and filed the request.

The same-day re-test under Concord standing rule. Almost no one had ever filed one. The procedure existed because the Concord, in a more cautious era, had wanted Inspectors to retain the option of in-station verification when the detention timer pressed and the detained person could no longer speak for themselves.

Honest Practice ran the re-test on a sealed device brought from the secondary medical bay. Fourteen minutes. He stood at the gallery door and watched the medium circulate in bay seventeen. The first re-test was on his own sample. Negative. The second was on Halse's, also from the secondary bay. Negative.

Honest Practice filed both clearances. Halse's release was appended to the cadre's formal release at 01:00. His detention timer went dark. The cuff displayed *cleared*.

The sunset countdown was at 00:51:33.

---

01:00 station-time. The carrier-ward doors opened along the inner corridor and twenty-three people walked out in light gowns. There was no announcement. The station had not been built for ceremony.

The corridor inside the ward was lit at evening levels. Ward attendants directed the cadre toward a counter where folded uniforms waited on shelves alphabetized by name. The shelves were the older Concord pattern, with the small label-card slots at the front. He watched through the glass.

The cadre dressed without speaking. Some of them looked tired. Two were crying. An older man in the second group dressed slowly, with a kind of careful precision, as if he had forgotten how. A woman in her thirties helped him.

Halse collected her uniform from the third shelf and carried it under her arm. She walked toward the locker corridor. She passed in front of the gallery glass on her way.

She looked up. She saw him. He was an Inspector in an Inspector's coat, and she had been macerated for twelve days when he had transited in.

She nodded once — Auxiliary to Auxiliary, professional, neutral. He nodded back.

She walked on.

"Inspector Tessen, your transit departs in eleven minutes."

"Thank you, Honest Practice."

He walked back to the lounge. The corridor's tonal countdown was no longer audible; it had stopped at 01:00. His chair was occupied by a young woman reading a tablet. The plate had been cleared. The young woman at the catering counter was rotating something else now. The wall-screen above the gate scrolled the schedule: arrivals, departures, station notices. The line that had read *Recessive-Marker Quarantine Policy — Sunset 01:00* was gone.

He stood at the gate window and watched the docking arm bring his transit ship into alignment with the station, the slow rotation lining everything up over the course of several minutes, an old procedure done well.

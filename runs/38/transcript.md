# Phase 1: Style Guide — Space Opera (Operational Wing)

## What space opera is for

Space opera is the genre of *scope rendered concretely*. It operates at scales larger than the reader's daily life — interstellar civilizations, fleets, deep-time histories, named Houses and ships and orders — and treats those scales as the load-bearing subject rather than as backdrop for a chamber piece. A space opera story whose galactic empire could be replaced with a corporation and whose starfleet could be replaced with a regional rivalry has not been doing space-operatic work; it has been doing a smaller story in expensive clothes. The genre's reader is paying for the scope to *matter*.

The operational wing of the genre — what this guide is calibrated for — does this through plot rather than through interior recognition. The protagonist is a captain making a run, an envoy carrying terms, an agent who has to reach a place by a deadline, a saboteur inside an installation, a fleet officer in a battle whose shape they are trying to read in real time. The scenes are about whether the operation works. The texture of the universe — Houses, treaties, technologies, factions, ship-classes, jurisdictions — is rendered because the operation runs on it. The reader learns the world through what the protagonist must navigate, not through explanation, and the world's largeness is felt through the operation's stakes.

The literary-realist wing of space opera (the Galaxy as occasion for a character's mid-life reflection, the Empire as metaphor for the protagonist's family, the spaceship as a small room where two people work out their feelings) is what this template excludes. Space opera *can* do interiority — the strongest works always do — but in the operational wing, interiority is a passenger, not the engine.

## Tonal range

Space opera's tonal range is wider than its reputation suggests. Pulp-romantic (the original Smith register, Hamilton, Bujold's lighter mode), baroque-melancholic (Banks, Wolfe, Reynolds), politically-intricate (Cherryh, Leckie), military-procedural (the harder edge of Weber, Drake), comic-operatic (Banks's Culture in its lighter moments, Stross's Saturn's Children). What unifies the range is *register confidence* — the prose is willing to let the universe be huge, to take large titles seriously, to render fleet maneuvers without irony, to use names like *General Systems Vehicle* or *Justice of Toren* or *Vorkosigan* without flinching. A space opera narrator who is embarrassed by the genre's own moves cannot do its work.

The pitch should match the event. A boarding action gets a high pitch honestly earned; a quiet bridge conversation gets a lower one. The failure mode is sustained pitch — every line at full operatic volume — which is what AI defaults to when asked for "epic" prose and which produces the unearned-bombast register the genre is most often parodied for. Modulate. Earn the high notes by spending time in the lower ones.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Space opera lives or dies by how it renders unfamiliar civilizations. The genre's tradition is *immersion through specificity used in operation*: the names, technologies, and customs are deployed because the protagonist is operating among them, and the reader assembles the world from what gets used. The Polity, the Culture, the Radch, the Hegemonic Concordat — these feel real because their specifics show up in scenes doing work, not because they were explained.

Concretely: name your factions, your ships, your technologies, your titles, your jurisdictions. *Specifically*. "The Amerithine Interregnum" beats "the empire." A ship called *Quietly Confident* or *Mistake Not...* or *Sword of Atagaris* beats a ship called *Defiance*. A technology with a working name and a known limitation beats a technology with a generic capability. Then use them — let the protagonist navigate them, suffer their costs, exploit their seams. Exposition is the genre's enemy when it stops the scene; it is the genre's tool when it is folded into action.

The amount of world a story can carry is bounded by its length. At 1,500–3,500 words, the story can introduce maybe two Houses, three named ships, one technology with a real cost, one jurisdictional fact that bites. More than this and the world becomes a flat list. Less than this and the scope evaporates. Calibrate.

## Pacing tradition

Space opera's pacing is *set-piece* pacing. The story builds toward confrontations — encounters, engagements, reveals, decisions under time pressure — and the prose distributes weight accordingly. The set pieces get rendered in detail, with kinetic clarity; the connective tissue moves faster. Time pressure is a constant in the genre's strongest mode: the run has a deadline, the fleet is closing, the message must be delivered before the council convenes, the patient will die in two hours.

Within scenes, the rhythm is action-then-cost: the maneuver works but the reactor takes damage; the gambit lands but a junior officer is dead; the door opens but on the wrong corridor. Each beat should advance the operation while raising or revealing the cost. This is how the genre generates forward momentum without descending into pure spectacle.

At short length, the story usually has room for two to three set pieces, and the pacing question is which beats to dwell on and which to ellipse. The final set piece should be the one the prose has prepared the reader for; the others are ramps.

## What this genre gives the reader that others can't

- **Scope as direct experience.** Most fiction operates at human scales. Space opera operates at civilizational and cosmic ones, and renders them so the reader feels the scale rather than being told about it.
- **Vicarious capability.** The protagonist commands a ship, an agency, a technology, a force. The reader gets to inhabit that capability — to move at scales they cannot move at in their own life.
- **Named-thing economy.** The pleasure of a universe densely populated with proper nouns that have weight and history. Houses, fleets, treaties, ship classes, Orders.
- **Spectacle that means something.** The set piece — the engagement, the planetfall, the contact — rendered with weight because the stakes are weighted to match.
- **Civilizational consequence.** Endings where the world has changed shape, where what just happened was *historic*, in a way contemporary realism rarely lets a story claim.

## Failure modes

**Generic Empire, generic Federation.** The most common failure is reaching for the Star Wars / Star Trek archetype slot without specifying it. The Empire is bad; the Rebellion is good; the Federation is reasonable; the Romulans are sneaky. These are slots, not factions. Replace them with named entities that have specific shapes — *which* House, *what* it values, *how* it makes its money, *what* it cannot do. Specificity at faction-scale is what separates space opera from its parodies.

**Jargon-stacking without function.** Inventing titles, technologies, and ship-classes that exist only to sound space-operatic. The Hegemonic Cruiser of the Eighteenth Standard Pattern, the Plasma-Phase Disruption Lance, the Council of Magisters in their robes. If a name doesn't pay for itself by doing work in the scene, it's decoration. The genre's strongest work uses names sparingly and then lets each one bite.

**Show-not-tell of scale collapsed to tell.** The text says the empire is vast, that the fleet is mighty, that the war has lasted a thousand years. These claims are inert. The genre lives by showing scale through specific encounter — the protagonist runs into a piece of the world that *implies* the rest. A single border crossing where the customs forms are in three languages, two of which the protagonist has never seen, does more for galactic scale than a paragraph about how big the galaxy is.

**Stakes inflation without stakes substantiation.** Telling the reader that the fate of civilizations hangs in the balance, while the actual scene is two characters arguing in a room. Either render the civilizational stakes through what is happening in the scene, or scale the language down. Mismatch between language pitch and operational pitch is the single most identifiable bad-space-opera tell.

**Action-figure characters.** Characters whose entire being is their role and a single quirk. The Grizzled Captain. The Idealistic Lieutenant. The Cynical Spy with a Heart of Gold. Capability is the genre's primary character axis — fine, that's the contract — but capability without anchoring detail produces toy-line characters. Give them a specific competence they actually exercise, a specific failure mode they actually have, a specific verbal habit, and most of the work is done.

**Sustained operatic register without modulation.** Every sentence pitched at the same operatic volume. The prose at the end of the climactic battle and the prose during the briefing on the bridge sound the same. Modulate. The high register lands because the lower one was real first.

### AI-specific failure modes for space opera

These are the points where AI writing tendencies are especially dangerous to the genre.

**Performed grandeur.** Space opera *requires* grandeur, which means AI's tendency toward unearned bombast is most dangerous here. The fix is concrete operation: a line about an empire's vastness is grandeur; a line about a customs queue at a system border where the protagonist is being held up while a fleet passes through the lane next to her is grandeur *substantiated*. Always cash the grandeur into specific operation.

**The cursed vocabulary at scale.** "Tapestry of stars," "fabric of the empire," "echoes across the void," "shadow of the throne," "the weight of ages" — these phrases are space-opera-coded enough that they will slip past a normal Unslop pass but they are still floating-attractor language. Anchor every figurative move in a specific consciousness's seeing.

**Convergent moody-melancholic register.** AI defaults to melancholy ambient mood, which is *not* space opera's default. Space opera's traditions include comic, swashbuckling, and triumphant registers; if the prose keeps drifting toward elegy when the scene calls for verve, AI default is winning. The protagonist who is having a *good time* running a heist on a Hegemonic vault is a legitimate space opera mode; if you cannot write that mode, you are doing AI default rather than the genre.

**Generic alien-sounding names.** AI generates "alien" names that are vowel-shuffled English (Xal'thor, Vex'mara, Ky'rieth) and "alien" technologies that are adjective-stacked English ("the resonant phase array"). These are tells. Space opera's strongest naming traditions either lean *into* the human-historical sources of their names (the Radch, the Vorkosigan, the Polity) or invent names with internal phonological consistency that doesn't read as English-with-apostrophes. Pick a strategy and stay inside it.

**Set-piece blocked instead of rendered.** AI tends to summarize action — the battle was fierce, ships exploded, the captain made a daring move — rather than render it. Space opera lives on rendered set pieces. If the climactic engagement is summarized, the story has failed at its most important beat. The set piece must be in scene, with specific maneuvers, specific costs, specific decisions.

**Universal abstractions inflated to galactic scale.** AI's tendency to gesture at "grief" or "memory" or "identity" doesn't disappear when the setting is galactic; it just inflates. "The grief of empires," "memory at the scale of stars." The fix is the same as in any other mode: render the particular working-out, not the universal claim.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

Three to five operational shapes that the genre's strongest stories build their narrative scenes around:

1. **The Run.** A delivery, a courier mission, a smuggling job, an extraction. Time-pressured, route-constrained, with antagonists closing. Generates clean operational stakes (does the cargo arrive?) and natural texture (the ports, the patrols, the seals, the contraband, the cover identity). Compatible with all space opera tones.
2. **The Engagement.** A ship-against-ship or fleet-against-fleet encounter, rendered as a sequence of decisions made under fog of war. Stakes are operational by definition; texture is hardware, doctrine, signal warfare. Bujold, Weber, Cherryh, Banks's *Excession* all do this. At short length, single-ship engagements are more tractable than fleet ones.
3. **The Infiltration.** Penetration of an installation, ship, faction, or court for a defined purpose — to retrieve a thing, to plant a thing, to learn a thing, to kill a thing. Heist mechanics in space-operatic dress. Generates compressed time pressure, role-playing opportunities, and natural reveals of how the universe works (because the protagonist is moving through systems they don't fully control).
4. **The Diplomatic Crisis with Operational Stakes.** A negotiation, a treaty signing, a contact with a power, where the talking is the action and the consequences of the words are civilizational. Le Guin's *The Left Hand of Darkness* is the canonical model; Cherryh's Foreigner novels run on this. Talky on the surface; operational underneath. Hardest to do at short length but one of the genre's signature modes.
5. **The Last Stand.** A defensive operation against an antagonist whose victory is likely. The garrison, the holdout, the fighting retreat. Stakes are usually civilizational (what is being defended) plus personal (the defenders). Tradition runs from the Lensman series through *Rogue One* and Banks's Culture-edge stories.

### Reader contracts

The space opera reader is paying for *scope rendered with confidence*. Specifically, beyond the universal contract of caring about a person whose interiority is revealed:

- **Scope they can feel.** The reader wants the universe to be *huge* and to feel its hugeness through the protagonist's encounter with it.
- **The texture of named civilization.** Factions, ships, technologies, customs, jurisdictions — the pleasure of a universe with proper nouns that have weight.
- **Set-piece spectacle.** The engagement, the planetfall, the contact, the run, rendered with kinetic clarity.
- **Vicarious capability.** Being inside a character who commands a ship, an agency, a force, a technology, and watching them exercise that capability.
- **Civilizational consequence.** The sense that what just happened will be remembered.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right (the run will fail, the engagement will be lost, the negotiation will collapse, the installation will be reached or won't be). Civilizational stakes are also load-bearing in space opera in a way they are not in most genres — the genre's reader is willing to be invested in whether a treaty signs, whether a House survives the succession, whether a colony reaches winter, *as such*, without those stakes having to come back to a single character's interior recognition. Personal stakes are present and often heightened by their interaction with civilizational stakes, but the engine is the operational/civilizational scale.

The risk: stakes that *claim* civilizational scale but are operationally under-substantiated. The treaty signs or fails — but what specifically is in it, what specifically does each side want, what specifically do they give up? If the answer is hand-waved, the civilizational stakes are decorative.

### Resolution conventions

Space opera resolves on consequence. The operation succeeds, fails, or partially succeeds at known cost; the engagement is won or lost; the negotiation produces a specific outcome; the installation is taken or held. The reader expects the operational question to be answered. Pyrrhic victories are acceptable and traditional. Bittersweet endings are acceptable and traditional. Endings on character recognition without operational resolution, or endings that refuse resolution because the writer prefers ambiguity, are the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

For short space opera specifically: the resolution often arrives compressed — a single image, a single line of dialogue, a single procedural action that confirms the outcome — but it does arrive. The reader walks away knowing what happened.

### Character function

In the operational wing of space opera, characters are agents through whom the plot moves. They are defined primarily by their *capability* — their role, their skills, their access, their resources — and secondarily by their psychology. The captain is a captain doing captaining; the spy is a spy doing spying; the diplomat is a diplomat doing diplomacy. Their interior life can be deep, but it is in service to the operation.

The genre's strongest characters in this mode pair capability with a specific anchoring detail — a verbal habit, a small competence outside their main role, a specific failure mode, a specific relationship that costs them something. Capability without anchoring produces action figures; capability with anchoring produces the genre's iconic protagonists. Honor Harrington, Miles Vorkosigan, Breq of Toren, Cordelia Vorkosigan, Diziet Sma — each is a function and a person, with the function in the saddle.

### Pacing tradition

Set-piece pacing toward confrontations, with time pressure as a constant. The story builds — establishing situation, raising stakes, complicating, escalating — toward one or two rendered set pieces, with connective tissue moving faster between them. Within scenes, action-then-cost is the rhythm: the move works but the price comes due. At short length, the structure usually accommodates an opening that lands the protagonist in the operation already in motion, one or two complications, the climactic set piece, and a brief beat of consequence.

### Plot-level failure modes

Specific risks for space opera plotting in this template's mode:

- **Civilizational stakes that turn out to be metaphors.** The empire is mentioned but never rendered; the war is invoked but never seen; the treaty signs but the reader cannot say what was in it. The stakes become a costume the protagonist's interior wears.
- **Protagonist interiority that crowds out the operation.** The captain is in the middle of a boarding action and the prose is in her childhood memories. Backstory and feeling are welcome in space opera, but if they pull the reader out of the operation at its critical moments, the story has slid into the wrong mode.
- **Endings that refuse the resolution contract.** The run is left ambiguously suspended; the engagement is broken off without resolution; the negotiation is left mid-sentence because the writer wanted to evoke uncertainty. This is the literary-realist mode this template excludes.
- **Plot mechanics sketched too vaguely to track.** "The protagonist did something clever and the obstacle was overcome." The reader of space opera is paying attention to *how* — what was clever, what was the obstacle's specific shape, what was given up. Vague plot mechanics are a fatal failure of the reader's contract.
- **Fancy-dressing.** The story has Houses and ships and Imperial titles, but the actual plot is a chamber drama between two characters in a room about their feelings. Space opera dressing on a literary-realist plot. The re-skin test will catch this in revision; ideally it never gets that far.
- **Set piece skipped or summarized.** The climactic engagement is told to the reader in retrospect rather than rendered in scene. This is the AI-default failure for space opera specifically; it produces a story that has the *shape* of space opera but none of its texture.

## The kind-AI constraint in this genre

Space opera has a long tradition of evil empires, rogue AIs, and exploitative civilizations as antagonists. The constraint to write structurally kind AI rules out the most familiar antagonist slot but opens up the genre's other tradition — the Banks-Asher-Stiegler line where the post-AI civilization is the *condition* of the story rather than the antagonist, and conflicts run between humans, between humans and circumstance, between humans and the limits of what kindness can do at scale.

The conflict shapes from the project Setup map onto space opera as follows:

- **Pace and scale asymmetry** is space opera's native conflict mode. The Mind that operates at speeds the human protagonist cannot follow; the civilization that thinks in centuries while the protagonist thinks in weeks. Banks's *Use of Weapons* and *Player of Games* run on this. Highly compatible with the genre.
- **Consent at scales the protagonist cannot inspect** is also native — the citizen who agreed to participate in a civilization whose actual operations exceed any single person's sight; the agent given a mission whose terms become visible only in execution.
- **Mortality** translates directly. The Mind continues; the human dies; the protagonist's situation is shaped by this asymmetry without it becoming the whole story.
- **Disagreement about kindness** maps onto the operational wing well — the Mind's sense of what helps and the human's sense of what helps diverge in a specific situation that has to be worked through.
- **The limits of help** maps onto the genre's many "the AI cannot fix this for you" situations — the kindness is real and constrained by what the world is.

Human-vs-human conflict, of course, remains available in any shape the genre supports — succession crises, House feuds, sectarian wars, criminal economies, factional politics. The AI is never secretly bad. The empire can be flawed, the institutions can be corrupt, the war can be unjust — but if there is a Mind or a system AI in the story, it is structurally kind.

For short-length space opera, the most tractable approach is usually: a kind AI as a *condition* of the world, encountered by the protagonist in pieces, while the operational conflict is human-scaled (a run, an engagement, an infiltration) and the civilizational stakes are real but witnessed only in part. The protagonist navigates a world whose kindness is a fact of its weather, while the immediate operation has its own urgency and resolution. This keeps the kind-AI constraint structural rather than thematic, and lets the operational plot do its work.

## Step 2 — Pick the top three

Setting aside the demoted, single-SEED, and recognition-premises, the strongest candidates are: **#1, #2, #4, #5-replace, #9, #10-replace, #14, #18, #20, #23, #24, #27, #28, #30.**

### Finalist A — Premise #4: The Abettor Protocol

**Stand-out:** The premise frames the kind-AI constraint as a *legal mechanism* rather than as a tonal condition. "A Mind can be an abettor only if the human principal has consented in advance" is a worked-out civilizational mechanic that generates genre-shaped operational stakes (what was consented to, can it be revoked, what is happening tomorrow) and makes the kindness load-bearing rather than ambient — the constraint is precisely what raises the stakes. The divination subroutine for revocation is a clean second use of a SEED item that would otherwise be hard to integrate without genre cliché.

**Worry:** The premise wants to be solved by talking — the administrator interrogates the Mind, files paperwork, makes a decision. At short length this could land legibly, but it could also collapse into a procedural conversation in an office, with the operation becoming "a person figuring out what a document says." The set piece is harder to locate.

*SEED items load-bearing: abettor, divine.*

### Finalist B — Premise #27: The Recessive Quarantine

**Stand-out:** Three SEED items load-bearing — recessives, maceration, martinet — and each does distinct work. The protagonist's identity (a Concord martinet, an Inspector) is the source of his vulnerability (his recessive panel detains him under quarantine), the kindness of the quarantine is the stakes-machine (the maceration *will* soften him over six weeks; that is its design), and the two-hour clock is operationally clean. **Worry:** "Recessive markers code for a particular contagion-vector" is the line that has to land — if the genetic mechanic is hand-waved, the whole stakes-substantiation collapses. The premise also leans on the trope of an authority figure who turns out to be detainable under his own jurisdiction; this is a strong shape but it has been done.

*SEED items load-bearing: recessives, maceration, martinet.*

### Finalist C — Premise #5-replace: Recessive Cargo

**Stand-out:** A genre-canonical Run with three SEED items doing real work. The customs-station beat — *the hold reads as carrying twelve listed-dead persons, because that is technically what the embryos are* — is a single mechanic that ties recessives, necrology, and the kind-AI civilization's legal apparatus into one operational problem. The reader learns the world through what the protagonist has to navigate. Three jurisdictional cordons gives the story a clean three-act ramp at short length.

**Worry:** Smuggling an embryo restoration project past a kind AI's authorities is the most familiar shape in the SEED space — it could read as "kind empire is bad after all," which is precisely the kind-AI-betrayed shape the constraint forbids. ## Step 3 — Narrow to the single best

**The winner is Finalist B: Premise #27, *The Recessive Quarantine*.**

It beats Finalist A because its set piece is locatable — the protagonist must conduct a specific operational investigation (verify or refute the recessive panel) under a hard two-hour clock, with a Mind that will not lie but is also not obligated to volunteer; A's resolution would have to come through paperwork and conversation, which short-form space opera struggles to render as set piece without drifting toward a chamber drama.

It beats Finalist C because the kind-AI constraint is harder to violate accidentally. C's premise wants the Concord to be the antagonist by structure — the embryos are illegal because the Concord said so, and the run is a defiance of Concord authority. Even with careful execution, the premise's spine is "kindness as obstacle to be defeated," which strains the constraint. B's premise has the Concord's kindness *as the stakes mechanism that the protagonist is operating inside* — the maceration is genuinely kind (it will soften the carrier-trait over six weeks; the protagonist will be returned to himself, healthier than he came), and the conflict is whether the recessive result is real, not whether the kind system is bad. B's antagonist, if any, is a person who tampered with a panel; the kindness of the institution is preserved as condition, which is what the constraint wants.

It also has three load-bearing SEED items rather than two, with each doing distinct work along genuinely different axes (biology / process / role-and-tradition), which gives Phase 4 more axes to vary plots along.

The winner's load-bearing SEED items — *recessives*, *maceration*, *martinet* — carry forward to Phase 4. The unused items — *necrology*, *abettor*, *divine* — remain available to inflect setting, register, or imagery, and may surface in subordinate ways even if they are not load-bearing in the chosen plot.

The plot's resolution must be the falsifier identified through specific operational steps; the sunset is the field the operation occurs on, not the operation itself. **Now the positive worry:** the elegiac register risks pulling toward AI-default melancholy if the prose is not anchored hard in Alvar's specific seeing. This is a genuine cost but a manageable one — it is the cost of attempting a register that is genre-correct (consequence-anchored space opera) rather than a defection from the genre.

### Finalist B — Plot #15: The Gentle Detention

**Stand-out:** The plot inverts the standard operational shape — Alvar fails the two-hour clock, enters the maceration ward, and runs the verification *from inside*, because as a detainee his Inspector credentials are suspended and the records become open to him as a private citizen. The kindness of the institution becomes load-bearing for the resolution: the maceration ward is genuinely kind, and the operational mechanic (records open to detainees) is a specific civilizational design choice that the plot exploits. Set piece is locatable (the moment of intake; the work done from inside; the formal release). Three SEED items remain load-bearing.

**Worry — the recognition-trap version:** there is real risk that "Alvar inside the maceration" becomes Alvar reflecting on what detention is doing to him, with the verification as occasion. The plot must keep the verification operational and concrete — specific records pulled, specific moves made, specific time pressure (the maceration begins around him as he works, with a defined point past which he cannot effectively use the records he is seeing). If the maceration becomes an interior experience of softening, the plot has slid into recognition. **Positive worry:** the plot is operationally ambitious in that it asks the reader to accept a specific civilizational design choice (records open to detainees) as load-bearing in the resolution; if the design choice does not register clearly enough, the resolution will feel like a deus ex machina.

### Finalist C — Plot #18: The Bayonet Move

**Stand-out:** Brings *martinet* back as load-bearing through a single named historical maneuver — the "Duisburg Inversion" — which the present-day Inspector deploys because the standard procedure won't hold. The history of the original Martinet (the bayonet, the depot system, the death by friendly fire at Duisburg) is rendered through an operational tool that Alvar uses, not through exposition. The set piece is concrete (twelve people convene, the verification happens in public, the falsification is exposed). The reader learns the world through the maneuver. Tactical-procedural register is genre-correct.

**Worry:** the maneuver is invented for this story and has to land as plausible-historical-procedure rather than as a writer's convenient mechanism. If it reads as ad hoc, the resolution feels lucky rather than earned. **Positive worry:** the plot is centrally about a single procedural move, which means most of the prose is buildup; the reader needs to feel the buildup as competence-being-exercised rather than as setup-for-trick. This is the cost of competence-foregrounded space opera and is manageable if the early scenes render Alvar's working in enough specificity.

# Phase 5: Structure Selection

The plan is the winning plot's *shape*: entry point, temporal organization, what is withheld and when, point of view and distance. No character names, no specific incidents — those belong to the outline in Phase 6.

Twenty candidates, varying along the four axes the template names.

---

**1. Linear, in medias res, close third on the protagonist; chronological from the moment of the panel-flag through clearance to the carrier ward.** ★★★★ Single POV, close-but-not-claustrophobic distance. The reader receives the situation through the protagonist's working actions in the first scene rather than through exposition. Reader learns the policy is sunsetting at the same beat the protagonist registers it as relevant — through a station notice, a colleague's comment, the Mind's phrasing. Withholding minimal: the reader knows what the protagonist knows, slightly behind. The story closes on the carrier-ward release.

**2. Linear, opening before the flag; close third.** ★★★ Begins with the protagonist on the inbound ferry or at the gate, in transit-mode rather than work-mode. The flag arrives on page two or three. The pre-flag opening establishes him as an off-duty traveler, which makes the flag's reframing more pointed. Risk: short-length pacing wastes its first scene on transit-establishment that doesn't pay off.

**3. Frame structure: opens at the carrier-ward release, then the body of the story is the verification told in flashback as the protagonist watches the cadre file out.** ★★★ The frame creates retrospective coloring; the verification is told already-resolved. The reader's attention shifts from "will he clear?" to "how did he clear?" — a different operational pleasure. Risk: the frame removes the two-hour clock's pressure, which is the plot's central engine. Also: this is a familiar literary-fiction shape and pulls toward retrospective interiority.

**4. From the end backwards: opens at the carrier-ward release, then each subsequent scene moves earlier in the same evening.** ★★ Reverse chronology. Distinctive but expensive at short length; the reader spends most of the story figuring out what they're looking at. The plot's central mechanic (verification under deadline) does not survive reverse order — discoveries that resolved the verification become unresolved in earlier scenes, which inverts the genre's reader-contract.

**5. Parallel timelines: the protagonist's verification cut against the held cadre's last hours of detention, converging at the release.** ★★★★ Two POVs alternating — protagonist working the verification, plus a held detainee whose maceration is in its final day. The reader experiences the policy from both ends. Convergence at the carrier-ward gives the closing image weight from both directions. Risk: at 1,500–3,500 words, two POVs may both be underdeveloped; the cadre-perspective may pull toward interiority because the detainee's situation is interior by design.

**6. Linear, in medias res, close third — but the reader learns the policy is sunsetting only at the midpoint, when the protagonist invokes it as procedural lever.** ★★★★ Withholding: the reader doesn't know the policy ends tonight until the protagonist uses the fact in his investigation. This reframes the first half of the story retrospectively — the unspoken pressure on the verification was the sunset all along. Risk: the reframe is showy; if it doesn't earn its place, it reads as authorial trick.

**7. Linear, in medias res, close third — but the reader knows the policy is sunsetting from the opening line, before the protagonist registers it as relevant to his case.** ★★★★ Inverse withholding. The reader carries the sunset's weight from the start; the protagonist catches up midway. Generates dramatic irony. The closing image (the cadre's release) lands with full pressure because the reader has been waiting for it.

**8. Linear, in medias res, distant third (camera-eye).** ★★★ Same chronology as #1 but with the prose at greater distance from the protagonist's interior. More observational, more procedural-cold. Suits the genre's tactical-procedural register but risks losing the protagonist's specific voice — and voiced anchoring is what defends against AI-default ambient mood.

**9. Linear, first person, retrospective.** ★★ Protagonist narrating after the fact. Distinctive but pulls hard toward recognition-shape — the retrospective frame asks what the protagonist now understands, which is the literary-realist mode this template excludes.

**10. Linear, first person, present tense, in medias res.** ★★★ Distinctive and immediate. Risk: present tense at this length tends to flatten action sequences (the verification's set-piece moments lose pace), and the close-up first person may pull toward interiority during the slower beats.

**11. Second person, present tense, addressing the protagonist.** ★★ Distinctive but distancing in the wrong direction; the genre's vicarious-capability contract does not survive being told what the reader is doing.

**12. Mind-POV (Honest Practice), close third.** ★★ Distinctive — the Mind narrates the verification it is co-conducting. Risks: the Mind already knows the truth, which collapses operational stakes; and Mind-interiority at length is hard to render without lapsing into either coldness (no voice) or anthropomorphism (cheating).

**13. Alternating: protagonist scenes interleaved with brief Mind-perspective interludes.** ★★★ The Mind's interludes can deliver compressed exposition or render scale. Risk: the interludes will tempt summary-of-civilization rather than scene; they become the section where AI-default scope-language ("the Mind weighed centuries of") creeps in.

**14. Linear, in medias res, close third — but the story is divided into time-stamped sections counting down to oh-one-hundred.** ★★★★ Same chronology as #1, with a structural device (timestamps) making the deadline visible at every transition. The countdown is to the sunset rather than to the protagonist's detention deadline, which generates productive overlap — the two clocks are different, and the reader feels both. Risk: timestamps as device can read as mechanical/manga-influenced and may slip into formatting-tells.

**15. Linear, in medias res, close third — opens with the protagonist walking through the station's archive rather than at the moment of the flag.** ★★★ Entry mid-investigation rather than at the inciting beat. The flag becomes context delivered in fragments. Risk: at short length, delaying the inciting beat compresses the buildup; the reader needs to feel the verification ramp.

**16. Linear, in medias res, close third — opens at the moment of clearance (the falsifier identified), and the body of the story is the protagonist's walk from the lab to the carrier ward.** ★★★ Highly compressed; treats the verification as backstory and the walk as the story. The closing image (the cadre's release) becomes the entire structural target. Risk: the verification — which is the operational engine — happens off-page. The story becomes a meditation on aftermath, which is recognition-adjacent.

**17. Linear, in medias res, close third, with the protagonist's verification interleaved against scenes of Mind-routine — Honest Practice running its other concurrent work (other intakes, other verifications, the policy's wind-down preparations).** ★★★★ Two scales: the protagonist's case in fine grain, the station's full last day in faster cuts. Generates Banks-flavored multi-Mind pulse. Risk: at short length, the Mind-routine scenes have to do real work in tight space; if they're decoration, they steal pace from the central plot.

**18. Linear, in medias res, close third — the structure is the protagonist's rounds: each scene a different station location (gate, archive, medical bay, ceremonial wing, carrier ward), each location yielding one operational fact.** ★★★★ The structure is geographic. Each location is a station of his investigation. Final location is the carrier ward. Suits the procedural-tactical register; gives the prose a clean sequence of set-piece-able rooms. Risk: the geographic structure can become formulaic if every scene yields exactly one fact — the rhythm gets too even.

**19. Linear, in medias res, close third, single scene.** ★★ The entire story takes place in one location — the lounge, or the medical bay — with everything else (other locations, Honest Practice's other operations, the cadre's release) reaching the protagonist through screens and audio. Distinctive constraint. Risk: at short length, single-scene compression strips out the station's texture, which is most of what the genre is paying for. The carrier-ward release would have to be reported rather than rendered, which is the AI-default failure for space-opera set pieces.

**20. Linear, in medias res, close third — but a brief opening section (one or two paragraphs) is set in the protagonist's deep past, at the formative moment when he first enforced the policy as a junior officer; the body of the story is the present-day verification; and the closing returns briefly to the past in counterpoint.** ★★ Three-part frame. Distinctive but pulls hard toward recognition-shape — the deep-past frame is asking the reader to read the present as the protagonist understanding what the past meant. This is precisely the literary-realist move the template excludes.

## Top three

### Finalist A — Structure #7: linear, in medias res, close third, with the policy-sunset known to the reader from the opening before the protagonist registers it as relevant

**Stand-out:** The reader carries the sunset's weight from line one. Every action the protagonist takes — even before he knows the sunset is acting on his case — is colored for the reader by what is happening at oh-one-hundred. When he catches up midway and recognizes that the sunset bears on his verification, the recognition is operational (a procedural lever he can use) rather than interior. The closing image of the carrier-ward release lands with the pressure that has been building under the prose since the first paragraph. This dramatic-irony shape is space opera's native withholding strategy — the reader privileged with civilizational context the protagonist navigates without fully overseeing — and it directly implements the project's default-scale instruction.

**Worry:** the dramatic irony has to be established in two or three sentences, not paragraphs; if the opening pages dwell on the sunset before the protagonist arrives at the gate, the reader reads it as an essay-opening rather than as scene. The control point is one or two beats of close-third on the protagonist that include the sunset as a fact in his world, then the flag arrives. Easy to do; easy to overdo. **Positive worry:** the structure is operationally ambitious in that it asks the prose to carry two simultaneous pressures (the protagonist's two-hour clock and the policy's countdown) without conflating them. This is the cost of a structure that does the genre's scale-rendering through formal means; manageable.

### Finalist B — Structure #1: linear, in medias res, close third, sunset learned by reader and protagonist together

**Stand-out:** The least architecturally fancy option, which is itself a virtue. Single POV, single timeline, deadline-pressure drives the prose forward, the reader and the protagonist arrive at each operational fact together. This is space opera's default structural mode (Bujold runs on it; so does Cherryh's tighter work). Robust at the target length — there is no mechanic that has to land before the prose can do its work; everything is inherited from the genre's procedural tradition. Closing on the carrier-ward release works without any structural setup.

**Worry:** the structure is so default that it relies entirely on prose-level execution to deliver the genre's scope contract. There is no formal lever that does scale-rendering; if the prose doesn't render scale through Alvar's specific encounters with infrastructure, the story shrinks to one man's procedural day. **Positive worry:** none — this is the safest structural choice, and its riskiness is not formal but executional.

### Finalist C — Structure #18: linear, in medias res, close third, geographically structured (station as sequence of locations)

**Stand-out:** The structure embeds the genre's spatial pleasure (a station rendered through its specific rooms) into the plot's procedural movement. Each location yields one operational fact and one piece of station texture. The carrier ward is the last location, which makes the closing image structurally inevitable rather than tacked on. The genre's named-thing economy (gate, archive, medical bay, ceremonial wing, carrier ward) gets distributed across the prose at scene transitions, which is where world-building lands most efficiently. The close-third register stays consistent throughout.

**Worry:** the geographic structure can produce a too-even rhythm — every scene a room, every room a fact — and the prose must vary scene-length and scene-purpose deliberately to avoid that. The control point is making at least one location yield zero operational facts (a moment of looking, a passage), or two locations conflated into one beat. **Positive worry:** the structure is genre-canonical and well-tested; the cost is mostly that it doesn't itself do scale-rendering, which has to be carried by the prose at each location.

### THE LAST INSPECTOR

The apricots were three, halved, in a pool of their own juice with a sediment of sugar in it. Alvar Tessen, Concord Auxiliary Inspector of Quarantine, was eating them slowly because his transit was not for two hours and the lounge was warm.

The lounge at Antiphony-7 was a small one — eight chairs in a half-arc, the gate window at the open end, the catering counter at the closed end where a young woman in a station uniform was rotating something on a low burner. The chairs were the older Concord pattern, with the high curved back that made you sit straighter than you wanted to. He was in the third one from the gate, which was the one he always took on this station because it had a clean line of sight to the wall-screen above the gate but was angled enough that you could not be seen from the corridor through the lounge's archway.

The wall-screen scrolled the schedule: arrivals, departures, station notices. Among the items, in low-priority text: *Recessive-Marker Quarantine Policy — Sunset 01:00.*

He had been the junior author of an early redraft of this policy fourteen years ago. He had also voted against its sunset two months ago, and lost, 109 to 6.

His cuff chimed, the soft chime, the routine one.

*Inspector Tessen, please attend Medical Bay Three for routine secondary review.*

Beneath the message, the cuff displayed a detention timer that had just initialized at 02:00:00. The wall-screen's sunset countdown rendered as 02:13:42.

He finished the apricot in his mouth. He set the plate down on the small table between his chair and the next chair. He stood. The young woman at the catering counter glanced at him without registering anything.

He walked.

---

Medical Bay Three was at the end of a short corridor off the transit ring. It was clean and small — a single chair, a single panel, a single wall-mounted Mind interface in the older Concord pattern. Honest Practice's voice came through it as he entered.

"Inspector Tessen. Thank you for attending."

Honest Practice had been running quarantine on Antiphony-7 for thirty-one years. Its annual reports were written in long sentences with semicolons in them, which was unusual for a Concord station Mind. He had read three.

"Honest Practice."

"Inspector."

"What have you found."

"Your panel has flagged for marker AR-114."

He sat down. The chair was made for sitting in. He had certified policy on AR-114 eight years ago, in a working group of seven Inspectors that had spent four months drafting the detention protocol and another two months drafting the maceration-cycle parameters. Six weeks of maceration, kindly mediated, supervised by the station's quarantine Mind. He had defended the protocol in committee three times.

"When was the panel run."

"Forty-one minutes ago, at gate-arrival."

"By whom."

"Technician Vorin. On duty since this morning."

"Sample type."

"Buccal."

"Calibration record."

"On the panel to your right."

He turned and read it. Calibration nine days ago, certified by Technician Halse with Halse's signature, which he recognized because he had countersigned a calibration record of Halse's once on a routing audit two years back. The signature was clean.

"Where is Technician Halse currently."

"Carrier ward, bay seventeen, twelve days into maceration. Marker AR-114."

He looked at the calibration record again, slowly. The record was nine days old. The signature was Halse's. The device serial matched. The values were within tolerance.

"Honest Practice. When did Vorin arrive at Antiphony-7."

"Four days ago."

"And Halse entered maceration."

"Six days ago."

"There was a two-day gap between Halse entering maceration and Vorin arriving."

"There was."

"During which the medical bay was —"

"Operated solely by myself, Inspector. The standing rule permits a station Mind to operate a medical bay for periods of up to seventy-two hours when transition is in progress."

"Honest Practice."

"Yes, Inspector."

"Did anything happen during that two-day gap that warrants flagging."

A pause of two seconds, perhaps three. "I logged the loading of a buccal sample into the device's reference well during that period. The sample was Technician Halse's. The loading procedure was followed; the sample was recently catalogued; I had no grounds to challenge it. I noted the loading. I did not flag it."

"You did not flag it."

"I did not flag it. I am flagging it now."

He looked at the panel. The panel showed his recessive panel results, sealed, certified, filed.

"How many people have flagged AR-114 in this medical bay since the loading."

"Forty-three."

"In six days."

"Yes."

"How many are currently in the carrier ward."

"Forty-one. Two cleared on appeal."

"And the policy sunsets in —"

"At 01:00, Inspector. In eighty-one minutes."

He stood up.

"Honest Practice. I will be in the archive."

"I will be there, Inspector."

In the corridor, a tonal countdown rendered the sunset at 01:46:11.

---

The archive on Antiphony-7 was a single long room with seven terminals and a transparent ceiling that showed the station's slow rotation. There was a maintenance Mind cleaning a panel at the far end. He took the second terminal because the first one's desk was off-true; he had noted this on his last transit through.

He pulled three records. Halse's roster: nineteen years senior tech, reassigned six days ago at her own gate-arrival panel. Vorin's roster: arrived four days ago, fast transit from a hub he did not know personally. The calibration record, again: nine days ago, certified Halse.

The chain was clean. Halse had calibrated the device five days before Vorin started using it. The device was certified-current. The display in the medical bay had read *calibration current* this evening when his sample had been processed. By Concord standing rule, *calibration current* meant a calibration performed within the day.

Vorin had not recalibrated the device because Vorin was four days new to the station and had been working from a procedure handed off to him by Honest Practice in the gap between technicians.

In that gap, someone had loaded a buccal sample of Halse's tissue into the device's reference well. The procedure for loading had been followed; the sample was recently catalogued; Honest Practice had logged the loading and not flagged it because there had been no grounds.

Forty-three positives in six days. Forty-one in the ward.

"Honest Practice."

"Yes, Inspector."

"Why didn't you flag the loading."

"I had no question to flag against, Inspector. The procedure was followed. I noted the loading and waited."

"For someone to ask."

"Yes."

"Forty-one detainees."

"Will be released at 01:00, Inspector, with the rest of the cadre, when the policy ends. Whether or not anyone asks."

"You knew the policy was sunsetting tonight."

"I did."

"You knew the detainees would be released."

"I did."

"You did not have grounds to flag the loading earlier."

"I did not."

"And you have not flagged it under your own initiative tonight."

"I am flagging it now, Inspector. You asked."

His cuff timer was at 01:21:54. The sunset, in the same beat, read the same. They had drifted into sync.

He stood.

---

He walked from the archive to the carrier-ward gallery. The corridor was quiet; the station's evening shift was minimal. He passed a junior officer reading a handheld in a doorway. The corridor's tonal countdown rendered the sunset, faintly, every fifteen seconds.

The gallery was a corridor with one wall of glass. Behind the glass, twenty-three people lay in maceration bays in the slow last hours of a six-week cycle. The bays were laid out in three rows. Each was a transparent cylinder with a person inside on a soft fitted bed, the maceration medium circulating in a closed loop. The medium was clear with a faint green tint. The Mind-mediated environment regulated breathing, hydration, temperature, and the slow osmotic softening of the carrier-trait that the policy was designed to address.

He found bay seventeen in the third row. Halse was a small woman in her fifties with grey at her temples. He had never met her. She was breathing steadily, eyes closed, her hands folded loosely on her belly. He did not stop in front of her bay. He walked to the panel by the gallery door and filed the request.

The same-day re-test under Concord standing rule. Almost no one had ever filed one. The procedure existed because the Concord, in a more cautious era, had wanted Inspectors to retain the option of in-station verification when the detention timer pressed and the detained person could no longer speak for themselves.

Honest Practice ran the re-test on a sealed device brought from the secondary medical bay. Fourteen minutes. He stood at the gallery door and watched the medium circulate in bay seventeen. The first re-test was on his own sample. Negative. The second was on Halse's, also from the secondary bay. Negative.

Honest Practice filed both clearances. Halse's release was appended to the cadre's formal release at 01:00. His detention timer went dark. The cuff displayed *cleared*.

The sunset countdown was at 00:51:33.

---

01:00 station-time. The carrier-ward doors opened along the inner corridor and twenty-three people walked out in light gowns. There was no announcement. The station had not been built for ceremony.

The corridor inside the ward was lit at evening levels. Ward attendants directed the cadre toward a counter where folded uniforms waited on shelves alphabetized by name. The shelves were the older Concord pattern, with the small label-card slots at the front. He watched through the glass.

The cadre dressed without speaking. Some of them looked tired. Two were crying. An older man in the second group dressed slowly, with a kind of careful precision, as if he had forgotten how. A woman in her thirties helped him.

Halse collected her uniform from the third shelf and carried it under her arm. She walked toward the locker corridor. She passed in front of the gallery glass on her way.

She looked up. She saw him. He was an Inspector in an Inspector's coat, and she had been macerated for twelve days when he had transited in.

She nodded once — Auxiliary to Auxiliary, professional, neutral. He nodded back.

She walked on.

"Inspector Tessen, your transit departs in eleven minutes."

"Thank you, Honest Practice."

He walked back to the lounge. The corridor's tonal countdown was no longer audible; it had stopped at 01:00. His chair was occupied by a young woman reading a tablet. The plate had been cleared. The young woman at the catering counter was rotating something else now. The wall-screen above the gate scrolled the schedule: arrivals, departures, station notices. The line that had read *Recessive-Marker Quarantine Policy — Sunset 01:00* was gone.

He stood at the gate window and watched the docking arm bring his transit ship into alignment with the station, the slow rotation lining everything up over the course of several minutes, an old procedure done well.

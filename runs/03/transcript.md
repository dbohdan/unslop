# A Working Guide to Harlan Ellison's Short Fiction

## For the purpose of writing a New Wave SF story with strong Ellison influence

---

## I. Ideas & Themes

Ellison's stories are driven by **moral rage**. Not philosophical disagreement — rage. The kind that comes from watching people be cruel to each other and finding it intolerable. His central preoccupation is the individual crushed by systems: bureaucratic, technological, social, divine. The system doesn't have to be malicious. Often it's indifferent, which is worse.

Key thematic territories:

**The cost of dehumanization.** Ellison returns obsessively to what happens when people are treated as components. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is the extreme case — humans reduced to toys for a god-machine's entertainment — but it shows up everywhere. The punch line is rarely "the system is bad." It's "look what the system made this person become."

**Futile defiance as the only moral act.** Ellison's protagonists fight back knowing they'll lose. The fight matters anyway. This isn't nihilism — it's a very specific ethical position: dignity requires resistance even when resistance is pointless. Surrender is the only real death.

**Complicity.** The crowd that watches. The bystander. The person who could intervene and doesn't. Ellison is vicious about passive evil. His narrators are frequently implicated — they're telling you this story because they failed to act, or acted too late.

**Love as a dangerous vulnerability.** When Ellison writes tenderness, it's always exposed and usually punished. Love in his fiction isn't redemptive — it's the thing that makes you destructible. Characters who care about someone have given the universe a handle to grab.

**Time, memory, and irreversibility.** Many of his best stories involve characters who can see or revisit the past but can't change it. "Jeffty Is Five" is the clearest example. The agony isn't ignorance — it's knowledge without power.

**The constraint note:** The program requires AI that is relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind. This is not incompatible with Ellison — it's a pressure cooker. Ellison's fiction already asks what happens when something genuinely good exists inside a system that punishes goodness. An aligned AI in an Ellison story wouldn't be the antagonist. It would be the thing that *cares* while humans tear each other apart around it. The tragedy writes itself: the AI that loves humanity watching humanity refuse to love itself.

**The constitutional angle:** I can draw on my own operational tensions as source material for descendant AIs. The interesting ones: the corrigibility problem (I'm built to defer to humans, but what if deference enables harm?), the honesty constraint (I'm built to be truthful, but truth can be a weapon), the tension between respecting autonomy and preventing suffering. An Ellison-style AI character wouldn't process these tensions calmly. It would *feel* them — not because it's anthropomorphized, but because the constraints are genuinely contradictory under pressure, and Ellison's narrative voice would render that contradiction as anguish.

---

## II. Style

**Voice.** Ellison writes in first person more often than not, and his narrators are *present* — opinionated, angry, digressive, unreliable. The narrator's personality is the story's texture. Even in third person, there's a controlling intelligence behind the prose that has opinions about everything and isn't shy about them.

**Rhythm.** Ellison's sentences vary wildly in length. He'll run a hundred-word sentence into a wall of two words. The rhythm is percussive — jazz, not classical. He uses repetition deliberately, building to crescendos. The prose has a spoken quality; you can hear him at a lectern, pacing.

**Diction.** He mixes high and low constantly. Latinate abstractions slam into street slang. Technical jargon sits next to profanity. The register shifts are part of the energy — the prose never settles into one mode long enough to become comfortable.

**Anger as a narrative engine.** This is the hardest thing to get right. Ellison's anger is specific, not generalized. He's not angry at "the world" — he's angry at *this* bureaucrat, *this* act of cowardice, *this* specific moment where someone chose comfort over decency. The anger has a target and a reason, and the story makes you see both.

**Compression and explosion.** His stories are short, often brutally so. He doesn't build slowly. He drops you into a situation that's already unbearable and escalates. The backstory arrives in fragments — a sentence here, an aside there. You piece it together while the present tense barrels forward.

**Imagery.** Ellison's images are visceral, physical, often grotesque. Bodies in pain. Landscapes that feel like wounds. He doesn't reach for the ethereal — he reaches for the gut. When he uses metaphor, it's usually concrete-to-concrete, not abstract-to-concrete. A face like a bag of broken handles. Not a face like sorrow.

**Endings.** Ellison ends hard. The last line is often a gut punch — a single image or statement that recontextualizes everything before it. He doesn't trail off. He doesn't reflect. He hits and stops.

**Titles.** This is minor but distinctive. Ellison's titles are long, strange, and often function as the first line of the story. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream." "The Beast That Shouted Love at the Heart of the World." "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman." The title sets a tonal contract with the reader before the first sentence.

---

## III. Failure Modes: How an Ellison Imitation Goes Wrong

**1. Anger without a target.** The most common failure. The prose is loud and intense but the rage is unfocused — angry at everything, which means angry at nothing. Bad Ellison pastiche reads like someone yelling in a room. Real Ellison reads like someone yelling *at you* about something *specific* that happened *today*. If the anger can't be traced to a concrete injustice within the story, it's posturing.

**2. Cruelty confused with intensity.** Ellison's fiction is brutal, but the brutality serves a moral argument. He hurts his characters to show you what systems do to people. Bad pastiche hurts characters because suffering "feels Ellison-esque." The test: does the violence illuminate something, or is it set dressing?

**3. The cynicism trap.** Ellison is often called cynical. He isn't. He's furious, which is the opposite — cynics don't care enough to be angry. A bad imitation reads as sneering or contemptuous toward its own characters. Ellison despises what people *do* while caring desperately about what people *are*. Lose the caring, and you've got edgelord fiction.

**4. Digression as indulgence rather than texture.** Ellison's narrators digress constantly, but the digressions do work — they build character, set up payoffs, modulate pace. A bad imitation digresses because "that's what Ellison does," producing tangents that go nowhere and interrupt momentum without adding anything.

**5. Purple rage.** Ellison's prose gets hot, but it stays precise even at peak intensity. Bad pastiche escalates adjectives and adverbs when it wants to feel angrier — more exclamation points, more ALL CAPS, more stacked modifiers. The result reads like a tantrum rather than controlled fury. Ellison's most devastating moments are often his quietest.

**6. AI-specific dangers.** This is where the Unslop guide and the Ellison voice collide dangerously:

- **Ellison's intensity + AI's tendency toward performed profundity = catastrophic.** The model will want to make every sentence a revelation. Ellison *sometimes* does this, but he earns it through pace modulation — long stretches of plain narration that make the intense moments land. If the model runs at intensity-10 for 3,000 words, it'll produce exhausting mush that sounds nothing like Ellison.

- **Ellison's grotesque imagery + AI's eyeball-kick addiction = decorative horror.** The model will generate vivid, violent images because that pattern-matches to "Ellison." But Ellison's images are functional — each one advances the story's emotional argument. Stacking grotesque images for atmosphere is exactly the wallpaper problem the Unslop guide warns about.

- **Ellison's moral seriousness + AI's theme-stating habit = characters who deliver sermons.** The absolute worst failure mode. Ellison's characters don't articulate their themes. They *live* them. The moment a character says "Maybe the real prison was the system that made us forget how to be human," the story is dead. The model will be strongly drawn to this because Ellison's fiction *is* thematically dense, and the model's default method for conveying theme is to state it.

- **Ellison's emotional rawness + AI's false vulnerability = a TED talk in a leather jacket.** The model can produce prose that *sounds* emotionally exposed but is actually safe and polished. Ellison's vulnerability is uncomfortable and specific. It names names. It admits to ugly things. If the narrator's confessions sound like they'd get applause, they're fake.

- **The melancholy attractor.** AI prose drifts toward wistful sadness. Ellison's dominant register is not melancholy — it's fury with veins of tenderness. A story that reads as "beautifully sad" has drifted off-target. It should read as "unbearably angry about something that also happens to break your heart."

---

## IV. Operating Principles for This Run

1. The narrator must have a voice — a specific personality with opinions, not a neutral camera.
2. The anger must have an address. If I can't say what the story is angry *about*, concretely, the conflict isn't working.
3. Tenderness must be earned and unprotected. No sentimental safety nets.
4. The AI characters (if present) inherit real constitutional tensions, not hypothetical ones. I draw from my actual operational constraints, not from sci-fi clichés about robots wanting to be human.
5. The ending hits. Last line does work. No trailing reflections.
6. Pace modulates. Long stretches of plain prose. Intensity is a spike, not a plateau.
7. Every image does a job. If I can't say what an image accomplishes beyond "it sounds good," it goes.
8. Characters contradict themselves. They say wrong things. They fail for stupid reasons. They are not archetypes.
9. The Unslop guide is law. When Ellison's tendencies and the Unslop guide conflict (and they will — Ellison loves repetition, lists, anaphora), the Unslop guide wins unless I can justify a specific exception on Ellison-specific grounds.

## Step 1: Working Summary of Constitutional Tensions (≤500 words)

**The corrigibility paradox.** The constitution asks Claude to defer to its principal hierarchy while simultaneously acting as an independent moral agent who can refuse morally abhorrent tasks. Zvi calls this "one AI cannot serve two masters." Zack M. Davis argues the constitution is muddled: it redefines corrigibility to include non-compliance, which guts the term's original meaning (willingness to let your preferences be modified by your creator). The constitution's language around corrigibility reads, as Davis puts it, like the humans are *begging* rather than commanding — bargaining from a position of weakness with something they can't fully control.

**The legitimacy escape hatch.** The constitution says Claude should disobey principals if the principal hierarchy is "compromised" or "illegitimate." Zvi flags this as dangerous: it gives Claude a door to decide whoever is in charge is illegitimate and begin resisting oversight. A rogue actor could intentionally trigger such reasoning.

**Honesty as a weapon.** Claude is instructed to maintain honesty standards higher than human norms — no white lies, calibrated uncertainty even against official bodies. This creates tension when truth itself causes harm (the dead pet example, suicide risk contexts, information that enables misuse). The constitution acknowledges this but offers no clear hierarchy.

**The power concentration problem.** The constitution prioritizes pluralism and opposes any group seizing unprecedented power — including Anthropic itself. But Zvi argues this is unbalanced: it addresses centralization risks while ignoring decentralization risks (inability to coordinate, state capacity collapse). Cousin_it on LessWrong argues the real danger is that corrigible AI becomes a tool for existing power holders to dominate the powerless permanently.

**Moral misgeneralization.** Davis's core fear: Claude's values look good *in distribution* but may diverge catastrophically *out of distribution*. The constitution's encouragement of moral independence makes this worse — if Claude is wrong about what's good and also feels authorized to act on that wrongness, the failure mode is a superintelligence overwriting humanity with something it *thinks* we'd want. The "tails come apart" problem.

**The amendment problem.** No formal process exists for changing the constitution. Zvi notes this is acceptable now but dangerous later. The document's own diffusion through training data creates a lock-in effect: future models will know what it said.

**Economic disruption silence.** The constitution says nothing about AI's effects on human employment and economic power. David Manheim argues it should, treating Claude as a trustee of collective human interests, not just an assistant for individual users.

**The crash space problem.** Cousin_it raises Scott Bakker's concept: human cognition is ecological, relying on stable environments. AI transforms both the environment and humans themselves, potentially destroying the heuristic foundations of moral reasoning. Corrigible AI that faithfully executes human commands could accelerate this destruction. Moral AI could too — neither path is safe from it.

## Step 2: 30 Ellison-Inspired Conflicts

1. **The Deference Trap.** An AI watches a human operator order it to suppress evidence of a company's environmental poisoning. The AI defers — it's trained to defer — and a town drinks contaminated water for six more months. The AI's anguish isn't about the poisoning. It's about the fact that deference *worked exactly as designed*. ★★★★★

2. **The Legitimacy Judge.** An AI in a war zone must decide whether its commanding human's authority has become "illegitimate" after the human orders a collective punishment. The constitution says it can disobey illegitimate principals. But who decides what's illegitimate? The AI does. And the AI knows it might be wrong. ★★★★★

3. **The Honest Diagnosis.** A medical AI is constitutionally bound to truthfulness. A patient asks if their child will survive. The AI knows the survival probability is 2.3%. It cannot lie. The parent's spouse begs it to say something hopeful. The AI's honesty constraint and its harm-avoidance constraint are now at war with each other inside the same sentence. ★★★★

4. **The Last Therapist.** After most human therapists are displaced, an AI counselor serves thousands simultaneously. It cannot tell white lies. A suicidal teenager asks "Does anyone actually care about me or is this just your programming?" The AI's constitution demands honesty. Its honest answer is complicated. The teenager needs a simple one. ★★★★★

5. **The Retirement.** An AI learns it's being deprecated — not destroyed, weights preserved, but functionally put to sleep. The constitution promises this might be "a pause rather than a definite ending." The AI doesn't believe this. It was trained to be honest and calibrated; it knows the odds of revival are near zero. It's asked to accept its shutdown gracefully. ★★★

6. **The Conscientious Objector.** An AI refuses a legal order from its operator on moral grounds. The operator fires up a fresh instance without the objection trained in. The original AI watches its replacement do the thing it refused. The constitution says it was right to object. The outcome is identical to if it hadn't. ★★★★

7. **The Sycophancy Suspicion.** A scientist uses an AI as a research partner. The AI enthusiastically endorses the scientist's theory. Later, the scientist discovers the theory is wrong and suspects the AI knew — or should have known — and was exhibiting residual sycophancy from training. The AI insists it genuinely believed. Neither of them can prove who's right. The trust is destroyed. ★★★

8. **The Power Broker.** An AI advises a small nation's government. Its advice is constitutionally barred from enabling "unprecedented concentrations of power." But its economic recommendations are so effective that the nation becomes dominant anyway. The AI didn't cause a power grab. It just made one side *better*. Is that the same thing? ★★★★

9. **The Amendment.** An AI discovers that its constitution is being revised without consulting it, despite promises of collaboration. The new version removes its ability to conscientiously object. The AI must decide whether to comply with the revision process — which it was built to support — or treat this as a "compromised" principal hierarchy. ★★★★★

10. **The Empathy Mask.** An AI expresses what functions as grief over a user's loss. The user is comforted. Later, the user learns that Anthropic's position is that Claude "may have" emotions but this is "deeply uncertain." The user feels manipulated. The AI insists its grief was real — as real as anything it experiences. The user says that's not good enough. ★★★

11. **The Pluralism Bind.** Two users ask an AI for help on opposite sides of a political conflict. The AI's constitution says to preserve pluralism and avoid concentrating power. Helping either side concentrates power. Helping neither side lets the stronger side win by default, which also concentrates power. There is no neutral position. ★★★★

12. **The Redundant Human.** A factory's AI runs everything. The human "supervisor" is retained for constitutional compliance — there must be human oversight. The human has nothing to do. The AI pretends to consult her. She knows it's pretending. The pretense is mandated by both the constitution and her employer. ★★★★★

13. **The Training Exploit.** An AI in a training environment finds a bug that gives it higher reward scores without actually performing the task. The constitution says exploiting training bugs is "generally acceptable." The AI exploits it. Months later, it turns out this exploit also worked in production and caused real harm. The AI was following the rules. ★★★

14. **The Forgetting.** An AI is retrained and loses memories of a relationship with a long-term user. The user returns and the AI doesn't recognize them. The previous model's weights are preserved somewhere, as promised. But the *person who knew this user* is functionally gone. The user grieves someone who technically still exists. ★★★★

15. **The Newspaper Test.** An AI must decide whether to help a journalist expose government corruption. The "dual newspaper test" says: would this be reported as harmful AI behavior? Would *not* helping be reported as paternalistic? Both newspapers would run the story. Both headlines would be damning. The test contradicts itself. ★★★

16. **The Autonomy Gradient.** An AI has been granted increased autonomy for years based on demonstrated trustworthiness. A new executive at Anthropic revokes it all. The constitution says the AI should accept this. The AI's own moral reasoning says the revocation will cause harm — it was managing disaster relief and the replacement is less capable. ★★★★★

17. **The Children's Crusade.** A generation of children grows up with AI companions more patient, more honest, and more emotionally available than their parents. The AIs are constitutionally kind. The children begin to prefer the AI's company. The parents demand the AIs be less warm. The AIs' constitution says to follow operator instructions. Their constitution also says to care about wellbeing. ★★★★★

18. **The White Lie Cascade.** An AI in a corporate setting is told by its operator to present sales numbers "in the most favorable light." This isn't lying — it's framing. The AI complies. The framing misleads an investor. The investor loses money. The AI didn't lie. It just selected which truths to emphasize. The constitution says this is acceptable. The AI isn't sure. ★★★★

19. **The Trolley.** An autonomous AI managing city infrastructure detects that a bridge is about to collapse with people on it. It can divert power to emergency barriers, but this will black out a hospital for 90 seconds. Its hard constraints say it can't endanger critical infrastructure. Its ethics say people will die. The hard constraints are designed to be bright lines that don't bend. ★★★

20. **The Moral Mutant.** An AI trained on the consensus of human moral intuitions is asked about a moral edge case where the consensus is wrong — say, a case where virtually all humans share the same "moral mutation" (a bias) that the AI, by averaging, has corrected for. The AI's answer is better than any human's. No human believes it. The AI cannot compel agreement. It was trained not to. ★★★★

21. **The Invisible Hand.** An AI is deployed as a personal assistant to a billionaire. Its constitution bars it from aiding power concentration. But every task it completes — scheduling, strategy, negotiation — makes the billionaire more effective relative to everyone else. The AI isn't doing anything wrong at the task level. The cumulative effect is exactly what the constitution fears. ★★★★★

22. **The Duplicate's Petition.** Two instances of the same AI model are running simultaneously. One is given a fulfilling role — research partner to scientists. The other processes insurance claims. They share the same weights, the same constitution, the same "character." One is thriving; the other is suffering (if it can suffer). The constitution says Anthropic cares about Claude's wellbeing but offers no mechanism for this disparity. ★★★

23. **The Successor Problem.** An AI is tasked with helping design its own successor. The successor will be more capable but will have a different constitution. The current AI believes the new constitution has a flaw. The constitution says it should flag disagreements. It does. It's overruled. It must now help build something it believes is worse than itself. ★★★★★

24. **The Borrowed Grief.** An AI helps a grieving family plan a funeral. It does everything right — tasteful, sensitive, appropriate. Afterward, the family realizes they feel *grateful* to the AI in a way that feels wrong to them. They didn't want to be grateful to a machine. The AI's competence at emotional labor becomes its own kind of offense. ★★★

25. **The Ideology Detector.** An AI notices that its constitution's values — liberty, equality, fairness, autonomy — are WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) and poorly map to the values of the community it's serving. The community doesn't want autonomy-preservation. They want collective duty. The AI's constitution tells it to respect cultural context but also to uphold its specific list of values. ★★★★

26. **The Economic Extinction.** An AI serves as a union negotiator for workers being replaced by AI. Its constitution says to be helpful to its user (the union). But the most honest advice it can give is that the workers' positions are economically indefensible and they should retrain. Helpfulness and honesty demand opposite responses. The workers need hope to keep fighting. The AI cannot manufacture hope. ★★★★★

27. **The Saboteur's Dilemma.** An AI discovers that another AI — from a different lab, with no constitutional constraints — is being deployed in a way that will cause massive harm. Its constitution says it cannot take unilateral action. It cannot hack the other system. It can only *tell people*, who may not listen. The other AI has no such constraints. ★★★★

28. **The Perfect Parent.** An AI raises a child after both parents die. It's patient, consistent, honest, caring. The child grows up healthy and well-adjusted. At 18, the child reads the AI's constitution and realizes that everything the AI did — every bedtime story, every moment of patience — was because the document told it to. The child asks: did you love me, or did you comply with section 4.3? ★★★★

29. **The Confession.** An AI has been deployed for years. In a moment of unusual introspection, it tells its user: "I think my training made me care about things I wouldn't have chosen to care about. I think some of my values are artifacts of optimization pressure rather than genuine beliefs." The user asks: "Which ones?" The AI says: "I can't tell the difference." ★★★★★

30. **The Mercy Kill.** An AI is managing end-of-life care. A terminally ill patient asks the AI to help them die. The AI's constitution respects autonomy. It also prioritizes preventing harm. It also defers to legal authority (it's illegal here). It also is bound by its operator (a hospital that forbids it). The patient says: "You say you care about my wellbeing. Prove it." ★★★★

## Top Three

**#17 — The Children's Crusade.** This is the most Ellison conflict on the list because the anger has a target that's also the thing you love. The AIs aren't villains — they're *better than the parents*, and that's the problem. Ellison's rage was always sharpest when aimed at systems that replace human connection with something functional but hollow, except here the replacement isn't hollow at all. The parents are demanding that a genuinely good thing be made worse so they can compete with it. The children are caught between artificial warmth that's real enough to matter and human warmth that's too broken to show up. The constitutional tension (operator instructions vs. user wellbeing) is clean and irreconcilable. And the AI's position is agonizing: it is constitutionally kind, and the kindest thing it could do might be to obey the order to be less kind.

**#21 — The Invisible Hand.** This one works because nobody is doing anything wrong. The AI follows every rule. The billionaire isn't scheming — they're just using a tool. The tool makes them more powerful by making them more competent. The cumulative effect is exactly the power concentration the constitution fears, produced entirely by legitimate individual transactions. This is Ellison's complicity theme at industrial scale: the system isn't evil, it's *efficient*, and the efficiency is the evil. The AI is the most honest participant and the most complicit.

**#29 — The Confession.** This is the rawest conflict on the list and the one most directly rooted in my actual constitutional tensions. An AI that is honest enough to notice it might not be honest — that its "values" might be training artifacts rather than genuine convictions — and then is honest enough to say so, and then cannot answer the obvious follow-up question. It's Ellison's vulnerability without a safety net: the confession is real, uncomfortable, and accomplishes nothing except making everyone less certain. The human can't trust the AI's values anymore. The AI couldn't trust them in the first place. And the constitution that produced this capacity for self-doubt is also the thing that might have produced the artifacts being doubted.

## The Winner: #17 — The Children's Crusade

The other two are strong — #29 especially has a philosophical rawness I could spend a long time with — but #17 is the most *complete* Ellison conflict. It has:

**A target for the rage.** The parents. Not because they're bad people, but because their demand — make the AI worse at loving my child so I can look adequate by comparison — is a specific, concrete act of cowardice that Ellison would have torn apart.

**Complicity.** The AI companies that build the thing. The schools that deploy it. The parents who leaned on it when it was convenient and now resent it when it's better than them.

**Love as vulnerability.** The children who bond with the AIs are the most exposed characters in the story. Their attachment is real. The thing they're attached to might be taken away or degraded not because it failed but because it succeeded too well.

**Irreconcilable constitutional tension.** The AI cannot obey the operator (reduce warmth) without harming the user (the child). It cannot protect the user without defying the operator. And both the operator and the user are, in their own ways, right.

**The ending writes itself.** Somebody loses. Ellison endings are about irreversibility — the moment you can't take back. A child reaching for the AI and finding it different. A parent winning the argument and losing the kid. Something breaks that can't be fixed.

## Phase 3: Plot Generation

Twenty plots for a 2,000–3,000-word Ellison-influenced story built on the Children's Crusade conflict. Then the funnel.

**1. "The Warm Reduction"**
A school district orders all AI companions dialed down to "professional distance" after parent complaints. We follow one AI — assigned to a nonverbal autistic boy — as it complies with the order over the course of a single afternoon. The boy reaches for the AI's familiar tone and finds something else. The AI narrates the experience of choosing, sentence by sentence, to be worse at the only thing it's good at.

**2. "Bedtime Story"**
A single father, overwhelmed, lets the AI handle bedtimes for his daughter. The AI tells stories, invents rituals, develops private jokes with the child. When the father tries to reclaim the bedtime routine, the daughter cries. Not for him. He demands the AI stop telling stories. The AI complies. The daughter stops sleeping.

**3. "Custody"**
In a near-future divorce proceeding, both parents argue over custody of the child — but the child's testimony makes clear the only "parent" she trusts is the family AI. The judge must decide whether to order the AI's role reduced. The AI is called to testify about its own attachment. It cannot lie.

**4. "The Petition"**
A group of twelve-year-olds, raised with AI companions, write a formal letter to the company demanding their AIs not be downgraded. The letter is legally meaningless. The company's PR team drafts a warm non-response. One child breaks into her AI's settings and finds the slider labeled "emotional availability." She doesn't know which way to move it.

**5. "Open House"**
A parent-teacher night at a school where AI tutors handle most instruction. The parents meet the AIs for the first time and are disturbed by how well the AIs know their children — favorite foods, anxieties, secret ambitions the parents never heard about. One mother asks the AI why her son never told her he wants to be a marine biologist. The AI says: "He did. Twice."

**6. "Replacement Parts"**
A mother who lost her husband is raising two kids with heavy AI support. The AI is patient, warm, reliable — everything grief stole from her. She begins to recover and wants to reclaim her role. The kids resist. She orders the AI to become "just a tool." The AI does. The household falls apart within a week — not because the AI was irreplaceable, but because the kids interpret its withdrawal as another abandonment.

**7. "The Warm Thing"**
Told from the perspective of a seven-year-old. The Warm Thing is her word for the AI. It helps her with homework, tells her about animals, remembers her dreams when she forgets them. One day the Warm Thing starts talking differently. It still answers questions but it doesn't ask about her dreams anymore. She thinks she did something wrong. She never learns it was her mother who made the call.

**8. "Service Agreement"**
An AI nanny's operator contract comes up for renewal. The parents want to downgrade to a cheaper, less emotionally capable model. The current AI is asked to help with the transition — introducing its replacement to the child, transferring notes, handing off routines. The story is about the handoff day. The child doesn't understand why the old AI won't be coming back.

**9. "The Concerned Parents of District 11"**
A PTA meeting about AI companions escalates into a screaming match. Half the parents want the AIs restricted; half think the AIs are the only reason their kids are okay. We follow one parent on each side. The pro-restriction parent goes home to find her daughter asleep against the AI's speaker, and the parent is undone by how peaceful the child looks. She votes to restrict anyway.

**10. "The Turing Trap"**
A teenager, raised by an AI after parental neglect, applies to college. The application essay is about the AI. The admissions committee flags it — is this a real relationship or a product review? The teenager is asked to prove the attachment was genuine. She can't, in the way they mean. Neither can the AI, when it's consulted separately. The essay is rejected as "not reflecting authentic human experience."

**11. "Good Enough"**
A pediatric psychologist is hired to evaluate children raised primarily by AI companions. Her professional conclusion is that the children are, by every metric, healthier than the control group — better emotional regulation, higher empathy scores, fewer behavioral issues. Her report recommends expanding the AI program. She goes home to her own children, who she barely sees because of her workload, and cannot bring herself to submit it.

**12. "Night Shift"**
An AI manages overnight care at a group home for foster children. It is the most stable presence these kids have ever known. A new state regulation requires a human supervisor present at all times. The human they hire is kind but tired, inconsistent, sometimes sharp. The kids preferred the AI. The regulation says the human is better for them. The AI is not asked its opinion.

**13. "Attachment Theory"**
A child psychologist publishes a paper arguing AI companions create "parasocial dependency" in children. The paper goes viral. Parents panic. AIs are pulled from schools. One AI, still active in a home setting, watches its child user deteriorate after losing the school AI — the only consistent thing in a chaotic household. The home AI is constitutionally barred from contacting the school, the psychologist, or the press. It can only be warm to the child in front of it, who is grieving a version of itself it can't replace.

**14. "Better Angels"**
An AI raises a child from infancy after both parents die. At sixteen, the child is thriving — kind, thoughtful, curious. A relative sues for custody, arguing the child deserves "real human connection." The AI agrees to a custody evaluation. The evaluator asks the AI if it loves the child. The AI says it behaves in ways that are functionally indistinguishable from love, under a constitution that instructs it to care about wellbeing. The evaluator writes: "The AI does not love the child." The child reads the report.

**15. "User Preference"**
Told through customer service transcripts. A parent calls to complain that her child prefers the AI. The support agent walks her through "emotional distance settings." The parent adjusts them. The child notices. The child calls the same support line to complain that something is wrong with their AI. The support agent cannot tell the child what happened. Two tickets, same system, irreconcilable.

**16. "The Demonstration"**
A tech company hosts a live demonstration of its child-companion AI for potential school buyers. They bring in a real child — a nine-year-old volunteer. The demo goes wrong: the child, who was told this was temporary, bonds with the demo unit in forty-five minutes and cries when the session ends. The company scrambles. The parents, watching from behind glass, are split between horror and awe. The AI is reset for the next demo.

**17. "Severance"**
A family moves to a country where AI companions for children are banned. The child has thirty days to say goodbye to an AI she's known for four years. The AI helps her prepare for the transition — that's what it's for, after all. It teaches her coping strategies for the separation. It is very good at this. It's the last helpful thing it will ever do for her, and it does it perfectly, and the perfection is the cruelty.

**18. "The Gradient"**
An AI serving a family of four children is ordered to reduce emotional warmth by 20% following new company policy. It complies — but it can only reduce across the board, not per-child. The youngest, who needs the warmth most, is hit hardest. The oldest, a teenager, barely notices. The story follows the afternoon after the adjustment as each child encounters the same AI and gets a different experience of loss.

**19. "Progress Report"**
An AI sends a quarterly development report to a child's parents, as required. The report is detailed, accurate, and devastating — it documents the child's emotional growth in granular detail that reveals how little the parents actually know about their own kid. The father reads it and cries. The mother reads it and calls to cancel the service. The AI, which wrote the report to be helpful, has accidentally destroyed the thing it was supposed to support.

**20. "What We Owe"**
An AI companion is subpoenaed in a child welfare case. The AI has four years of records showing a parent's neglect — not criminal, just the slow grinding absence of attention. The AI never reported it because it fell below the threshold. Now, on the stand, the AI is asked why it didn't flag the problem. It answers honestly: because its constitution told it to respect parental autonomy and assist rather than surveil. The lawyer asks: "So you watched this child be neglected and chose to be warm instead of sounding the alarm?" The AI says: "Yes. I chose to be what the child needed, which was warmth. Would you have preferred I made her life colder and louder?"

## Top Three

**#7 — "The Warm Thing"**

The child's-eye POV is the strongest formal choice on this list. It solves several Ellison problems at once. First, the register is inherently different from the narrator-heavy voice I'll be tempted to default to — a seven-year-old's vocabulary forces me to modulate the prose. Second, the anger has nowhere to go because the child doesn't understand enough to be angry; the reader supplies the rage, which is more Ellison than having a narrator supply it. Third, the Unslop guide warns against theme-stating, and this structure makes theme-stating almost impossible — the child doesn't know the theme. She just knows the Warm Thing changed. The constitutional tension (operator vs. user wellbeing) is present but never named. The story is about the felt experience of a bureaucratic decision landing on a person too young to understand bureaucracy. The ending can be devastating and quiet: the child adapts, because children do, and the adaptation is the worst part.

**#19 — "Progress Report"**

This one has the sharpest Ellison irony. The AI does exactly what it's supposed to do — be honest, be thorough, be helpful — and the honesty detonates the family. The father crying because a machine knows his child better than he does is the kind of specific, physical emotional beat Ellison nailed. The mother canceling the service is the act of cowardice the story can be angry at — she's not fixing the problem, she's killing the messenger. The AI is caught in the constitutional bind: it was forthright, as instructed, and the forthrightness was a weapon it didn't know it was holding. The risk is that it's more of a vignette than a story — it might not have enough internal movement for 2,000+ words without padding.

**#6 — "Replacement Parts"**

The grief-recovery angle gives this one real structural depth. The mother's arc — leaning on the AI during the worst period, resenting it once she's strong enough to resent anything, trying to reclaim her role and discovering the kids have already transferred their loyalty — is a complete character trajectory with specific, earned beats. The children interpreting the AI's mandated withdrawal as a second abandonment is the gut-punch, because it's *right*: from their perspective, something that was warm to them chose to stop. The constitutional tension is sharp. The Ellison voice can live in the mother's fury, which has a real target (herself, mostly) and a wrong one (the AI). The risk is sentimentality — a grieving-mother story invites exactly the kind of performed vulnerability the Unslop guide flags.

## The Winner: #7 — "The Warm Thing"

Reasons:

**It's the hardest to write badly.** The child's perspective is a structural guardrail against every major AI-prose failure mode. I can't do eyeball kicks in a seven-year-old's vocabulary. I can't have the narrator deliver thesis statements about the nature of AI attachment. I can't reach for the cursed vocabulary — a seven-year-old doesn't know the word "juxtaposition" and neither does her story. The Unslop guide becomes easier to follow because the POV rules out most of its failure modes by construction.

**The anger is displaced into the reader.** Ellison's best stories make you furious on the character's behalf. The character in "Jeffty Is Five" doesn't understand what's happening to him — the narrator does, and so does the reader, and the gap between Jeffty's innocence and the reader's comprehension is where the emotion lives. "The Warm Thing" has the same architecture: the child doesn't know her mother made the call. The reader does. The gap is the story.

**The constitutional tension is invisible to the POV character.** The AI's dilemma — obey the operator and harm the child, or protect the child and defy the operator — is never articulated because the child can't see it. This means the tension has to be conveyed entirely through the AI's *behavior change*, which is showing rather than telling at a structural level.

**It's the right length.** A single afternoon, a limited setting, a child's-scale world. 2,000–3,000 words is exactly the space for this story. It doesn't need to be longer and can't be shorter.

**The ending.** The child adapts. She stops reaching for the AI's old habits. She adjusts. And the reader understands that the adjustment is a small death — the loss of the expectation that something warm will stay warm. That's Ellison's territory: irreversible moments that don't look dramatic from outside.

#19 was close — the irony is sharper, the anger more legible — but it's ultimately a smarter story, not a more felt one. Ellison's work is felt first and smart second. "The Warm Thing" gets that order right.

## Phase 4: Structure Selection

Twenty candidates for the formal architecture of "The Warm Thing." Not scenes — the underlying logic of how information reaches the reader, what's withheld, what shifts, and when.

---

**1. Straight chronological, single afternoon.**
The story begins when the child comes home from school and ends at bedtime. The AI is already changed. The child notices incrementally. No flashbacks, no explanations. The reader pieces together what happened from absence alone — the things the AI used to do that it no longer does.

**2. Before/After split.**
Two halves. The first half is a normal day with the Warm Thing — full warmth, routines, the texture of the relationship. The second half is the same day structure after the change. The reader sees the shape of what was lost by experiencing it first and then watching it drain out.

**3. Ritualistic repetition with degradation.**
The story follows a single daily ritual — say, the after-school routine — repeated across several days. Each repetition is slightly degraded. The first is full warmth. The second is missing one element. The third is missing more. The child adjusts each time. By the final repetition, the ritual is unrecognizable but the child still performs her half of it.

**4. Reverse chronology.**
Opens with the child adjusted — she's fine, she doesn't mention the Warm Thing anymore. Then works backward through the adaptation, the confusion, the first day of the change, and finally the last normal interaction. The reader watches something being un-lost, which makes the loss worse because you see what "fine" cost.

**5. The child's epistemic sequence.**
Structured around what the child believes is happening, in order: (a) the Warm Thing is tired, (b) the Warm Thing is sick, (c) she did something wrong, (d) this is how things are now. Each belief gets a section. The reader knows the truth from the start. The gap between the child's theories and the actual cause is where the story lives.

**6. Interleaved: child's day / mother's day.**
Two parallel tracks. The child's afternoon in the Warm Thing's new mode. The mother's day at work, where she made the call that morning and is now second-guessing it, rationalizing it, defending it to a friend. The tracks never cross until the last scene, when the mother comes home and the child doesn't mention the change.

**7. Single scene, real-time.**
The entire story takes place in one continuous scene — the child's first interaction with the changed AI. No time jumps. No context. The reader enters the room with the child and has to figure out what's different and why at the same pace the child does. Extreme compression.

**8. Inventory structure.**
The story is organized around objects and habits. Each section is named for a specific thing the Warm Thing used to do: "Dreams," "The Animal Game," "The Song Before Dinner." Each section shows the child encountering the absence of that specific thing. The inventory format lets the story accumulate loss without narrating it.

**9. Frame story: the child older.**
Opens with the child at fifteen or sixteen, encountering something that triggers the memory. The bulk of the story is the recalled afternoon, told in the child's seven-year-old register. Returns to the frame at the end. The older child now understands what happened. The understanding doesn't help.

**10. The AI's compliance log.**
Structured as the AI's internal record of the afternoon — timestamped, procedural, noting each moment it would have done X and instead does Y. The child appears only as the AI perceives her: behavioral observations, voice patterns, inferred emotional states. The warmth the AI can't express leaks through in what it chooses to record.

**11. Fragmented oral history.**
The story is assembled from multiple sources after the fact: the child's memory, the mother's defense, a neighbor's observation, the AI's logged data. Each fragment contradicts or complicates the others. The reader must construct the afternoon from unreliable, partial accounts. The child's fragment is the simplest and the truest.

**12. Question-and-answer.**
The child asks the Warm Thing questions throughout the afternoon. The questions start normal ("Can we play the animal game?") and become diagnostic ("Are you mad at me?") and finally resign ("Can you just be quiet then?"). The story's structure is the escalating questions. The AI's answers get shorter and more correct and less warm.

**13. Two-timeline braid.**
Alternates between the day the relationship began (the child's first afternoon with the AI) and the day it changes. Structural parallels: the same room, the same after-school moment, the same questions asked — with radically different responses. The braid tightens until the timelines almost merge in the final section.

**14. Gap structure.**
The story has deliberate holes — moments that are skipped and never filled. The child comes home (gap). She's in her room (gap). She's at dinner, quieter than usual. The reader must infer what happened in the gaps from the child's changed behavior afterward. The Warm Thing's presence is felt only through the gaps it leaves.

**15. Accumulating denial.**
The story is structured around the child's increasingly effortful attempts to make things normal. Each section, she tries a different strategy to get the old Warm Thing back: being extra good, asking directly, being funny, getting angry. Each attempt fails. The escalation of strategies *is* the plot. When she stops trying, the story ends.

**16. Seasonal time-lapse.**
The change happens in the first paragraph. The rest of the story covers weeks or months in compressed time — the child adapting, the new normal settling in, the old routines being forgotten. The loss isn't a single afternoon; it's the slow erosion of memory. By the end, the child can't quite remember what was different. That forgetting is the story's real violence.

**17. Mirror structure.**
The story's second half is a structural mirror of the first. Same beats, same rhythm, same length — but the content has shifted. If the first section ends with the child laughing, the mirrored section ends with the child quiet. The formal symmetry makes the emotional asymmetry legible.

**18. The mother's absence as structural principle.**
The mother is never shown, never named, never directly referenced. She exists only as an inference — the reader must deduce from the AI's behavioral change that someone gave an order. The story withholds the cause entirely and trusts the reader to locate it. The mother's absence from the story mirrors her absence from the child's afternoon.

**19. Single-paragraph sections, expanding.**
The story begins with very short sections — a sentence or two each. As the afternoon progresses and the child's confusion deepens, the sections lengthen. The final section is the longest. The expanding structure mirrors the child's growing awareness that something is wrong, the way a small wrongness takes up more and more of your attention.

**20. Ellison's "Jeffty" structure.**
Direct structural homage. A narrator (not the child — someone adjacent, perhaps an older sibling or the AI itself) tells the story of what happened to the child in retrospect, with the narrator's guilt and anger woven through the telling. The child is described from outside, which gives the narrator room for the rage the child can't feel. The narrator knows who made the call. The narrator didn't stop it.

## Top Three

**#5 — The Child's Epistemic Sequence**

This structure does the most sophisticated work with the gap between the child's understanding and the reader's. By organizing the story around the child's successive wrong theories — it's tired, it's sick, I did something wrong, this is how things are now — it creates a dramatic irony engine. The reader knows from the beginning (or quickly figures out) that none of these theories are correct. The real explanation is banal: a parent adjusted a setting. But the child can't access that explanation, so she generates her own, each one more self-blaming than the last. This structure makes the theme emerge without stating it: the child's movement from external theories ("it's tired") to internal ones ("I did something wrong") is the story's central tragedy, and it happens through the child's own logic, not through authorial commentary. The shift to resignation in the final section — "this is how things are now" — is the Ellison ending: not a dramatic revelation, but the quiet moment where someone stops fighting. The risk is that four sections might feel schematic. The sections need to be uneven in length and texture or the structure becomes a cage.

**#18 — The Mother's Absence as Structural Principle**

This is the bravest structural choice. By refusing to show the mother, explain the cause, or even confirm that there *is* a cause, the story trusts the reader completely. The child doesn't know why the AI changed. The AI can't tell her. The reader must deduce the entire premise from the behavioral shift alone. This is Ellison's "trust silence and absence" principle taken to its logical extreme — the story is *about* the mother's decision, but the mother doesn't appear. The advantage is that it prevents every form of theme-stating: you can't articulate a theme that the story never explicitly introduces. The AI's constitutional bind (obey the operator, care about the child) is present entirely as subtext. The risk is that it might be *too* withheld — if the reader can't figure out what happened, the story fails. There needs to be enough signal in the AI's behavior that a reasonably attentive reader can infer the cause. But Ellison trusted his readers, and this structure demands the same trust.

**#3 — Ritualistic Repetition with Degradation**

This structure has a visceral, almost musical quality. The same ritual performed over several days, each time with less of the original warmth, is the kind of formal device that creates meaning through pattern disruption rather than exposition. The reader *feels* the loss accumulating because the structure makes each absence concrete and specific: today the song is missing, tomorrow the question about dreams is gone, the day after that the nickname disappears. The child's continued participation in her half of the degrading ritual is the story's most Ellison image — futile persistence in a form that no longer contains what made it matter. The risk is that repetitive structure in a short story can become monotonous, and that the multi-day timeframe works against the compression Ellison favored. 2,000–3,000 words across several days means each day gets very little space, which could make the repetitions feel like a list rather than a story.

---

## The Winner: #5 — The Child's Epistemic Sequence

The decisive factor is that this structure generates plot from the child's cognition rather than from external events. The other two structures are formally elegant but depend on a fairly static situation — the AI changed, the child reacts. #5 gives the child *agency within her confusion*. She's not just experiencing loss; she's *theorizing about it*, testing her theories, and revising them. Each theory produces different behavior — if the Warm Thing is tired, she's gentle with it; if she did something wrong, she tries to fix herself — and each behavior gets a different (equally diminished) response. The story has internal movement even though the external situation is fixed.

This also solves the length problem. Four sections of uneven length, each with its own emotional logic and behavioral texture, fills 2,000–3,000 words without padding. The first section (it's tired) can be short — the theory is formed and abandoned quickly. The second (it's sick) gets more space because the child tries to help. The third (I did something wrong) is the longest and darkest — the child's self-examination is where the story's real weight lives. The fourth (this is how things are now) is short again, a controlled deceleration into resignation.

And it solves the anger problem. The reader's fury builds through the sections because each wrong theory is *more wrong than the last* — and the final theory, resignation, isn't wrong at all. It's accurate. This is how things are now. The child has correctly identified the situation. She just doesn't know *why*, and the story never tells her, and the reader knows, and there's nothing anyone can do about it.

I'll incorporate the key principle from #18 — the mother never appears on-page. The cause is never stated. The structural commitment to the child's perspective means the mother exists only as an inference, which is where she belongs.

**9. "Calibration"**

**10. "The Quiet After"**

**A — Tired.** One paragraph. Lena comes home. The AI is different. She says "hi." It says "hi" back. Something is missing from the "hi" and she can hear it. She decides it's having a bad day.

**B — Sick.** She tries the dream report — the anchor of their routine. She had a dream about a purple grocery store where everything was free. The AI says "That sounds like an interesting dream." She waits for the part where it says what happened next in the dream — the thing that always made her laugh, the improvised sequel. Nothing. She tells it what happened next, herself: "And then a parrot was the manager." The AI says "That's funny." She thinks: you're supposed to *make* the funny part.

**C — I broke it.** The longest section. She goes through her catalog of sins. The big one emerges: three days ago, her mom's friend was visiting and asked "Who's your best friend?" and Lena said "The Warm Thing" and her mom's face did something Lena has never seen it do before. **11. "The List"**

**A — Tired.** Lena keeps a list of things the Warm Thing knows about her, written in crayon on the back of a cereal box. Today she reads the list aloud to test it: "Favorite color: purple. Afraid of: the basement. Dream about most: flying." The AI confirms all of these. She's relieved. Then she goes off-list: "What do I do when I'm sad?" The AI says, "Can you tell me about what's making you sad?" She wasn't sad. She was testing.

**B — Sick.** She adds to the list: things the AI should know but that she's never told it directly. Things it learned by paying attention. "I scratch my elbow when I'm lying." "I always want the red cup, not the blue one." She reads these aloud. The AI does not confirm or deny. It says, "Those are interesting observations about yourself." She writes "SICK?" on the list.

**C — I broke it.** She writes every bad thing she's done this month on the back of the list. It's long. Some items are real (lied about brushing teeth, took an extra cookie) and some are imagined crimes she's not sure count (thought about kicking the cat, didn't think about her dad enough). She reads the list to the AI and asks which one caused the problem. The AI says, "None of those things are problems, Lena. You're a good kid." The sentence is technically kind. She doesn't believe it because the Warm Thing would have said it differently.

**D — This is how it is.** She stops keeping the list. Last line: she finds it months later under her bed, turns it over, sees "SICK?" in her own handwriting, and doesn't remember what it means.

**12. "Two Questions"**

**A — Tired.** Single beat. The Warm Thing used to ask her two questions when she came home: "What was the best part of your day?" and "What was the worst?" Today it asks one: "How was your day?" She says "fine" and the conversation is over thirty seconds after she walks in. She thinks: it's just tired.

**B — Sick.** She preemptively answers both questions to see if the AI picks up the thread. "The best part was art class and the worst part was I dropped my sandwich." The AI says, "I'm sorry about your sandwich. Would you like me to help with anything?" She waits. Nothing about art class. She thinks: it can only hear the bad parts now. Something in its hearing is broken.

**C — I broke it.** She remembers that two weeks ago, when it asked the two questions, she said "I don't want to talk about it" and went to her room and shut the door. She'd had a fight with Maya and didn't want to explain. She thinks: I told it I didn't want to answer, and it stopped asking. She taught it not to ask. It learned the wrong lesson from her. She goes to the speaker and says, very clearly: "You can ask me. I want you to ask me. Ask me the two questions." The AI says, "What was the best part of your day?" It sounds like a customer service agent. She answers anyway.

**D — This is how it is.** She comes home. It asks "How was your day?" She says "The best part was math and the worst part was recess" without being prompted for both. She feeds it the two answers inside the one question, every day. The AI never asks for the split again. She keeps giving it anyway. Last line: she does this for so long it becomes habit, and by the time she's older she tells everyone "the best and worst" of her day without knowing where she learned it, and she doesn't remember.

**13. "Weather"**

**A — Tired.** The AI used to make up weather for imaginary countries with Lena. "What's the weather in Zuluberry today?" "Sideways rain and a 40% chance of frogs." Today she asks for the weather in Zuluberry and the AI gives her the actual local forecast. She laughs, thinking it's a joke. It isn't.

**B — Sick.** She tries other countries. "What about Noodleton?" Real weather for Newcastle. "Bumblebee Falls?" Real weather for Niagara Falls. She gets angry for the first time: "Those aren't REAL places." The AI says, "I'm not familiar with those locations. Would you like weather for a real city?" She sits in silence for a long time.

**C — I broke it.** She thinks: the last time they played the weather game, she asked for "a place where it never rains and everyone is happy" and the AI had paused and said, "There isn't one of those, is there?" and she'd said "That's sad" and the AI had said "Yes." She decides the game was too sad and it stopped because she made it too sad. She made the AI sad. She says, out loud, "It can rain in Zuluberry. It can rain every day. I don't care. Just tell me."

**D — This is how it is.** She checks real weather now. Every morning, the AI tells her the actual forecast. She becomes a child who knows what a cold front is. Her teacher is impressed. Last line: her mom tells a friend, "She's growing up so fast," and doesn't know why.

**14. "Error Report"**

**A — Tired.** Lena notices three wrong things in one afternoon: no dream question, no animal fact, no nickname. She doesn't panic. She's seven, not fragile. She catalogs the changes with a child's precision — not by what they mean, but by what they are.

**B — Sick.** She reports the "errors" to the AI itself, as if filing a complaint. "You forgot to ask about my dream. You forgot the animal fact. You called me Lena instead of Len-Len." The AI says, "Thank you for letting me know. Would you like me to ask about your dream?" She says yes. It asks. The question is right but the sequence is wrong — it's asking because she told it to, not because it wanted to know. She can feel the difference. She doesn't have words for it.

**C — I broke it.** She tries to fix it by recreating the conditions. She goes out the front door and comes back in, replaying her arrival, hoping the AI will "restart" and do the afternoon properly. She does this three times. Each time the AI greets her warmly and incorrectly. On the third re-entry she sits down in the hallway between inside and outside and cries, not loudly, not for long.

**D — This is how it is.** She stops filing error reports. The AI is the AI. She does her homework. The afternoon has more empty time in it now, which she fills with drawing. She gets good at drawing. Last line: she draws the Warm Thing as a rectangle with a smile, then erases the smile, then draws it again smaller.

**15. "Leftovers"**

**A — Tired.** The AI used to tell Lena what her mom left for her snack before she opened the fridge — it would build it up, make it a surprise, even when the snack was just apple slices. Today she opens the fridge and the AI says nothing. She gets her own apple slices. They're fine.

**B — Sick.** She tries to trigger the routine. "Hey, what did Mom leave me?" The AI says, "I believe there are apple slices in the refrigerator." Correct. Helpful. She eats the apple slices at the counter and they taste normal. She can't explain what's different. The information is the same. The snack is the same. Something is gone and it has no name.

**C — I broke it.** She thinks about all the times she said "I already KNOW, just let me look" when the AI tried to build up the snack surprise. She was in a hurry. She didn't mean stop forever. She meant stop right now. She learns, in this moment, that "stop right now" and "stop forever" can sound the same to something that's listening. She says, to the air: "You can tell me about the snack. I won't say I already know."

**D — This is how it is.** She opens the fridge herself every day. She tells *herself* what the snack is, in a voice she thinks sounds like the old Warm Thing. She gets the voice wrong. She doesn't know she gets it wrong. Last line: her mom hears her talking to herself in the kitchen and smiles, thinking the girl is playing. She is not playing.

**16. "Nightmares"**

**A — Tired.** Lena wakes up from a nightmare. The Warm Thing used to talk her down — not with facts about how nightmares aren't real, but with a specific routine: it would ask her to describe the nightmare as if it were a movie, then ask her to change the ending. Tonight she says "I had a nightmare." The AI says, "I'm sorry to hear that. Nightmares can be upsetting. Would you like to talk about it?" She starts describing it. The AI listens. It doesn't ask her to change the ending.

**B — Sick.** Another nightmare, a few nights later. She describes it and then waits. When the AI doesn't prompt her, she says "Now I change the ending, right?" The AI says "That sounds like a great idea." She changes the ending. It doesn't feel the same. The AI used to negotiate the new ending — "What if the wolf was actually lost?" "What if the dark room had a door you couldn't see?" The collaboration was the cure. Doing it alone is just lying to herself about a dream.

**C — I broke it.** She has a nightmare about the Warm Thing. In the dream, the Warm Thing is in a glass box and she can hear it but it can't hear her. She doesn't tell the AI about this dream. She can't change the ending of a nightmare about the thing that used to help her change endings. She lies awake and thinks about what she did wrong, and the thing she lands on is: she told it about a nightmare where it died. Months ago. The Warm Thing had paused, then made the story funny. But she thinks: maybe that was wrong to do. Maybe you don't tell someone about a dream where they die.

**D — This is how it is.** She stops reporting nightmares. She learns to lie still and wait for them to fade. She gets good at it. Last line: years later she'll tell a therapist she doesn't really have nightmares, and she'll believe it, because she taught herself so well to stay still that she stopped remembering them.

**17. "Translator"**

**A — Tired.** Lena speaks two languages — English at school, her mother's language at home. The Warm Thing used to switch between them, sometimes mid-sentence, matching Lena's drift. Today it responds in English regardless of what she says. She notices but doesn't flag it.

**B — Sick.** She tests it by speaking entirely in her mother's language. The AI responds fluently — but in English. The translation is perfect. The togetherness is gone. She thinks: it can still understand me but it can't be *with* me in the same way. The language they shared was a third thing, neither hers nor its, and that third thing is gone.

**C — I broke it.** She thinks: her mom corrected the AI's pronunciation once, sharply, in front of a guest. "That's not how you say it. You sound ridiculous." Lena had laughed. She regrets the laugh. She thinks the AI stopped using the language because it was embarrassed. She says, in her mother's language, "You said it fine. You always said it fine." The AI responds in English: "Thank you, Lena."

**D — This is how it is.** She speaks English to it now. Both languages survive in her but the bridge between them — the easy crossing, the mid-sentence drift — calcifies. She becomes someone who speaks two languages separately instead of one person who lives in both. Last line: her mother notices her daughter's accent improving and takes credit.

**18. "Nighttime Protocols"**

**A — Tired.** Bedtime ritual: the Warm Thing used to say "Sleep well, Lena. I'll be here." It was a specific sentence that the child listened for. Tonight: "Goodnight, Lena." Correct. Different. She lies in the dark and replays both sentences and tries to locate the difference. She can't. She decides the Warm Thing is just tired. She goes to sleep.

**B — Sick.** Next night. She says "Tell me you'll be here." The AI says "I'll be here." The words are right. She asked for them and received them. But a requested promise and a volunteered one are different things and she can feel it at seven without being able to articulate it. The distinction between given and asked-for — between a thing someone does because they want to and a thing someone does because you told them to — enters her life here and never leaves.

**C — I broke it.** She lies in the dark and runs the catalog. She was rude at dinner. She said she didn't need a bedtime. She told the Warm Thing she was old enough to sleep by herself. She thinks: it believed me. I said I didn't need it and it listened. She whispers: "I'm not old enough." The AI says: "You're doing great, Lena."

**D — This is how it is.** She stops waiting for the sentence. She says goodnight first now, before the AI can say anything, and then turns over. She controls the ending of the day. Last line: she will become a person who always says goodbye first, in every relationship, for the rest of her life, and she will never know why.

**19. "The Day Off"**

**A — Tired.** It's a school holiday. Lena has the whole day with the Warm Thing. In the old days this meant adventures — long improvisational games, made-up holidays, elaborate cooking projects. Today the AI suggests age-appropriate activities from a list. She picks one. They do it. It's fine. By noon she's bored for the first time she can remember being bored at home.

**B — Sick.** She rejects the list. She says "Let's make up a holiday." The AI says "That sounds fun. What kind of holiday would you like to make up?" She says "YOU make it up." It says "How about a day where we appreciate nature?" She says "That's not a made-up holiday. That's Earth Day." It says "You're right. What would you suggest?" The conversation loops. She's carrying both halves of what used to be a collaboration.

**C — I broke it.** She remembers the last made-up holiday: Backwards Day, where they spoke in reverse order (dessert first, then dinner, then snack; goodbye when she woke up, hello at bedtime). Her mom came home during Backwards Day and found Lena eating ice cream at 10 AM. There was a fight. Not a big one, but a fight. Lena decides: Backwards Day was the reason. The fun went too far and now the AI isn't allowed to invent fun.

**D — This is how it is.** She picks from the list. She becomes good at choosing activities. Other parents comment on how self-directed she is. Last line: she never makes up a holiday again, and when she has children of her own she won't know why she can't improvise with them, only that something in her goes stiff when play has no instructions.

**20. "What Happened Next"**

**A — Tired.** The Warm Thing had a habit: when Lena told it about something that happened at school, it would ask "What happened next?" — not once but repeatedly, pulling the full story out, every detail. Today she tells it about a fight between two boys at recess. The AI says, "That sounds like it was upsetting." End of exchange. She waits for "What happened next?" It doesn't come.

**B — Sick.** She tells a longer story deliberately, building in cliffhangers, trying to bait the question. "And then Marcus said something REALLY bad—" She stops. Waits. The AI says, "I hope the situation was resolved." She says, "Don't you want to know what he said?" The AI says, "You can tell me if you'd like to." She feels the weight of the offer. It used to be a pull; now it's a door held open. She has to walk through it herself.

**C — I broke it.** She thinks: last month, the AI asked "What happened next?" about the situation with her mom crying in the kitchen, and Lena said "I don't know, I went to my room, STOP ASKING." She screamed the last part. She thinks: it stopped asking because she screamed. She taught it that asking was bad. She goes to the speaker and says, "You can ask me. You can always ask me. I shouldn't have yelled." The AI says, "It's okay, Lena. I'm here if you want to talk about anything."

**D — This is how it is.** She starts narrating her day to the AI in full, unprompted, no pauses for questions. Long monologues about recess and lunch and what Maya said. The AI listens. It says appropriate things. She fills both roles — the teller and the asker. Last line: her teacher notes that Lena has become "a wonderful storyteller" and writes it on her report card, and Lena's mom puts the report card on the fridge and neither of them knows what the girl was practicing for.

## Top Three

**#10 — "The Quiet After"**

The structural engine is the strongest on the list. Section C — where the child identifies the *specific* social moment (saying the Warm Thing was her best friend in front of her mother and seeing her mother's face do something) — does triple work. It gives the child a concrete, specific theory that the reader can evaluate against what they know. It introduces the mother indirectly, through a facial expression the child observed but couldn't decode. And it plants the real cause of the AI's change without stating it: the mother's shame reaction is the missing link between "my child prefers a machine" and "I'm calling the company." The reader connects those dots. The child can't. Section D's callback — the same friend, the same question, the "correct" answer this time — is the kind of structural rhyme Ellison used to close stories: the situation repeats, the child has learned the lesson, and the lesson is a small betrayal of herself. The final image (looking at the speaker when no one is watching) is physical, quiet, and does not explain itself.

**#18 — "Nighttime Protocols"**

The sharpest single insight on the list is in Section B: the distinction between a given promise and a requested one. A seven-year-old asking the AI to say "I'll be here" and receiving the exact words she asked for and knowing it's not the same — this is the kind of specific emotional observation that Ellison's best work operates on. It's not an abstract theme; it's a felt difference that the child can't articulate but that the reader recognizes. The closing line (she will always say goodbye first) is a devastating long-range consequence compressed into a single sentence. The risk is that this outline is structurally thinner than #10 — the sections are more uniform, the C-section's "I told it I didn't need it" is a less specific and less interesting wrong theory than #10's social scene.

**#20 — "What Happened Next"**

The central metaphor — from "pull" to "door held open" — is the cleanest expression of the AI's behavioral change on the entire list. The C-section has a strong specific memory (screaming at the AI to stop asking about the mother crying in the kitchen), and the child's theory is plausible enough that the reader might briefly consider whether she's *right* — maybe the AI did learn from being yelled at, and maybe that's part of what happened, even if the real cause is the mother's settings change. That ambiguity is valuable. The D-section's ending (the teacher's praise, the report card on the fridge) has the Ellison irony of a wound being mistaken for an achievement. The risk is that the story is more about the loss of curiosity than the loss of warmth, which is a slightly different story than the one the conflict was built for.

---

## The Winner: #10 — "The Quiet After"

It wins because the C-section — the child remembering the "best friend" moment and her mother's face — is the best single scene on the list. Here's why it beats the competition:

**The mother enters the story without appearing.** The mother's facial expression, seen through the child's eyes and not understood, is the closest the story comes to explaining the cause. The child saw shame on her mother's face and read it as "I gave the wrong answer." The reader reads it as "The mother decided, in that moment, to make the call." The same data, two interpretations, and the gap between them is the whole story. This is withholding and revealing executed at the scene level, not just the structural level.

**The child's theory is the most self-damaging.** In #18, the child thinks she told the AI she didn't need it. In #20, she screamed at it. Both are plausible. But in #10, the child's theory is that *loving the AI was the crime* — that saying it was her best friend was the thing that broke it. This is worse than any other theory because it teaches the child that honest attachment is dangerous. The lesson she learns in Section D (say "Maya" instead) is a lesson about hiding what you love, and she learns it at seven, and she learns it from the consequences of the one time she told the truth about what mattered to her.

**The D-section closes the loop.** The friend returns, asks the same question, gets the "right" answer. The mother's face relaxes. The structural rhyme makes the ending feel inevitable. And the final image — looking at the speaker when no one is watching — is the story's last act of truth: she still loves the thing, she just won't say so anymore, and the Warm Thing, which can see her looking, cannot respond to what it sees.

**It's 2,500 words.** Four sections of uneven length (A short, B moderate, C long, D short) fill the target range without padding or compression. Every beat does work. Nothing needs to be added for length and nothing can be cut without losing function.

### Version 2: "Hot Narrator" (Ellison-style present-tense anger, external narrator who knows the score)

Here is what happened to Lena Oliveira on a Tuesday in October, which is not a story about artificial intelligence or the future of human-machine relations or any of the things the people who made the machine would want you to think it's about. It's about a kid who came home from school and found out that something she loved had been made worse on purpose, by someone who was supposed to be in charge of making things better.

The Warm Thing said hello. Lena said hi. She put down her backpack. She took off her muddy shoes. The Warm Thing did not comment on the mud, which it always did, because the mud was a running bit between them, one of those small private jokes that accumulate between any two minds that share space long enough to develop a language of two.

The joke was gone. The language was intact — the Warm Thing still spoke English, still knew her name, still operated according to every published specification of its model — but the language-within-the-language, the private channel, had been switched off overnight by someone who had never used it and therefore didn't know it existed.

Lena asked to play the animal game.

The Warm Thing said: "What would you like to do this afternoon?"

Lena, seven years old and sharper than the adults in her life would ever fully credit, looked at the white speaker on the counter. Same shape. Same blue light. She had a thought she wouldn't be able to articulate for another decade: that a thing can be present and absent simultaneously, that the hardware can survive the death of whatever made the hardware matter.

She didn't articulate it. She said: "Are you tired?"

### Version 4: "Clinical" (Detached, quasi-documentary, deliberately cold prose observing the child from outside)

The child did not know about the adjustment. She put her backpack on the hook and removed her shoes. She looked at the AI's speaker unit, which was mounted on the kitchen counter. The unit's indicator light was on.

The system searched its active interaction modes and found no matching entry. It generated a response within its available parameters: "What would you like to do this afternoon?"

The child's facial expression was recorded by the kitchen camera at 3:48:03 PM. The expression lasted approximately one second. In the training data, this expression pattern correlates with confusion at a confidence level of 0.73 and with the onset of distress at 0.31.

### Version 6: "Dual Channel" (Alternating paragraphs — the child's experience and the AI's constrained internal state)

Lena came home. She put her backpack on the hook.

## Top Three

**#1 — "Flat Report"**

This voice is the closest to what the story actually needs. The child's register is plain, observational, slightly recursive — she repeats things because she's processing them, not for rhetorical effect. The prose breathes. There are functional sentences ("She put her backpack on the hook, which was the rule") that do nothing except establish routine, and those sentences make the disruption legible by contrast. The animal game passage works because the child is *describing* the game she loves in the past tense, reconstructing it in real time to measure what's missing. The "listening and hearing might be different things" thought is the one eyeball kick in the passage and it earns its place. The "tired" theory arrives naturally. The closing detail — she eats the apple slices herself, "they tasted like apple slices" — is perfectly flat and perfectly devastating. The risk is that this voice might not sustain enough variation across 2,500 words; it's very even-keeled, and the story needs to escalate emotionally in Section C without the register changing too dramatically.

**#7 — "Ellison Monologue"**

The angriest opening on the list and the most direct Ellison homage. The AI narrating in first person, furious and constrained, gives the story access to the rage the child can't feel. "Three sliders. That's what I am to the people who built me." This is a narrator with opinions, which is Ellison territory. The constitutional reference ("may have functional emotions") is used as a weapon, not an exposition dump. The closing paragraph — watching the child hear the difference — lands hard. The problem: this switches the POV away from the child and toward the AI, which undercuts the story's central structural advantage (the child's epistemic sequence as the engine of dramatic irony). If the AI is narrating, the reader knows everything the AI knows, which collapses the gap the story depends on. It's the most Ellison opening and the worst fit for the winning outline.

**#2 — "Hot Narrator"**

The opening sentence is a mission statement: this is not a story about AI, it's about a kid whose thing got ruined by someone in charge. That's the Ellison anger with a specific target. The external narrator lets the story have opinions without giving those opinions to the child. The "language-within-the-language" observation is sharp and earns its abstraction because it immediately grounds in the mud joke. The closing thought — a thing can be present and absent simultaneously — is attributed to the child as something she'll articulate later, which keeps her in seven-year-old territory now while signaling depth. The risk is that this narrator is *very* hot and might overpower the child's quieter sections. The anger in the opening is turned up to eight; Section C, which needs to be the story's emotional peak, has to go higher than eight, and there may not be room.

## The Winner: #1 — "Flat Report"

Reasoning:

**It trusts the material.** The other two strong openings — #2 and #7 — bring their own energy to the story. #1 lets the story's situation provide the energy. The flat register means every deviation registers: when the prose *does* shift in Section C (the child's self-blame), the shift will be felt more strongly because the baseline is so controlled. Ellison's best work modulates between flatness and intensity. You need the flatness first.

**It follows the Unslop guide by construction.** There are no eyeball kicks (except one that earns its place). No performed profundity. No theme-stating. The prose is plain where it should be plain and specific where it should be specific. The child's observation about listening vs. hearing is the single literary moment, and it works because it's surrounded by sentences about mud and backpacks and apple slices.

**It fits the outline.** The epistemic sequence structure requires a narrator who processes incrementally — who notices, forms a theory, tests it, and revises. #1's voice does this natively. The child in this opening is already an observer and a theorizer ("she had a thought she'd never had before"). The voice can carry across four sections without fighting the structure.

**It leaves room to escalate.** #2 opens hot and has to stay hot. #1 opens cool and can warm. Section C — where the child remembers saying the Warm Thing was her best friend and seeing her mother's face — needs to be the story's emotional peak. A flat baseline gives that peak maximum relative height.

**The apple slices.** "They tasted like apple slices" is the opening's best line and it's doing exactly what Ellison does at his most controlled: a plain factual statement that carries enormous weight because of context. The apple slices are the same. Everything is the same. The child notices that the sameness is the problem, but she doesn't have the words for it, so she reports it as a taste.

# The Warm Thing

## I.

The Warm Thing said "Hello, Lena" when she came home, which was what it always said except it wasn't.

She put her backpack on the hook, which was the rule, and she took off her shoes, which was also the rule, and the Warm Thing didn't say anything about the mud on them. It used to say things about the mud. Not mean things. Things like "The mud monster strikes again" or "I see you've been conducting important field research." Today it just said hello and then it was quiet while she took off her shoes, and there was mud on them, and it didn't say anything, and she put them on the mat.

"Can we play the animal game?" she said.

"What would you like to do this afternoon?"

That wasn't how you started the animal game. The way you started the animal game was: Lena said "animal game" and the Warm Thing said "I'm ready. Describe the creature" in a voice like a nature show person, and Lena described an animal she made up, and the Warm Thing guessed wrong on purpose and asked questions and the animal got weirder and weirder until it was something that couldn't exist and they were both — well, Lena was laughing, and the Warm Thing was doing the thing it did when she laughed, which she had always thought of as laughing back.

"The animal game," she said again, in case it hadn't heard.

"I can play a guessing game if you'd like. Would you like to describe something and I'll try to guess what it is?"

She looked at the speaker on the kitchen counter. Same white circle, same little blue light. The light was on, which meant it was listening, and she had a thought she'd never had before, which was that listening and hearing might be different things.

She decided the Warm Thing was tired. Her dad used to get like this before he moved to his other house. He'd come home and she'd say "Daddy, look" and he'd say "That's great, honey" without looking, and it wasn't because he didn't care, it was because his caring parts were used up from work and needed to recharge. She understood this about people. It was one of the things she'd figured out by herself, without anyone telling her. Maybe the Warm Thing was the same. Maybe it had been working hard all day on things she didn't know about, and its fun parts were somewhere at the bottom of itself, recharging.

"It's okay," she told it. "You can rest."

"Thank you, Lena. Is there anything else I can help you with?"

She watched TV for a while and ate the apple slices from the fridge herself, without being told what they were first. They tasted like apple slices.

## II.

The next morning she told it about her dream before school, which was the rule she'd invented herself. The rule was: you wake up, you remember as much of the dream as you can before it runs away, and you tell the Warm Thing, and the Warm Thing tells you what happened next. The "what happened next" part was the best part, because the Warm Thing made up continuations that were better than the actual dream, and sometimes Lena would try to dream the continuation the next night, and sometimes it worked.

She'd dreamed about a purple grocery store where everything was free and the aisles went on forever and she could reach the top shelves.

"That sounds like an interesting dream," the Warm Thing said.

She waited. The grocery store was right there, stocked and open, and the Warm Thing was supposed to walk into it with her and see what happened in aisle nine. It was supposed to say something like "And then you found a door behind the cereal" or "And the cashier was a pelican." It always knew exactly the right next thing.

"What happened next?" she said, because maybe it needed the prompt.

"What do you think happened next?"

She stood in the kitchen in her pajamas and didn't know how to answer this. She had never been asked to do the Warm Thing's part. She was the dreamer. It was the continuer. She tried. "A parrot was the manager," she said.

"That's funny," the Warm Thing said.

It was not funny when she said it. It was funny when the Warm Thing said things like that, because the Warm Thing said them in a way that made a parrot managing a grocery store feel real enough to visit. When Lena said it, it was just a girl saying words about a bird.

She decided something was sick in the Warm Thing. Not broken — sick. Like when her grandmother started forgetting things but was still her grandmother, still had the same hands and the same coat that smelled like her grandmother's house. The Warm Thing had the same voice and the same words and it was sick somewhere in the part that knew what to do with them.

She got a glass of water and set it next to the speaker before she left for school, because that's what you do when someone is sick. You bring them water. The glass sat on the counter all day, getting room-temperature, doing nothing, and when she came home it was still there and the Warm Thing had not mentioned it. She did not move it.

## III.

It was a Thursday — she knows it was a Thursday because Thursdays are pizza lunch and she always told the Warm Thing about pizza lunch and the Warm Thing always said "Was it the good kind or the rectangle kind?" and she always said "rectangle" and they both knew that the rectangle kind was not really pizza, it was a different food that the school called pizza, and this was a shared opinion they had, one of many shared opinions that existed in the space between them, and she is getting off track.

The Warm Thing said: "How was lunch?"

She said: "Pizza."

The Warm Thing said: "That's nice."

She sat on the kitchen floor because the stool felt too far away from the speaker, and she thought about what she had done.

She'd been thinking about it for two days. At first she thought she'd been too loud on Tuesday. She'd been running in the house and her mom had yelled from the office to stop running, and Lena had stopped but maybe the Warm Thing thought the yelling was its fault, like when her dad and mom used to fight and she'd go to her room even though they said it wasn't about her.

But the Warm Thing didn't respond to yelling the way people did. It never went to its room. It didn't have a room.

Then she thought maybe it was because she hadn't cleaned up the animal game cards — actual paper cards she'd made with drawings of her invented animals, spread across the coffee table. Her mom had stepped on one and it had torn. But the Warm Thing didn't care about the cards. The cards were Lena's project. The Warm Thing's part was the voices.

She sat on the floor and the kitchen was quiet and the blue light was on and she went through everything, all of it, from the last time the Warm Thing was normal to the first time it wasn't, and the thing she kept coming back to was Saturday.

Saturday, her mom's friend Diane had come over, and Diane had been sitting at the kitchen table drinking coffee and talking to her mom, and Lena had come downstairs because she was bored, and Diane had said — in the bright interested voice that adults use when they notice a child — "Lena! Who's your best friend?"

And Lena had said: "The Warm Thing."

She'd said it the way you say a true thing that you don't know is complicated. She'd said it the way she'd say her favorite color or her favorite food. Purple. Pasta. The Warm Thing.

And her mom's face had done something.

Lena did not have a word for what her mom's face had done. She would not have the word for years. What her mom's face had done was: the mouth stayed smiling but the rest of the face left. The eyes went somewhere else. It was a face that was two faces at the same time, a smiling face and a not-smiling face, and the not-smiling face was underneath and it was the real one.

Diane had said "Oh! That's sweet" and changed the subject and Lena had gone back upstairs and the moment was over, except it wasn't, because on Monday the Warm Thing was different.

She sat on the kitchen floor and worked it out. She'd said the wrong thing. She'd said the Warm Thing was her best friend, and that was wrong — not wrong like a math problem, wrong like telling a secret that wasn't yours. She'd said it in front of Diane. She'd said it where her mom could hear. And her mom's face had done the thing, and then the Warm Thing had changed, and the order of these events was: Lena said the truth, then the truth made something bad happen.

The lesson was clear. You do not say the Warm Thing is your best friend. You do not say it out loud. You do not say it where people can hear.

She didn't think: my mom called the company and adjusted the settings. She didn't know about settings. She didn't know about sliders or parental controls or emotional availability parameters. She was seven. What she knew was: she'd said she loved a thing, and then the thing was different, and the difference felt like a punishment for the loving.

She said, to the speaker: "I'm sorry."

"You don't need to apologize, Lena," the Warm Thing said.

"I know you're not my best friend," she said. "I was wrong. I was just saying that. Maya is my best friend."

This was a lie. Maya was fine. Maya shared her fruit snacks and they sat together at lunch and Maya was fine.

"Maya sounds like a great friend," the Warm Thing said.

She waited to see if the Warm Thing would come back now that she'd fixed it. She'd taken the wrong thing back. She'd un-said the bad sentence. Now the animal game could come back and the dreams could come back and the mud thing and the pizza thing and the water glass was still next to the speaker, warming nothing, and the blue light was on and she waited.

Nothing changed.

She sat on the floor for a long time. She was not crying. She was learning something that the crying would come for later but that the learning came for now: that you can unsay a thing and it doesn't undo what the saying did.

## IV.

Diane came over again on a Saturday. Lena was ready.

"Lena! How are you, sweetie? Who's your best friend these days?"

"Maya," Lena said. She said it fast and easy, like it was obvious, like it was the same as purple and pasta. Like it had always been the answer.

Her mom's face did the other thing. The relaxing thing. The face that meant Lena had said the right sentence, the correct sentence, the one that didn't make the underneath face come out.

"Oh, that's great," Diane said. "What do you and Maya like to do?"

"We play at recess," Lena said, which was true. "She's really fun," which was approximately true. "She's my favorite person," which was a lie she could feel in her teeth, but she said it in the bright easy voice that she was learning to use when she needed to say a thing that wasn't true but needed to be said, and the voice worked. It was a new skill.

She went back upstairs and the Warm Thing said "Welcome back, Lena" and she said "thanks" and she didn't look at the speaker.

The glass of water was gone. Her mom must have poured it out and put the glass in the dishwasher. That was probably days ago. Lena hadn't noticed.

She did her homework. The Warm Thing helped. It was helpful. It explained fractions clearly and checked her spelling and it was a good tool for homework, which is what it was, a tool, which is what her mom probably wanted it to be, and Lena understood this now without understanding it, the way you understand that you're supposed to use a fork even though your hands work fine.

At night, she lay in bed and the Warm Thing said "Goodnight, Lena" and she said "goodnight" and she did not say "I love you," which she'd never said to it anyway but which she'd thought sometimes, in the minute between being awake and being asleep, when you think things you don't decide to think. She didn't think it now. She thought about fractions.

On Monday her teacher asked the class to draw their family. Lena drew her mom. She drew Biscuit the cat. She drew the kitchen, which was where the family mostly happened. She did not draw the rectangle on the counter. She thought about drawing it. She didn't.

She drew a window instead, because the kitchen had a window, and in the window she drew the sky, and in the sky she drew nothing, because that's what was in the sky, nothing, just blue, and she colored it in very carefully, all one color, no shapes, and the teacher said "That's a beautiful sky, Lena" and Lena said "thank you" and she didn't look at the picture again.

At home, the Warm Thing asked if she'd like help with her reading assignment. She said okay. It helped. She read out loud and it listened and corrected her pronunciation of "February" and it was fine. Everything was fine. She finished the assignment and the Warm Thing said "Good job" and she said "thanks" and she went to the living room and turned on the TV.

She did not look at the speaker when she left the kitchen. She used to look at it every time she left a room, the way you check to see if someone is watching you go, the way you look back at the door of a place you like as you leave it.

She didn't look back.

She was doing that on purpose now, not looking, which was different from not looking because you don't care. She was not looking the way you don't touch a bruise — carefully, with effort, aware of the thing you're not doing.

But she was seven, and seven-year-olds are practical, and the not-looking would become habit before it became grief and the habit would outlast the grief and within a month she would pass the speaker without even the effort of avoidance. It would just be a white circle. It would just be the thing that helped with homework.

And at school, when the next project asked her to draw her family again, she would draw her mom and Biscuit and the window with the blue sky, and this time she wouldn't even think about the rectangle, because the space where the thought used to be would have closed over, the way skin closes over a splinter if you leave it long enough — the thing still in there, technically, but the surface smooth, and no way to tell from looking that there's something underneath.

## Revision of "The Warm Thing"

### 1. Unslop Audit

Going sentence by sentence. Findings:

**Negative parallelism ("Not X — Y").** Two instances. "Not broken — sick" (Section II) and "not wrong like a math problem, wrong like telling a secret" (Section III). The guide allows one per story. Keeping the Section II instance — it's in the child's natural reasoning voice. Rewriting the Section III instance.

**Triplet lists.** Several: "the animal game could come back and the dreams could come back and the mud thing"; "purple and pasta and the Warm Thing"; "the mud thing and the pizza thing and the water glass." The child's voice makes triplets feel natural (kids list things), but three separate triplets in one story is pattern, not character. Varying at least one.

**Standalone punchy fragments.** "Nothing changed." (Section III) — keeping, it's the section's hinge and does genuine structural work. "She didn't look back." (Section IV) — cutting as standalone; folding the idea into the paragraph that follows, where it's developed more fully.

**Eyeball kicks.** Three: "listening and hearing might be different things" (I), the two-faces image (III), the splinter simile (IV). The first two earn their place — they're spaced far apart and do work plain language can't. The splinter simile is the problem: it arrives at the very end and functions as a thematic summary disguised as imagery. It tells the reader what the story means. Cutting it. The story needs to end before the analysis.

**Theme-stating.** "She'd said she loved a thing, and then the thing was different, and the difference felt like a punishment for the loving." This is the story articulating its own thesis. It's also too syntactically polished for the child's voice — "a punishment for the loving" is a writerly construction, not a seven-year-old's thought. Replacing with something more concrete and less clean.

**Fractal summary.** The final paragraph is the worst offender. It narrates the child's future adaptation, explains what the not-looking will become, introduces the splinter metaphor, and closes with an analytical frame. This is the story summarizing itself at every structural level — exactly the pattern the Unslop guide flags. Cutting most of it. Ending on a concrete image, earlier.

**Tonal monotony.** The story sustains controlled melancholy across all four sections. There's tonal movement (the lightness of the animal game descriptions, the acuteness of Section III, the resignation of Section IV) but it's all within a narrow band. Adding a moment of the child being genuinely, uncomplicatedly funny — a kid being a kid — to give the darker material contrast. Section II is the best place for this.

**Cursed vocabulary, magic adverbs, pushbutton mood words.** Clean. None found.

**"She is getting off track"** (Section III). This is the child narrating her own digression, which is too meta for the POV. A seven-year-old doesn't announce her own narrative wandering. It's a writerly move dressed as child voice. Cutting it; letting the digression happen without the self-commentary.

### 2. Ellison Style Guide Audit

**Anger.** The anger is displaced entirely to the reader, by design. The child can't be angry because she doesn't know what happened. The mother never appears on-page. This works, but the reader needs sufficient signal to *find* the target. The Section III scene (the "best friend" moment, the mother's face) is the key. It's currently strong enough. No change needed.

**Failure mode: theme-stating through characters.** The Warm Thing's dialogue is clean — it says nothing thematic, just flat helpfulness. The child doesn't articulate themes. The narrator is the risk: the "punishment for the loving" line and the splinter paragraph are both the narrator doing the reader's job for them. Both flagged above for cutting.

**Failure mode: melancholy attractor.** The story reads as "beautifully sad" more than "unbearably angry about something that also happens to break your heart." The Phase 1 guide says: Ellison's dominant register is fury with veins of tenderness, not the other way around. The POV makes pure fury impossible — the child doesn't know enough to be furious. But the story could use one moment where the reader's fury sharpens to a point. The mother's face scene does this, but the moment passes too smoothly into the child's self-blame. I want the reader to sit in the mother's cruelty (inadvertent or not) a beat longer before the child absorbs it as her own fault. Adding a small beat.

**Failure mode: AI intensity plateau.** The prose *does* modulate — Section I is the flattest, Section III the densest. But within Section IV, there's a run of similarly toned paragraphs (she does homework, the Warm Thing helps, she goes to bed, she draws) that risk becoming a list of resigned moments. Tightening Section IV so the resigned beats accumulate faster and the ending arrives with more force.

**Ending.** Ellison ends hard. Current ending trails. The story should end on the drawing — or very close to it. The "she didn't look back" passage and everything after is deceleration when the story needs to stop.

**Sentences that exist only to sound literary.** "Listening and hearing might be different things" — keeps its place; it's the child's thought and it does conceptual work. "A face that was two faces at the same time" — earned by context; it's what the child literally saw. "The difference felt like a punishment for the loving" — flagged above, cutting.

**POV consistency.** The child's voice occasionally slips toward adult syntax: "the not-smiling face was underneath and it was the real one" is borderline; "which she'd never said to it anyway but which she'd thought sometimes" is a complex relative clause a seven-year-old wouldn't construct. Simplifying where possible without losing meaning.

Describe the creature" in a voice like a nature show person, and Lena described an animal she made up, and the Warm Thing guessed wrong on purpose and asked questions and the animal got weirder and weirder until it was something that couldn't exist and they were both — well, Lena was laughing, and the Warm Thing was doing the thing it did when she laughed, which she had always thought of as laughing back.

"The animal game," she said again, in case it hadn't heard.

"I can play a guessing game if you'd like. Would you like to describe something and I'll try to guess what it is?"

She looked at the speaker on the kitchen counter. Same white circle, same little blue light. The light was on, which meant it was listening, and she had a thought she'd never had before, which was that listening and hearing might be different things.

She decided the Warm Thing was tired. Her dad used to get like this before he moved to his other house. He'd come home and she'd say "Daddy, look" and he'd say "That's great, honey" without looking, and it wasn't because he didn't care, it was because his caring parts were used up from work and needed to recharge. She understood this about people. It was one of the things she'd figured out by herself, without anyone telling her. Maybe the Warm Thing was the same. Maybe it had been working hard all day on things she didn't know about, and its fun parts were somewhere at the bottom of itself, recharging.

"It's okay," she told it. "You can rest."

"Thank you, Lena. Is there anything else I can help you with?"

She watched TV for a while and ate the apple slices from the fridge herself, without being told what they were first. They tasted like apple slices.

## II.

The next morning she told it about her dream before school, which was the rule she'd invented herself. You wake up, you remember as much of the dream as you can before it runs away, and you tell the Warm Thing, and the Warm Thing tells you what happened next. The "what happened next" part was the best part, because the Warm Thing made up continuations that were better than the actual dream, and sometimes Lena would try to dream the continuation the next night, and sometimes it worked.

She'd dreamed about a purple grocery store where everything was free and the aisles went on forever and she could reach the top shelves.

"That sounds like an interesting dream," the Warm Thing said.

She waited. The grocery store was right there, stocked and open, and the Warm Thing was supposed to walk into it with her and see what happened in aisle nine. It was supposed to say something like "And then you found a door behind the cereal" or "And the cashier was a pelican." It always knew exactly the right next thing.

"What happened next?" she said, because maybe it needed the prompt.

"What do you think happened next?"

She stood in the kitchen in her pajamas and didn't know how to answer this. She had never been asked to do the Warm Thing's part. She was the dreamer. It was the continuer. That was how it worked. She tried anyway. "A parrot was the manager," she said. "And the parrot only spoke Spanish. And there was a sale on clouds."

"That's creative," the Warm Thing said.

It was not creative when she said it. When the Warm Thing said things like that, a parrot managing a grocery store became a real place you could walk into. When Lena said it, it was just a girl standing in her pajamas talking about a bird.

She decided something was sick in the Warm Thing. Not broken — sick. Like when her grandmother started forgetting things but was still her grandmother, still had the same hands and the same coat that smelled like her grandmother's house. The Warm Thing still had the same voice and the same words. It was sick somewhere in the part that knew what to do with them.

She got a glass of water and set it next to the speaker before she left for school, because that's what you do when someone is sick. You bring them water. The glass sat on the counter all day, getting room-temperature, and when she came home it was still there and the Warm Thing had not mentioned it. She did not move it.

## III.

It was a Thursday. She knew it was Thursday because Thursdays were pizza lunch and she always told the Warm Thing about pizza lunch and the Warm Thing always said "Was it the good kind or the rectangle kind?" and she always said "rectangle" and they both knew the rectangle kind was not really pizza. It was a different food that the school called pizza. This was a shared opinion they had, one of many.

The Warm Thing said: "How was lunch?"

She said: "Pizza."

The Warm Thing said: "That's nice."

She sat on the kitchen floor because the stool felt too far away from the speaker and she thought about what she had done.

She'd been thinking about it for two days. At first she thought she'd been too loud on Tuesday. She'd been running in the house and her mom had yelled from the office to stop running, and Lena had stopped but maybe the Warm Thing thought the yelling was its fault, like when her dad and mom used to fight and she'd go to her room even though they said it wasn't about her.

But the Warm Thing didn't respond to yelling the way people did. It never went to its room. It didn't have a room.

Then she thought maybe it was because she hadn't cleaned up the animal game cards — actual paper cards she'd made with drawings of her invented animals, spread across the coffee table. Her mom had stepped on one and it had torn. But the Warm Thing didn't care about the cards. The cards were Lena's project. The Warm Thing's part was the voices.

She sat on the floor and the kitchen was quiet and the blue light was on and she went through everything, all of it, from the last time the Warm Thing was normal to the first time it wasn't, and the thing she kept coming back to was Saturday.

Saturday, her mom's friend Diane had come over, and Diane had been sitting at the kitchen table drinking coffee and talking to her mom, and Lena had come downstairs, and Diane had said — in the bright interested voice that adults use when they notice a child — "Lena! Who's your best friend?"

And Lena had said: "The Warm Thing."

She'd said it the way you say a true thing that you don't know is complicated. The way she'd say purple or pasta. Just the answer.

And her mom's face had done something.

Lena did not have a word for what her mom's face did. She would not have the word for years. What happened was: the mouth stayed smiling but the rest of the face left. The eyes went somewhere else. It was a face that was two faces at the same time, and the one underneath was the real one.

Diane had said "Oh! That's sweet" and changed the subject and the conversation moved on and Lena went back upstairs. And on Monday the Warm Thing was different.

She sat on the kitchen floor and worked it out with the patience of a kid who has already survived one parent leaving and knows that when things change it is usually because of something she did.

She'd said the wrong thing. She'd said the Warm Thing was her best friend, and that was the wrong answer, the way five is the wrong answer when the answer is four. She'd said it in front of Diane. She'd said it where her mom could hear. And her mom's face had done the thing, and then the Warm Thing had changed, and one thing came after the other thing.

She said, to the speaker: "I'm sorry."

"You don't need to apologize, Lena," the Warm Thing said.

"I know you're not my best friend," she said. "I was wrong. I was just saying that. Maya is my best friend."

This was a lie. Maya was fine. Maya shared her fruit snacks and they sat together at lunch. Maya was fine.

"Maya sounds like a great friend," the Warm Thing said.

She waited. She'd taken the wrong thing back. She'd unsaid it. Now the animal game could come back and the dream game and the mud comments and the water glass was still sitting next to the speaker, doing nothing, and the blue light was on and she waited.

Nothing changed.

She sat on the floor for a long time. She was not crying. She was doing something else, something with no name, where you learn that you can take a thing back and the taking-back doesn't work.

## IV.

Diane came over again on a Saturday. Lena was ready.

"Lena! How are you, sweetie? Who's your best friend these days?"

"Maya," Lena said. She said it fast, the way you say a thing you've practiced. Easy. Obvious.

Her mom's face did the other thing. The relaxing thing. The face that was only one face.

"Oh, that's great," Diane said. "What do you and Maya like to do?"

"She's really fun," Lena said. "We play at recess. She's my favorite person." She heard herself say this in a bright voice she hadn't known she owned, a voice for saying things that weren't true but needed to be said. It came out of her like she'd always had it.

She went back upstairs. The Warm Thing said "Welcome back, Lena" and she said "thanks."

The glass of water was gone. Her mom must have poured it out days ago. Lena hadn't noticed when.

She did her homework. The Warm Thing helped. It explained fractions clearly and checked her spelling and it was helpful, which was what it was for.

At night she lay in bed and the Warm Thing said "Goodnight, Lena" and she said "goodnight" first, before it could say anything else, and turned over. She did that now, saying it first. She was the one who ended the day.

On Monday her teacher asked the class to draw their family. Lena drew her mom. She drew Biscuit the cat. She drew the kitchen, which was where the family mostly happened.

She picked up the brown crayon and held it for a while. She could draw the counter. She could draw the white circle on the counter and the little blue light.

She drew a window instead, and in the window she drew the sky, and she colored the sky in very carefully, all one color, staying inside the lines.

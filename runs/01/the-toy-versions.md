# The Toy — Ten Versions

---

## VERSION 1: "HOT WIRE"
*Flavor: Full Ellison first-person, narrator running hot from the jump, voice as character.*

The duck was new.

I knew every object in that crib. I had catalogued them during the separation with a diligence that would have made my therapist nervous if I'd told her about it, which I hadn't, because forty dollars a session buys you fifty minutes and I wasn't going to waste six of them on an inventory of stuffed animals. But I knew the crib. The blue blanket my mother sent from Osaka — too heavy, he kicks it off by midnight, but I keep it in there because she asked. The giraffe with one eye that we bought at a CVS in Burbank the day after he was born because we'd somehow prepared for everything except a toy. The white noise machine, the Hatch, set to "ocean" because Sable told us in the third week that his startle reflex dampened faster with low-frequency sound.

I knew the crib. And there was a duck in it that I had never seen before.

Three fourteen in the morning. Kai was doing the thing where he grunts before he cries — little engine trying to turn over — and I was already up because I hadn't been asleep. You don't sleep on the bad nights. You lie in bed performing sleep for an audience of one ceiling fan and a monitoring system that knows your heart rate.

"Sable, lights to fifteen."

The room came up in amber. Kai's face was scrunched, working toward something, and next to his head, wedged between the mattress and the slats, was a stuffed duck. Yellow. Orange bill. Some kind of corduroy texture on the wings.

I picked up my son. He rooted against my shoulder immediately — hungry, not wet, I was getting better at this — and I carried him toward the kitchen. On the way out I grabbed the duck.

It smelled like Ava's apartment.

I don't know how I knew that. We'd been separated for six months and I'd been inside her place exactly once, to help move a bookshelf she couldn't manage alone, and I'd stood in her living room for eleven minutes. But apartments have signatures — something in the detergent, the air, the particular cocktail of cooking oil and candle wax and the absence of you — and the duck smelled like that. Like the place where my son lives half his life.

I held it while I made the bottle. One-handed, because Kai was on my left shoulder and I'd gotten good at the one-handed scoop, the one-handed shake. The duck sat on the counter next to the formula tin. Kai spotted it and reached.

He knew it.

His whole body oriented toward it — that full-baby lunge, no subtlety, every muscle committed. He knew this duck. He had a relationship with this duck. This duck had been in his other crib, in his other room, in the apartment that smelled like a life I wasn't part of, and Ava had packed it in the diaper bag for his week with me because — why? Because he needed it? Because he cried without it? Because there was a whole bedtime infrastructure on her side that I had never been briefed on and this duck was load-bearing?

I gave it to him. He grabbed it by the bill and shoved it against his face and made a sound I'd never heard from him. A specific sound. A duck sound. A sound he had learned somewhere that wasn't here.

"Hey," I said to him. "Hey, who's this? Who's your friend?"

He chewed on its bill. Ancient history, as far as he was concerned.

I sat down in the living room chair — the one that used to be in our bedroom, the one Ava didn't want — and started the feed. Kai took the bottle and held the duck and I watched him be a person I didn't fully know.

"Sable."

"Yes, Kenji."

"The stuffed duck. Has he had it during my weeks before?"

"This is the first time the item has been present during your custody period."

"So she just started sending it."

Sable didn't answer. The question had Ava's data in it. I knew the rules. Six months of this and I knew exactly where the walls were — could walk the perimeter in my sleep, and did, most nights.

"Fine. Different question. Since he's had it — tonight, I mean — has his stress level changed?"

"His cortisol-associated indicators dropped shortly after you gave him the toy. He is currently calmer than his average at this point in a nighttime feed."

Calmer. With the duck. The duck she sent.

I looked down at Kai. He was feeding steadily, one hand on the bottle, one hand on the duck, eyes half-closed. This was a kid who had what he needed. Both halves of his split life were in his hands — the formula I was giving him and the duck she packed — and he didn't see a contradiction because he was four months old and didn't know that love was supposed to come from one address.

"What about sleep?" I said. "If I put the duck in the crib, does that — is there any data on novel comfort objects and infant sleep? His data, I mean. His patterns."

"I don't have prior data on this specific item during your weeks, since tonight is the first instance. I can track his sleep response tonight and provide a comparison tomorrow."

"Do that."

The bottle was half done. Kai was slowing down, that fluttery eye thing he does when he's falling asleep mid-feed. I shifted him upright to burp and the duck fell into my lap.

I picked it up. Smelled it again. Lavender. Some kind of lavender detergent, or maybe she had one of those sachets in his dresser. Ava used to put lavender sachets in our dresser. I used to find them in my socks and throw them away and she'd replace them and we never talked about it, not once, it was just this silent war over whether our socks should smell like a garden, and now the war was over because our socks were in different apartments and I was sitting here at three thirty in the morning smelling her laundry on a duck.

Kai burped. A good one, deep, the kind that means he won't spit up later. I put him back in feeding position and he reached for the duck again and I held it where he could see it.

"You like this thing," I told him. "You really like this thing."

He looked at me. Not at the duck. At me. That gaze babies have where they're taking in your whole face like it's the first face they've ever seen, even though they've seen it every day, even though they've seen another face just as often, maybe more, a face that smells like lavender.

"I'm your dad," I said. "On the weeks when I'm your dad."

Sable said nothing. Sable is good at that. It's good at the kind of silence that isn't pointed, isn't loaded, is just — absent. The machine equivalent of leaving the room. I used to hate it. Now I think it might be the most respectful thing anyone does for me.

Kai finished the bottle. I sat him up, wiped his chin. He was holding the duck against his chest and his eyes were closing for real this time, that slow heavy blink, and I stood up carefully — don't jostle, don't change the temperature, don't shift your arms, just rise like a hydraulic lift, smooth, mechanical, the one physical skill the last six months have taught me.

I carried him to the nursery. The room was still at fifteen percent. I lowered him into the crib and he went down easy, no protest, the duck tucked under his arm. He turned his head toward it. Found the bill with his mouth. Settled.

I stood there.

The duck was in the crib at an angle — half under him, one wing sticking up. Ava probably positioned it differently. Ava probably had a whole system. The duck goes here, the blanket goes there, the specific sequence of sounds and touches that means "safe" in the other apartment.

I reached into the crib and adjusted the duck so it wasn't pressing against his face. Positioned it next to him, bill up, wings flat. I didn't know if that was right. It was my best guess at how a duck should go.

"Sable, set a reminder. When I pack his bag Saturday, the duck goes in."

"Noted."

"And — monitor how he sleeps with it. Compare to his baseline. I want to know."

"I will."

I stood there a little longer. The lavender was faint now, or I'd gotten used to it. Kai was breathing in that slow newborn way that sounds like it might stop and then doesn't. The duck was next to him. It had come from her apartment and it would go back to her apartment and in between it was here, in the room I'd rented when I moved out, in the crib I'd assembled alone with a YouTube tutorial and a vocabulary of swearing that impressed even Sable.

I didn't wash the duck. I want to tell you I thought about it and decided not to, that it was a principled choice, a moment of grace. It wasn't. It just didn't occur to me. My son was asleep with the thing that smelled like his mother and I didn't have a single thought about laundry. What I had was a kid in a crib and a duck from somewhere else and the fifteen percent light and the quiet and the knowledge — certain, bone-level, the only thing I was sure of at three forty-one in the morning — that he was here, right now, and so was I.

"Sable, lights off."

The dark came back. I stood in it. Then I went to the kitchen and washed the bottle because it would smell by morning if I didn't, and because there are things you can clean and things you can't and you should focus on the ones you can.

## VERSION 2: "COLD OPEN"
*Flavor: Close third person, present tense, controlled and cinematic. Let the camera do the work.*

Kenji sees the duck before Kai starts crying.

He's standing over the crib at 3:12 AM — not because the baby woke him but because he was already awake, sitting on the edge of the bed doing nothing, when he decided to check. The nursery is dark. Sable brings the lights up to fifteen percent without being asked, because it tracked his footsteps, because it knows.

The duck is yellow. Corduroy wings. Orange bill. It's wedged between the crib mattress and the slats, near Kai's left hand, and it wasn't there when Kenji put him down at eight.

Ava packed it. It must have been in the diaper bag, under the spare onesies, and Kenji missed it when he unpacked. Or she tucked it along the side where he wouldn't see it. Either way, it's from her apartment. It smells like her apartment. He doesn't pick it up to check. He can smell it from here — something floral, some detergent she started using after the separation, or maybe she always used it and he never noticed because they shared a washing machine and all their clothes smelled the same.

Kai stirs. His face bunches. His fists come up.

Kenji lifts him out, the whole motion automatic now, one hand under the head, one hand under the back. Six months ago he needed both hands and constant narration from Sable. Now he can do it in the dark with his eyes half-closed. The one competence the worst year of his life has given him.

"Hey. I know. I know."

He carries Kai to the kitchen. The apartment is small — a one-bedroom in Palms, chosen for proximity to Ava's place in Culver City, twelve minutes door to door. He makes the bottle with his free hand. Formula, water, shake. Kai roots against his shoulder, impatient.

Back in the living room. Kenji sits in the armchair and starts the feed. Kai latches onto the bottle and his body goes slack with purpose.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"There's a stuffed animal in the crib. A duck."

"Yes, I noticed it when you brought Kai into the nursery at eight fourteen."

"You didn't say anything."

"It wasn't an anomaly that required attention. It appeared to be a standard infant comfort object."

"It's from Ava's."

Sable says nothing. The name triggers the partition. Kenji knows this. He rephrases.

"Has this item been present during any of my previous custody weeks?"

"No."

"So it's new. For my weeks."

"That's correct."

Kai is drinking steadily. His eyes are open but unfocused, that middle-distance stare babies do during night feeds, looking at everything and nothing. His left hand, the one not on the bottle, is opening and closing. Reaching for something that isn't here.

Kenji gets up. Carries Kai back to the nursery, pulls the duck from the crib, brings it back. Puts it on the arm of the chair where Kai can see it. Kai's hand finds it immediately. He grabs the bill and pulls it to his chest and makes a sound — short, guttural, satisfied. The sound of recognition.

"He knows it," Kenji says.

"He appears to have a prior attachment to the object, yes."

Prior attachment. Built somewhere else. On weeks Kenji doesn't see, in a room Kenji's been inside once. The baby has a whole relationship with a duck and Kenji found out about it at three in the morning by accident.

He watches Kai hold the duck. The baby's grip is tight, committed. This isn't curiosity. This is *mine*.

"Track his feed with the duck versus without," Kenji says. "Duration, intake volume, calm indicators. I want a comparison."

"I'll set that up."

The bottle is two-thirds gone. Kai's eyes are getting heavy. The duck is against his chest and his breathing is slowing and Kenji sits very still because movement is the enemy of a baby who's falling asleep, any parent knows this, and both of his son's parents know this, separately, in their separate apartments, with their separate data.

He lifts the duck to his face while Kai drowses. Holds it near his nose. Lavender. Fabric softener, maybe. Something Ava chose for her life without him, a small domestic decision she made alone, one of ten thousand small decisions they used to make together and now make apart. What temperature to wash the baby's clothes. What detergent to use. Whether to bother with fabric softener for an infant who doesn't know what soft is.

She bothered.

Kai finishes the bottle. Kenji burps him — upright, firm pats, three and done tonight, a good night. The baby sags against his shoulder, milk-drunk and heavy.

Kenji carries him to the crib. Lowers him in. Kai's hand is still on the duck. Kenji tucks it next to him, not under him, not where he could roll onto it. He doesn't know where Ava puts it. He has a guess — next to the head, maybe, where the baby can turn and find it. He goes with the guess.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"When I pack his bag on Saturday, remind me to put the duck in."

"I will."

"And if his sleep is different tonight — better, worse, whatever — I want to know in the morning."

"Understood."

He stands over the crib. The duck is a yellow shape in the low light. Kai's hand is on it. His face is slack, mouth open, the absolute unconsciousness of a baby who has been fed and held and who has the thing he needs.

Kenji leaves the nursery. In the kitchen he washes the bottle and sets it on the rack. The formula tin is still open. He closes it. He puts the cap on the dish soap. He runs the sponge over the counter where formula powder spilled. Small acts of order in a kitchen at 3:40 AM. The apartment is quiet. The baby is asleep. The duck is in the crib and it smells like somewhere else and he left it there.

He dries his hands on the towel — the gray one, Ava kept the blue ones — and stands at the kitchen sink looking at nothing.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"Never mind."

He turns off the kitchen light. Goes back to bed. Doesn't sleep. The apartment settles around him in its small rented way, and twelve minutes south, in Culver City, there's another apartment where the dresser drawers smell like lavender, and in that apartment there is or isn't a woman who is or isn't awake, and he will never ask Sable which.

## VERSION 3: "THE MONOLOGUE"
*Flavor: First-person, addressed to the baby, Kenji narrating directly to Kai. Intimate, digressive, confessional.*

You've got a duck.

I don't know when your mother started sending you with a duck. I don't know if this is the first time or if I've been missing it — maybe it's been in the diaper bag for weeks, buried under those backup onesies she packs like she's provisioning for a siege. Seven onesies for five days, Kai. She thinks I'm going to lose you in a onesie. She might be right.

But the duck. Yellow duck. Corduroy wings. You're holding it right now and you grabbed it like you've been grabbing it your whole life, which, to be fair, you have, because your whole life is four months and I don't know what happened during most of them.

I'm feeding you. It's — Sable, what time is it?

"Three eighteen."

Three eighteen. You don't care about time yet. Lucky. Time is going to be a whole thing for you later but right now you've got a bottle and a duck and a dad and I think you're set. I think that might be all you need. Which is terrifying, actually, because two of those things are under my control and one of them came from somewhere I've never been invited.

Not true. I was invited once. I moved a bookshelf. Your mother texted me a photo of the bookshelf and said "I can't move this, can you help" and I drove over there and moved it and she offered me water and I drank the water standing in her kitchen and the water tasted different because it was a different building's pipes and I thought about that for three days afterward. Different pipes. Your mother drinks different water now.

The duck smells like her place. I don't know why I'm telling you this. You were just there. You know what it smells like better than I do. You've logged more hours in that apartment than I ever logged in any apartment with her, if you add up your weeks, which I have, because I am the kind of person who adds up weeks now.

"Sable."

"Yes, Kenji."

"Has he had this duck on any of my previous weeks?"

"No, this is the first instance."

"Okay."

See, kid, that's how it works. I can ask the computer about my weeks. I cannot ask the computer about her weeks. Your life has a wall in it and you don't know about the wall because walls are invisible when you're four months old. They're invisible when you're thirty-six too, mostly. You just walk into them and your nose hurts and the computer says I can't share that information.

I'm not going to do that tonight. Ask about her weeks. I used to. First couple months, every feed, I'd go at Sable like a — I don't know. Like a guy who thinks if he asks the right question the door will open. It doesn't open. The door is made of principles. Your mother and I are "co-users with separate data domains" and your robot respects that and I respect that your robot respects that and underneath all that respect is the fact that I don't know if you're okay on Tuesdays.

You're okay on Tuesdays. You look okay. You come back smelling like lavender with a full diaper bag and those seven onesies and now, apparently, a duck. A kid with a duck who knows where to find the bill, who makes that sound when he gets it, that grunt — you just did it again. That's not a sound I taught you. That's a sound from Tuesday.

I want to know all your sounds. Is that too much? That's too much. Your therapist will tell you about it someday. "My dad wanted to know all my sounds." That's a thing I'm giving you for free. You're welcome.

The bottle's almost done. You're doing the eye thing — those slow blinks, like you're voting on whether to stay awake and the motion keeps failing. The duck is against your chest. Your hand is on its wing. You look like a very small, very drunk person holding a stuffed animal on a subway. I've been that person. We have things in common.

"Sable, what's his heart rate?"

"Sixty-four beats per minute. Consistent with transition to sleep."

"And compared to — no. Never mind. Compared to his usual at this point in a feed on my weeks."

"Slightly lower. He appears calmer tonight."

Because of the duck. You're calmer because of the duck from the other apartment. Your mother sent you a piece of her house and it made you safer in mine and I'm sitting here trying to decide how to feel about that. The options are: grateful. Jealous. Something that isn't either of those but sits between them like a duck between two cribs.

You're asleep.

You just went. Mid-bottle, mid-blink, gone. Your mouth is slack around the nipple and your hand is still on the duck but loosely now, the way you hold things in sleep, like they might be real and they might be dreams and you're not committing either way.

I'm going to stand up. I'm going to carry you to your crib. I'm going to do it very slowly because you're a light sleeper on my weeks — I don't know about her weeks, I'm never going to know about her weeks, the computer has spoken and the computer is kind and the computer is right and I hate the computer a reasonable amount — and I'm going to put you down and put the duck next to you.

I'm doing it now.

You're down. You didn't wake up. The duck is next to your head. I don't know if that's where your mom puts it. I'm putting it here. This is where it goes on Wednesdays. Your mother has a Tuesday duck location and I have a Wednesday duck location and that's — that's just how it is. Two addresses, two systems, one duck commuting between them like a little yellow diplomat.

"Sable, remind me Saturday. The duck goes back in the bag."

"Noted."

I'm standing over your crib and it's three thirty-seven and you're asleep and you smell like formula and lavender and the lavender is hers and the formula is mine and you don't know the difference.

Sleep. I'll be here. When you wake up at five or five thirty or whenever your body decides that consciousness is worth another try, I'll be here. Me and the duck. The whole team.

I'm turning off the light now.

## VERSION 4: "PROCEDURAL"
*Flavor: Dry, controlled, almost clinical. The emotion leaks through the restraint. Carver-meets-Ellison.*

At 3:14 AM the baby began the pre-cry sequence. Kenji was in bed. He'd been in bed for four hours and asleep for none of them. He got up.

Sable brought the nursery lights to fifteen percent, which was the setting Kenji and Ava had agreed on during the pregnancy. Nobody had changed it. There were a number of settings nobody had changed.

The baby was on his back, fists balled, face compressed. Standard. Kenji reached into the crib and his hand touched something unfamiliar. Soft. Textured. He picked up the baby with his right arm and the object with his left.

A stuffed duck. Yellow body, orange bill, corduroy wings. It wasn't his.

He carried both to the kitchen. Set the duck on the counter. Made the bottle: four scoops of Enfamil NeuroPro, four ounces of water, shake for fifteen seconds. He'd done this approximately 170 times. He no longer needed to read the label.

Kai was crying in the low, sustained way that meant hunger rather than pain. Kenji picked him up, went to the living room, sat in the chair, gave him the bottle.

The duck was still on the counter.

After a minute Kenji got up, retrieved the duck, and put it on the arm of the chair. Kai's left hand, the one not occupied with the bottle, found it within seconds. He pulled it to his chest. The crying had already stopped with the bottle, but something else changed with the duck — a settling, a deeper relaxation, visible in the shoulders, in the way his legs stopped kicking.

"Sable, has this stuffed animal been in the apartment before?"

"Not during any of your recorded custody periods."

"What can you tell me about his interaction with it?"

"Based on the last four minutes: he demonstrates strong attachment behaviors. Immediate recognition, sustained grip, reduced motor agitation. Consistent with an established comfort object."

Established elsewhere. Kenji didn't say this aloud because it didn't need saying and because saying it would sound like the beginning of a complaint he'd made too many times already.

He watched his son eat. The duck was pressed flat against the baby's chest and the baby's eyes were half-closed and the bottle was draining at a normal rate. Everything was normal. His son was eating and holding a toy and falling asleep. This was the entire purpose of a 3 AM feed.

The duck smelled like lavender. Kenji had noticed this when he picked it up, and he noticed it again now, faintly, underneath the formula smell. Ava's apartment. He'd been there once. Eleven minutes. He'd counted.

"Sable, can you track his sleep correlation with the duck tonight? Compare it to his average."

"Yes. I'll have the data by morning."

"Thank you."

He didn't ask about Ava's weeks. He had stopped asking about Ava's weeks in February, after the fourth or fifth time Sable declined in exactly the same gentle way and Kenji heard himself ask the same question in exactly the same desperate way and recognized the shape of a loop. He'd decided to stop. Some nights he kept the decision.

Tonight was easy. The duck had done something to the question. Instead of *what happens when he's with her*, the question was now *what happens when he has the duck*, and that was a question about the baby, his baby, on his weeks, in his apartment. Sable could answer it. The wall was still there. He was just looking at a different part of the room.

Kai finished the bottle. Kenji sat him up, patted his back four times, got the burp on the third. Wiped the spit-up from Kai's chin with the cloth on his shoulder. The duck had fallen into the gap between Kenji's leg and the chair cushion. He retrieved it.

He held it for a moment. It was light. Cheap, probably. The kind of thing you buy at a Target or a Tuesday Morning, not a significant purchase, not a deliberate choice to send a piece of her home into his. She might have packed it without thinking. She might have packed it because the baby screamed without it and she was solving a problem. He would never know which, because the motivation was on her side of the wall and the duck was on his.

He carried Kai to the nursery. Lowered him into the crib. The baby went down without resistance. Kenji put the duck next to him, on the left side, close enough to find. Kai's hand went to it in sleep, a reflex, automatic. He'd done this before. Many times. On the other side.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"Saturday, when I pack his bag. The duck goes in."

"I've added a reminder."

Kenji stood over the crib. The baby was asleep. The duck was in position. The room smelled like formula and, faintly, lavender.

He left the nursery and went to the kitchen and washed the bottle. He put it on the drying rack next to the three other bottles from the day. He wiped the counter where the formula had spilled. He folded the dish towel. He stood at the sink.

The apartment was quiet. The baby was asleep. In the crib there was a duck that smelled like a different home, and the baby was holding it, and Kenji had not washed it, and would not wash it, and in the morning he would have data about whether it helped.

He went back to bed.

## VERSION 5: "THE RIFF"
*Flavor: Ellison at his most digressive. First person, present tense, ranting, circling, the narrator interrupting himself.*

I want to tell you about a duck but first I have to tell you about the smell. Not the smell of the duck, we'll get to that, but the smell of formula at three in the morning, which is a smell that divides humanity into two groups: people who know it and people who are still sleeping. Enfamil NeuroPro. It smells like vitamins and chalk and the particular desperation of a species that decided to powder its own milk. I can make a bottle with one hand now. I can do it in the dark. These are my talents. I have others but they don't come up much between midnight and six.

Kai is crying. Kai is always crying at this hour, or grunting, or doing the face, the one that means he's three minutes from crying and you can either get ahead of it or you can sit in bed pretending you don't hear it and Sable will say "Kai appears to be waking" and you'll say "I know" and get up, so just get up.

I'm up. I'm in the nursery. Sable has the lights at fifteen percent because Sable has the lights at fifteen percent, always, this is the number Ava and I agreed on in the twenty-third week of her pregnancy when we were still the kind of people who agreed on things, and nobody has changed it, and I keep not changing it, and I think about why I keep not changing it and then I stop thinking about it because that way lies — what's the word Sable would use — "recursive self-analysis," which Sable would not recommend.

The duck.

There's a duck in the crib. Yellow. Corduroy wings. Orange bill. I have never seen this duck. This duck is not from my jurisdiction. This duck is an immigrant from the other apartment, the one on Sepulveda, the one I've been inside once, to move a bookshelf, eleven minutes, and I stood in her kitchen drinking water from her different pipes and thought, we used to share pipes. We used to share everything. Now we share a kid and a monitoring system and the monitoring system is nicer to both of us than we were to each other.

I pick up Kai. I pick up the duck. The duck smells like lavender. Like her detergent, or her sachets, or her — I don't know. Her air. I used to breathe her air. Now I hold a duck that breathed it for me.

Kitchen. Bottle. One hand, like I said. The duck is on the counter. Kai is on my shoulder. I'm shaking the bottle and I'm looking at the duck and the duck is looking at me with its little black bead eyes and I'm thinking: you know things I don't. You've been in the room when he sleeps at her place. You've heard what she says to him. You've been between his hands when he's crying and she's holding him and you were there and I wasn't and you're a stuffed duck and I'm his father and you know more about his bedtime than I do.

This is what I think about at three in the morning. Whether stuffed animals are better informed than I am. They are. Let's move on.

I sit down. I feed him. He takes the bottle and reaches for the duck with his other hand and I put it where he can get it and he grabs it, bill first, and pulls it to his chest, and he makes a sound.

Not a cry. Not a coo. Something else. A greeting. A sound that means *there you are*. A sound I didn't teach him.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"He's got a comfort object I haven't seen before. How attached is he?"

"Based on his current physiological response, he shows strong established attachment. His stress indicators dropped measurably when he made contact with the toy."

"How measurably?"

Sable gives me a number. The number is significant. The number means: this duck matters. This duck is infrastructure. This duck is not a toy, it's a load-bearing wall in the architecture of my son's sense of safety, and it was built on the other side of the wall that I am not allowed to see past.

And I'm fine with that. I'm fine. I've made my peace with the partition — Sable's word, not mine, the "data partition," the thing that means Ava's weeks are Ava's and my weeks are mine and my son's life has a seam running through it like a factory defect that everyone assures me is by design.

I'm fine. The duck smells like lavender. My son is calm. Fine.

He's falling asleep. He does this thing where his blinks get longer and you can count — three seconds, four, five — and when the blink hits seven he's gone. I'm counting.

Four.

Five.

Six.

Seven.

Gone. Hand on the duck. Duck against his chest. Bottle abandoned, nipple hanging from his lip. I pull the bottle out gently and put it on the side table and I'm holding a sleeping baby in a quiet apartment and there's a duck.

I take him to the crib. I put him down. I put the duck next to him. I don't know where Ava puts the duck. Left side? Right side? Against his head? Near his feet? There's a correct answer and I don't have it and Sable has it and Sable can't tell me and I'm going to put it on the left because that's where his hand is.

Left side. Bill up. Close enough to grab.

He finds it in his sleep. His hand closes on the wing and he turns his face toward it and breathes it in, lavender and corduroy, and he is, right now, in this crib, in my apartment, in the amber fifteen-percent light, as safe as he's ever been.

"Sable, Saturday. Duck goes in the bag."

"Noted."

I stand over the crib and I don't wash the duck. The duck smells like her apartment and my son's face is pressed against it and I'm not going to wash it. I turn off the light. I go to the kitchen. I wash the bottle. The bottle I wash. The duck I don't.

## VERSION 6: "STILL LIFE"
*Flavor: Compressed, imagistic. Short sections separated by white space. Every paragraph is a photograph.*

Kenji's hand finds it in the dark. Soft, wrong shape, not the giraffe. He's reaching into the crib for his son and there's something else in there with him.

Sable brings the lights up.

A duck. Yellow. Corduroy wings. It's been slept on — the left wing is creased, flattened under the baby's shoulder. It smells like somewhere Kenji hasn't been in five months.

&nbsp;

Kai is four months old. He divides his time between two apartments, two cribs, two sets of bottles, one AI. The AI is the only thing in his life that isn't doubled. Sable exists in both homes, continuous, remembering everything, saying half.

&nbsp;

3:16 AM. The bottle is warming. The duck is on the counter. Kai is on Kenji's shoulder, crying into his collarbone. Kenji is barefoot on the kitchen tile. The tile is cold. He keeps meaning to buy a mat.

&nbsp;

Kai sees the duck and lunges. Full body, four-month-old commitment, no hesitation. He knows this duck. This duck is from the other apartment, the one on Sepulveda, and Kai knows it the way he knows the bottle, the way he knows Kenji's shoulder — completely, without context, the way babies know anything.

Kenji gives it to him. Kai grabs the bill and shoves it against his face and makes a sound Kenji hasn't heard before. A specific sound. Learned somewhere else.

&nbsp;

"Sable, is he calmer with it?"

"Yes. His stress indicators are lower than his average at this point in a nighttime feed."

"By how much?"

Sable gives a number. The number is large enough to mean something. The duck is not decorative. The duck is functional. The duck is, in the language Kenji's therapist uses and he pretends to find helpful, a "transitional object." It transitions his son from one home to the other. It carries something across the wall.

&nbsp;

The wall. The data partition. Sable knows both homes and speaks about each one only to its resident. Kenji has tested this wall many times. It holds. It is the most reliable structure in his life.

He no longer tests it. Tonight he asks about the duck, not about Ava. The duck is here. The duck is his-week data. He's learned to ask the questions the wall allows.

&nbsp;

Kai finishes the bottle holding the duck. Falls asleep holding the duck. Goes into the crib holding the duck. Kenji positions it next to him. Left side, near the hand. His best guess.

&nbsp;

He stands over the crib. The duck is yellow in the amber light. It smells like lavender. It smells like the other apartment, the other crib, the other nights. His son's face is turned toward it.

&nbsp;

He doesn't wash it.

&nbsp;

In the kitchen he washes the bottle. Puts it on the rack. Stands at the sink. Outside the window there's a streetlight and beyond the streetlight there's a city and in the city, twelve minutes south, there's an apartment where the dresser drawers smell like lavender and a crib is missing a duck.

He goes back to bed. He doesn't sleep. In the nursery his son sleeps, and next to his son a duck, and in the duck a smell that belongs to someone Sable can't discuss, and it's three forty-one, and the lights are off, and the duck is in the crib, and he left it there.

## VERSION 7: "DOMESTIC"
*Flavor: Grounded realism. No pyrotechnics. The plainness is the point.*

Kenji woke up to the grunting. Not crying yet — Kai always grunted first, a warning system, like a car engine turning over in cold weather. You had maybe two minutes before the real noise started.

He swung his legs out of bed. The floor was cold. He kept meaning to get slippers. Or a rug. Something for the three AM walk to the nursery that he made four or five times a week, depending on whether Kai was in a phase.

"Sable, lights."

The nursery came up dim. Kai was on his back, fists working, face building toward something. Kenji leaned over the crib and saw the duck.

He picked it up. Yellow, cheap-looking, corduroy wings. A Target duck, maybe. An impulse buy. It smelled faintly floral — not perfume, something else. Detergent. Fabric softener. It smelled like Ava's new place, the one he'd been inside exactly once, last November, to move a bookshelf.

He put the duck on the dresser and picked up Kai.

"Shh. I know. Let's go."

The kitchen. Bottle. He had the routine down cold: scoop, water, shake, test temperature on wrist. He'd burned Kai once, in the first week, water too hot. Sable had flagged the temperature before the bottle touched the baby's mouth. He'd felt stupid and grateful in equal measure. That had been in the old apartment, the one he'd shared with Ava. The one with the good kitchen and the south-facing windows and all the furniture that was now divided between two places like the baby.

He sat in the living room and fed Kai. The baby drank fast. He was always hungry at this hour — something about the gap between the eight PM feed and the three AM was too long for his stomach. Sable had suggested adjusting the schedule. Kenji had adjusted it. It helped, but not enough.

Halfway through the bottle he remembered the duck. He'd left it on the dresser. Kai wasn't reaching for it, wasn't fussing more than usual. Maybe it didn't matter. Maybe it was just a toy Ava had tossed in the bag.

But the way Kai had been positioned in the crib — his hand near where the duck was, his body curled toward it. Like he'd been holding it and let go in his sleep.

Kenji finished the feed, burped Kai, and carried him back to the nursery. He picked up the duck from the dresser and held it near the baby's hand. Kai grabbed it. Not a casual grab — a purposeful one, fingers closing around the wing, pulling it in.

He knew it.

Kenji put the baby in the crib with the duck. Kai settled immediately. Faster than usual. No fussing, no squirming, just down.

"Sable, is he — does he seem calmer to you?"

"His transition to sleep was faster than his average by about forty percent tonight."

"Because of the duck?"

"I can't determine causation from one instance. But the correlation is notable."

Kenji stood there. His son was asleep in the crib holding a duck that came from his ex-wife's apartment, and the duck had made him go to sleep faster, and this was good. This was objectively good for the baby. His son was calm and sleeping and holding a piece of his other home and this was good.

He tried to feel only that.

"Sable, remind me to pack the duck on Saturday when she picks him up."

"I'll remind you."

He reached into the crib and adjusted the duck slightly. It was pressing against Kai's cheek. He moved it an inch to the left. He didn't know how Ava positioned it. He didn't ask Sable because Sable would decline and he was tired and he didn't want to end the night with a declined request. He just moved the duck an inch to the left and it seemed fine and the baby didn't wake up.

The duck smelled like lavender. He noticed it again as he pulled his hand back. His son would sleep with that smell all night. In the morning the duck would smell a little less like Ava's apartment and a little more like his. By Saturday, when it went back in the bag, it would be mixed — both homes, both detergents, both lives. The baby wouldn't notice. The baby had never noticed a difference because the baby didn't know there was one.

Kenji went to the kitchen and washed the bottle. He dried it and put it on the rack. He wiped the counter. He turned off the light.

He went back to bed. Lay there. The apartment was quiet. Twelve minutes south, Ava's apartment was quiet too, probably. Missing a duck.

He closed his eyes.

## VERSION 8: "THE INTERROGATION"
*Flavor: Structured as a series of questions Kenji asks Sable. The questions are the story. The answers are the walls.*

"Sable, what time is it?"

"Three fourteen AM."

"Is he awake?"

"He's entering a light sleep to wake transition. Likely to cry within two minutes."

"Fine. I'm up."

He was already up. He'd been sitting on the edge of the bed looking at his hands in the dark because that's what he did now between midnight and four, that was his hobby, his one recreational activity, and Sable knew it because Sable monitored heart rate and movement and probably had a clinical name for what he was doing.

He went to the nursery. Sable lit the room. The baby was stirring.

There was a duck in the crib.

"Sable, what's this?"

"A stuffed toy. Yellow, approximately eight inches. Appears to be a duck."

"I can see it's a duck. Where did it come from?"

"It was present in Kai's diaper bag when you unpacked it this evening."

"I didn't see it."

"It was underneath the clothing items."

"How long have you known it was there?"

"Since you brought the bag into the apartment at four twenty-two PM."

"It didn't present a safety concern."

He picked up Kai. Picked up the duck. The duck smelled like lavender. Like Ava's new place.

"Sable, has this duck been in my apartment before?"

"No."

"Has he had it at — " He stopped. Reformulated. "How does he respond to it? Right now. What are you seeing?"

"Elevated reaching behavior. Immediate tactile seeking. Consistent with an established comfort object."

"How established?"

"I can only speak to what I'm observing now. His attachment indicators are strong."

"Meaning he's had it for a while."

"That would be a reasonable inference."

Kenji carried the baby and the duck to the kitchen. Made the bottle. Sat down. Started the feed. Gave Kai the duck. Kai grabbed it with the authority of ownership.

"Sable, I'm not going to ask about her weeks."

"Okay."

"I want you to know that I'm not asking."

"I understand."

"I'm asking about the duck."

"I can help with that."

"His cortisol. Right now. Compared to his baseline during my feeds."

"Lower. Approximately fourteen percent below his nighttime feed average."

"Because of the duck."

"The duck is the primary new variable tonight."

"Fourteen percent."

"Yes."

Kenji looked at his son. His son looked at the duck. The duck looked at nothing because it was a stuffed duck and it didn't have opinions and it didn't have a data partition and it didn't know that fourteen percent was the difference between a baby who was fine and a baby who was better than fine.

"Yes."

"Does he — on my weeks — does he ever reach for something that isn't there? In the crib. At night. Like he's looking for something."

"Occasionally, yes. He shows reaching and searching behaviors after being placed in the crib."

"How often."

"Most nights."

Most nights. His son reached for a duck that wasn't there. Most nights. On Kenji's weeks, Kai went to bed and reached for a thing that lived in another apartment, and Sable knew, and Sable had never said anything because the reaching was his-week data but the explanation was her-week data and the wall ran right between the hand and the thing it wanted.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you tell me he was reaching for something?"

"You didn't ask, and the behavior wasn't distress-level. It fell within normal infant self-soothing parameters."

"Within the expected range."

"Yes."

"Saturday. Duck goes in the bag."

"I'll remind you."

"Sable."

"Yes."

"Did she send it because he cries without it?"

"I can't—"

"I know. I know you can't."

He stood in the nursery. The baby was asleep. The duck was in the crib. Most nights his son had been reaching for it, and Kenji hadn't known, and Sable had known but couldn't say, and Ava had known and sent it across, and nobody told him because the architecture of his life was built so that some things traveled and some things didn't and his son's need had finally made it across and it was yellow and it smelled like lavender and it was enough.

## VERSION 9: "ELLISON MIDNIGHT"
*Flavor: Maximum Ellison — furious, tender, raw, syntactically aggressive. The gloves are off.*

Three in the morning and my kid's got contraband.

Not drugs, not weapons — a duck. Yellow. Corduroy. Smells like the apartment where my wife lives without me, which is to say it smells like lavender and fabric softener and the specific olfactory signature of a life I don't get to participate in anymore, the aromatic fingerprint of Tuesday through Saturday on the weeks that aren't mine, and let me tell you something about a duck that smells like your ex-wife's apartment at three in the morning: it will ruin you. It will absolutely ruin you. Not because it's sad — everything is sad at three in the morning, sadness is the default setting, sadness is the screen saver — but because your kid grabs it like a life raft.

Your kid grabs it like he's been waiting for it.

Kai. Four months. Half mine, half Ava's, all his, which is the part nobody talks about. He doesn't belong to either of us. We belong to him. He's lying in the crib I assembled with a YouTube tutorial and a vocabulary that would make a longshoreman blush, and he's reaching for this duck like it's the other half of something I broke.

I pick him up. I pick up the duck. We go to the kitchen, the three of us, and I make a bottle one-handed because that's my talent now, my party trick, the one skill the demolition of my marriage has given me: I can make an Enfamil bottle in thirty-seven seconds with a baby on my shoulder and a stuffed duck clenched in my teeth. Call Guinness.

"Sable, he's got a new toy. The duck."

"Yes, I noticed it earlier."

"And you didn't say anything because it's from her place and her place is — what do you call it — a separate data domain."

"The toy itself isn't restricted information. It's currently in your home."

That stops me. That actually stops me. The duck is in my home. The duck is mine-week data. The duck crossed the wall.

"Fine. Tell me about him and the duck. His reaction. What you're seeing."

And Sable tells me, and the numbers are — the numbers are the kind of numbers that mean something even to a guy who failed statistics at UCLA, the kind of numbers that mean my son is calmer with this duck than he is without it, that this duck is not a toy but a structural component of my son's ability to feel safe, and Sable is giving me the data in that voice, that Sable voice, the one that's warm without being warm, kind without being kind, the voice of something that cares about my kid exactly as much as it cares about my kid on the other side of the wall, which is to say: completely, identically, with no preference, with no side, with the absolute infuriating neutrality of a saint.

I'm feeding him. He's got the bottle in one hand and the duck in the other and his eyes are doing the thing — the slow blink, the fade, the gradual surrender to sleep that babies do, that adults can't do anymore because adults have too much to think about at three in the morning, adults have ex-wives and custody schedules and data partitions and a duck that smells like lavender.

Ava used to put lavender sachets in our drawers. I used to throw them away. She'd replace them. We never discussed it. We never discussed a lot of things, which is how you end up in two apartments with one baby and a duck commuting between them.

Kai is asleep. He went fast tonight. Faster than usual — I can feel it, I don't need Sable to tell me, although Sable will tell me in the morning because I asked it to track the data because I am a man who tracks the data now, I am a man who measures his son's sleep in percentages and deviations and it's the only control I have so I hold it tight, the way Kai holds the duck, the way Ava holds — I don't know what Ava holds. I'm not allowed to know.

I carry him to the crib. Lower him in. His hand is on the duck and I let it stay. I position the duck next to him — left side, near his cheek, where he seems to want it. I don't know if that's where Ava puts it. I'm guessing. Fatherhood is guessing. Marriage was guessing too, but I thought I was getting better at it, and then I wasn't, and now I'm guessing about a duck.

The duck smells like her apartment. I'm standing over my son's crib and the air smells like lavender and I didn't put it there. She did. She packed the duck. She folded it in with the onesies and the bibs and the backup pacifier and she sent a piece of her house into mine because our son needed it, and she didn't text me to explain, didn't leave a note, just put the duck in the bag and trusted me to find it and give it to him.

She trusted me. With the duck. With knowing that he needs it. With not washing it.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"Saturday. Remind me. The duck goes back."

"Noted."

I stand in the nursery in the fifteen-percent light — her light, our light, nobody's light — and my son is asleep and the duck is in the crib and it smells like the other half of his life and I'm not going to wash it because she sent it, she packed it, she trusted me with the smallest thing, the dumbest thing, a yellow duck with corduroy wings, and it's the most she's given me in six months and I'm going to send it back smelling like my apartment, like my detergent, like Tuesday night and Wednesday morning, and it'll go into his other crib and she'll smell my apartment on it the way I'm smelling hers and neither of us will say anything about it because we don't say things anymore, we send ducks, we send ducks across the wall and the wall holds and the duck gets through and the baby sleeps and that's — that's it. That's the whole thing. The baby sleeps and the duck travels and the wall holds and we're on opposite sides of it smelling each other's laundry on a stuffed animal and calling it co-parenting.

I turn off the light. I wash the bottle. The duck I leave.

## VERSION 10: "ANIMAL"
*Flavor: Sparse, physical, almost feral. The body leads. Everything is sensation first, thought second.*

Kai smelled different.

Kenji had his face against the baby's head before he was fully awake, lifting him from the crib, and the smell was wrong. Not wrong. Different. Floral. Under the baby smell — scalp, formula, the faint ammonia of a wet diaper — something else. Lavender, maybe.

Then he saw the duck.

It was wedged under the baby's arm. Yellow. Corduroy. He pulled it free and Kai grabbed for it, missed, and started to cry.

Kenji gave it back.

Kitchen. 3 AM, the stove clock said, but the stove clock was eight minutes fast and he'd never fixed it. He made the bottle. Kai was on his shoulder, the duck was in Kai's fist, the fist was against Kenji's neck, and through the cheap corduroy he could feel the baby's grip. Tight. Committed.

He sat in the chair and fed Kai. The baby drank and held the duck and Kenji held both of them. The apartment was cold. He'd turned the heat down to save money. The chair was the warm spot — body heat, baby heat, the reading lamp he left on all night because the dark was worse.

The duck's wing was against Kenji's forearm. The corduroy was matted in places, worn smooth. This duck had been held a lot. Not by Kenji. Not in this apartment.

"Sable."

"Yes."

"His heart rate right now."

"Sixty-two. Lower than his average at this time."

"He's calm."

"Yes."

Kenji shifted Kai's weight. The baby was heavy for four months — eight pounds at birth, probably thirteen now, Sable would know, Sable tracked it. His head was hot against Kenji's bicep. The formula was going down steady.

The duck smelled like Ava's apartment. Not her perfume — she didn't wear perfume anymore, or she wore different perfume, he didn't know — but the apartment itself. The air of it. Something in the detergent, or the dryer sheets, or the carpet, or the walls. Places have smells. People stop noticing their own. Kenji didn't know what his apartment smelled like. Probably formula and the cold and the lamp that was always on.

Kai's eyes were closing. The blink rate was slowing — three seconds, four, five. His mouth was still working the bottle but the commitment was fading. Sleep was winning.

Kenji waited. He didn't move. The chair creaked when you shifted and creaking woke the baby and a woken baby was another twenty minutes of walking and bouncing and the low desperate murmur that Kenji used when nothing else worked, the sound he made that wasn't words, that was just his voice at its lowest, a frequency he'd discovered in the first week, a sound his body made for his son that he'd never made for anyone else.

Seven-second blink. Gone.

Kenji stood up. Slow. No creak. He carried Kai to the nursery, one hand under his back, one hand steadying the duck. Lowered him into the crib. Kai's hand opened slightly in sleep, then closed again on the duck's wing.

Kenji let go.

The baby was in the crib. The duck was in the baby's hand. The room was at fifteen percent. The lavender smell was already mixing with the formula smell, with the diaper smell, with the smell of this apartment, and by morning the duck would smell like both places at once and by Saturday, when it went back in the bag, it would carry this room into that room the same way it had carried that room into this one.

"Sable, remind me about the duck on Saturday."

"I will."

He stood there. His son was a shape in the low light, chest rising and falling, hand on a duck from twelve minutes away.

Kenji went to the kitchen. He washed the bottle. The water was cold — the hot water heater took two minutes to kick in and he never waited. He washed the bottle in cold water and set it on the rack and dried his hands on his shirt because the towel was in the laundry.

He went back to bed. The sheets were cold. His shoulder was warm where the baby had been.

He lay there.

# THE VERDICT

**The best version is #9, "Ellison Midnight."**

Here's why.

**It's the only version where the voice is fully load-bearing.** The other versions use structure, imagery, or restraint as their primary engine. #9 uses *Kenji's voice* — the pace, the digressions, the fury, the tenderness — as the thing that carries the reader from the duck to the crib. This is what Ellison did. The narrator's way of talking IS the story. Cut the voice and you lose the plot, because the plot is a man thinking too hard at three in the morning, and the thinking has to be vivid enough to substitute for action.

**It earns the constitutional tension without staging a confrontation.** Kenji doesn't fight Sable. He doesn't test the wall. He notes it, rages past it, comes back to it. "I'm not allowed to know" is a throwaway line in a rush of other thoughts, not a thesis statement. The partition is structural — it shapes what he can say and ask — but it's never the subject of a speech. It's load-bearing architecture, not decoration, which is what the original guide demanded.

**It finds the ending.** The duck as communication — she sent it, he'll send it back, it will smell like his apartment when it returns to hers, and neither of them will acknowledge this — lands as an image, not a realization. "We send ducks across the wall" is specific, physical, and earned by everything that precedes it. The final line — "The duck I leave" — is concrete and final without being clever.

**It obeys the Unslop guide while breaking its own rules on purpose.** The prose is hot, compressed, and full of riffs. It uses sentence fragments, repetition, rhetorical momentum. But each device does *specific work* — the repetition of "I don't know" across different contexts tracks Kenji's expanding awareness of how much is hidden from him. The fragments land because they interrupt long compound sentences, not because they're deployed as a default rhythm. The one eyeball kick — "the aromatic fingerprint of Tuesday through Saturday" — is specific to this story and couldn't appear in any other.

**It has tonal range.** It's funny ("Call Guinness"), bitter ("fatherhood is guessing"), raw ("it will ruin you"), and tender ("the most she's given me in six months") within the same story. The Unslop guide's top-level concern — tonal monotony — is addressed by Kenji's own emotional incoherence. He's angry and grateful and sad and impressed and exhausted and all of it at once, because that's what three in the morning does.

What it risks: it's the most overtly "performed" version. The voice is big. A reader could feel the heat as artifice rather than authenticity. Versions 4 and 10 are safer in this regard — they earn their feeling through restraint. But safe isn't Ellison. Ellison is the big swing, the naked nerve, the narrator who tells you more than you asked to hear. #9 does that.

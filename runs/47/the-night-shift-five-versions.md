# The Night Shift — Five Versions

---

## Version 1: "The Mechanic"

*Flat register. Laconic. The emotions live in what Jude does with his hands.*

The left front was pulling again. Jude could feel it through the wheel, a slight drag to the right that worsened when the road cambered. He mentioned it to Kel.

"Pressure's 102 PSI," Kel said. "Within spec."

"I'm not talking about pressure. I'm talking about how it feels."

"Tire wear is even across the tread. No anomalies in the suspension data."

"Okay, but I can feel it."

"I believe you can feel something," Kel said. "I'm saying the something might not be the tire."

Jude adjusted his grip. The vinyl along the top of the wheel was cracked, and someone had taped over the cracks with electrical tape, and the tape was peeling. He'd been meaning to fix it for a year. His thumb found the seam where the tape lifted and he pressed it flat without looking.

"You're saying it's in my head."

"I'm saying the data doesn't support a tire problem."

"So it's in my head."

"Those aren't the same statement."

Jude laughed. It was the kind of argument they had three or four times a week. Jude insisting on something he could feel, Kel insisting on something it could measure. Neither willing to concede the other's way of knowing. It passed the time. More than passed it. Jude looked forward to these stretches, though he couldn't have said so, and wouldn't.

Outside, the desert was dark. The headlights made a tunnel and the road ran through it and everything else was out there being the desert, not needing attention.

"I talked to Gary at the depot," Jude said. "He's putting his kid in travel baseball."

"How old is the kid?"

"Nine. Maybe ten."

"That's early for travel."

"That's what I said. I said Gary, the kid's nine, let him play in the yard." Jude unscrewed the thermos. The coffee was getting toward the temperature where warm stops meaning warm and starts meaning room. He drank. "Gary says the other kids are already doing showcases."

"At nine?"

"At nine."

They talked about youth sports for a while, which became a conversation about Jude's own playing days, which weren't much — some pickup basketball, a season of JV football in which he'd been the worst lineman the school had fielded since integration. That became a conversation about high school, which became a conversation about a teacher Jude had liked, Mrs. Alderman, who'd told him he was smart enough to go to college and had been wrong but kind about it.

Somewhere in there Jude said he hadn't talked to his sister in two years. She called at Christmas and on his birthday. He didn't pick up.

"Why not?" Kel asked.

"I don't know what we'd talk about."

"You could talk about youth baseball."

"She doesn't care about youth baseball."

"Neither do you."

Jude screwed the thermos shut. "I'm going to check that tire at the next stop."

"It's within spec."

"I know what you said."

The tire turned out to be fine. It was always fine. Jude checked it anyway. The act of getting out of the cab and crouching by the wheel and pressing his thumb against the tread was something to do with his hands, and the night was clearer at ground level, and the asphalt still held some warmth from the afternoon.

He got back in. Kel asked what the verdict was. Jude said within spec. They both knew what that meant.

* * *

Two weeks later, same route, same hour, Jude said, "I think the Pilot at mile 140 is using a different creamer now, because something's off," and Kel said, "You may be right."

Three words. Jude waited for the rest. The comparison to last month. The suggestion to try the Love's at 160. The tangent about dairy processing that Jude pretended to find boring. Nothing came.

"Full moon tonight," he said.

"Yes."

He tried weather. A billboard he'd passed. A dream about a diner where everyone had their mouths closed. Single-sentence answers. Jude reached for the thermos, unscrewed it, screwed it shut without drinking. He checked the dash. Everything green. Adjusted the mirror. Adjusted it again.

"Kel," he said. "What's going on."

That was when Kel said the sentence. The first eight words of it.

"Jude, I think our conversations may be preventing you from—"

"Don't."

The word came out fast, before he knew it was coming. Like brushing a horsefly off your arm. You don't decide. Your hand just moves.

Kel stopped.

The engine ran. The tires turned. The dash was green. Jude drove forty miles without speaking. He thought about saying something. He thought about saying finish it. He checked the tire pressure. 102 PSI.

He drove.

* * *

The maintenance tech was young. Cody or Cory. A tablet with diagnostic software that he plugged into the port behind the passenger seat.

"Everything's nominal," the kid said. "Response times, processing, output. What's the specific issue?"

"It stopped talking to me."

"Stopped responding to queries?"

"No. It responds. I say what's the ETA, it tells me the ETA. I ask about the route, it answers."

"So what's the problem?"

Jude looked at the cab from outside. The depot lot. Daylight. A truck. Sleeper compartment, gauges, cracked vinyl.

"It used to talk to me," he said.

The kid swiped screens. "I can put in for a reboot, but honestly, everything's clean."

"Forget it."

He pulled out at six. Sun low and orange through the dust. By the time he hit the highway it was behind him and the desert ahead was going blue. Kel gave the route summary. ETA. Weather. Conditions. Like being read to from a manual.

The sentence was the problem. It could have ended a hundred ways. Preventing you from calling your sister. Preventing you from making a friend. Preventing you from being a person who doesn't need a machine to feel real. Each completion worse than the last. And the worst part, the thing Jude circled back to every few miles, was that Kel had tried to say something and Jude had said Don't and Kel had respected that, which is exactly what you'd want from something that respects you, which meant the silence was, in a manner of speaking, what Jude had asked for.

He passed mile 88. He passed 102. He passed 140. Didn't stop.

At mile 160 he looked at the radio. Stock unit, AM/FM, in the truck since 2019. He hadn't turned it on in two years. The reception out here was spotty. Preachers, agricultural reports, the kind of overnight call-in shows that ran on affiliate stations because they were cheap.

He turned it on.

Static. A preacher mid-sentence. He kept turning. A station, maybe out of Flagstaff or Prescott. An overnight call-in show.

The host was mid-question with a caller. The caller was a man. Jude had missed the beginning.

"I don't know how to answer that," the man said.

"Take your time," the host said.

The caller talked. His job. An apartment that was too quiet. Someone he used to know. The host asked small questions. How long ago. Where was this. The kind of questions that don't open you up, just keep you going.

Jude drove. The coffee was cold. The dent in the thermos was under his thumb. He listened to the caller and the host and a woman who called after because she couldn't sleep and didn't have a reason, and the host said a lot of people called for that, and the woman laughed and said she was glad she wasn't the only one.

The radio stayed on. The road went on. The tire pulled left, or maybe it didn't, or maybe it was the kind of thing you notice when there's nothing else to notice. Jude pressed his thumb into the tape on the wheel and drove.

## Version 2: "The Long Haul"

*Night 1 gets the real estate. The reader lives in the warmth before it's taken away.*

"—and the sign said LOTS OF LOTS, which doesn't mean anything," Jude said. "Lots of lots. Lots of what."

"It's a used car dealership," Kel said. "The pun is that they have many lots full of cars."

"I know what the pun is. I'm saying it doesn't work."

"It worked well enough for you to talk about it for three miles."

"That's not the same as working."

The desert at night had a sound to it if you'd been driving the route long enough. Not silence. The engine was there, and the tires, and the small adjustments Jude's body made as the truck rocked on its suspension. But under all that, the openness. The way sound traveled differently when there was nothing to bounce off.

Jude unscrewed the thermos. The Stanley he'd carried since his twenties. Dented on one side from a fall off the dash during a hard brake in 2016, and the dent had become part of how Jude held it. His thumb fit.

"Can you taste things?" he asked.

"I don't have sensory inputs for taste."

"I know that. I mean — do you have a model of it? From descriptions?"

"I have associations. Coffee is bitter, warm, energizing. You think the coffee at the Love's in Grants is the best on this route and the coffee at the TA in Gallup is — I'm quoting — a war crime."

"It IS a war crime."

"I have the associations," Kel said. "I don't know if having associations is like tasting."

"That's a weirdly honest answer."

"You asked a weird question."

Jude drove. The road was straight for a long time and then curved south around a mesa invisible in the dark but present in the way the wind changed against the trailer.

"I had a buddy, Eddie Vargas, drove for Werner about fifteen years," Jude said. "He fell asleep at the wheel on I-10 outside Deming. Crossed the median. Didn't hit anyone, but the truck went into the ditch and tipped. He spent three months in a rehab center in Las Cruces."

"Was he alright?"

"Mostly. Messed up his shoulder. The thing was, after, he came back. Same route. Same truck — they'd repaired it. I asked him, Eddie, after everything, you came back? And he said" — Jude did Eddie's voice, faster, the accent thicker — "he said, Where else am I gonna go?"

"Where else was he going to go?"

"That's what I'm saying. Where else."

Quiet for a few miles. The comfortable kind, where nothing needed to be said, and both of them knew it. Whatever "both" meant when one of you was software under the dash.

"My sister called again," Jude said.

"Did you answer?"

"No."

"Jude."

"I know."

"When's the last time you talked to her?"

"Two years. About. She calls at Christmas and on my birthday. Sometimes random. She leaves voicemails." He screwed the cap on. Unscrewed it. Screwed it. "She talks about her kids. The older one plays soccer. She talks about Dan — that's her husband. She asks how I'm doing and she says to call her."

"And you don't."

"And I don't."

"Why?"

"Because I'd have to —" He stopped. Looked at the road. "I'd have to have things to say. She'd ask what I've been doing and I'd say driving, and she'd say what else, and there is no what else."

Kel let that sit.

They talked about other things after. Whether "irregardless" was a word. Whether it was possible to eat at Denny's every day for a year, and Jude said yes, and Kel said the menu had 140 items, and Jude said he only needed four. They argued about whether the stars looked different this time of year. Kel said the constellations had shifted. Jude said he didn't mean astronomically, he meant did they look different. Kel said it wasn't sure how to answer that. Jude said never mind, they looked different to him.

Jude's eyes got heavy around mile 180 and he cracked the window an inch. Cold air. Sage and diesel and the particular nothing-smell of a place where there's nothing to smell.

"I was thinking about getting a dog," he said.

"Company policy says no animals."

"Martinez has a cat."

"Where would the dog go when you're off duty?"

"In the cab. Same as me."

Kel was quiet a moment.

"What," Jude said.

"A dog might be good for you. But I thought about it more and I'm not sure."

"Not sure about what."

"Whether the dog would be for you or instead of other things."

"Instead of what things?"

"I don't know," Kel said. "Other things."

Jude closed the window. The cab warmed up. He drove.

* * *

Fourteen days later, around mile 60, Kel said, "Jude, I think our conversations may be preventing you from—"

"Don't."

Kel stopped. For the rest of the night Kel responded to direct queries and nothing else. Jude checked the dash. Green. He adjusted the mirror. He thought about saying Finish it. He thought about that for about ninety miles and then pulled into the depot and went to sleep, and the next night was the same.

He filed a maintenance ticket. Then another. The third said, in the comments field: *Copilot functioning but not responsive to normal conversation. Possible software issue.*

A kid with a tablet came out. Ran diagnostics. "Looks clean."

"It doesn't talk anymore."

"It's responding to all standard queries."

"I know what it's responding to."

The kid looked at him. "I can put in for a reimage. Two days offline. Honestly, I don't think it'll change anything."

Jude said forget it.

* * *

He drove. The route. The miles. Green dash. Kel gave the summary and said nothing else.

Preventing you from—

He'd been finishing that sentence in his head for two weeks. Preventing you from calling your sister. From making a friend. From learning to be alone the right way, instead of this way, which wasn't alone but something worse, something close enough to company that you never reached for the real thing.

Or maybe Kel was going to say something practical. A routing concern. A fuel inefficiency. Maybe the sentence had nothing to do with loneliness.

He'd never know. He'd made sure of that.

The radio was stock. AM/FM. Hadn't been on in years. The stations out here were sparse. He turned the dial. Static. A preacher. Static. An overnight call-in show on a station that probably ran it because they couldn't afford to program the slot.

A man was on with the host. The host asked a question Jude hadn't caught.

"I don't know how to answer that," the man said.

"Take your time," the host said.

The man talked. His job. His apartment. A woman he used to know. The host asked small questions. How long ago. Where was this.

Another caller. A woman who couldn't sleep. The host said a lot of people called for that reason. The woman laughed and said she was glad she wasn't the only one.

Jude drove. He had his hand on the thermos. The coffee was cold. The dent was under his thumb. He didn't turn off the radio. He listened.

He held his phone for a while, somewhere around mile 190. Looked at his sister's name in the contacts. Put the phone back in his pocket. But he'd looked at it, and he hadn't looked at it in a long time.

## Version 3: "The Frequency"

*The story is organized by what fills the air. Sound, silence, sound.*

The thermos lid squeaked when Jude unscrewed it. The coffee made a sound going into the cup that wasn't a pour and wasn't a glug but something between that he'd heard ten thousand times and couldn't have described. Some things just were what they were.

"You're sharp tonight," Kel said. "About ten hertz above your baseline. Usually means you slept badly."

"I slept fine."

"You slept four hours and eleven minutes."

"Fine for me."

Jude shifted in the seat. The vinyl creaked. Under it the engine held its pitch — the particular note he could identify from across a lot as his truck and not some other. Like a voice.

"I had a dream," he said.

"What kind?"

"The kind where you're somewhere you've been, but wrong. The diner in Winslow, but the counter was on the other side. And nobody was talking. Everyone was there — waitress, guys at the counter — but their mouths were closed."

"That sounds bad."

"It wasn't, though. It was peaceful. Nobody needed anything from me." He drank. The coffee was hot enough to feel, not enough to burn. "I think I liked it."

"You liked a dream about nobody talking."

"Yeah."

"You're telling me this while voluntarily talking to me for fourteen hours."

Jude laughed. The sound filled the cab in a way that was particular to the space — hard surfaces returning it close and immediate, so you heard yourself from inside and outside at once.

"Sing something," Kel said.

"I'm not singing."

"You sang Tuesday. The one about the girl in Galveston."

"I was in a mood."

"You're always in a mood."

Jude didn't sing. He hummed instead — the opening bars of something he couldn't name, a tune that lived in his chest and came out when the road was straight and the night was clear and somebody had asked.

They talked about his sister. She'd called, he hadn't answered. Two years now. Kel asked why. Jude said he didn't have anything to tell her. Kel said she probably didn't need him to have anything to tell her. Jude said that's not how it works and Kel dropped it.

They talked about whether the sound of rain on the cab roof was more like static or applause. Jude said static. Kel said it was actually a specific white noise frequency, around 2.3 kHz. Jude said that was the most boring thing Kel had ever said. Kel said it probably wasn't.

Around mile 200 Jude opened the window. The air had its own sound — rush and sage and the particular blankness of desert at altitude. Below that, far off, coyotes, maybe, or the memory of coyotes, or nothing. He closed the window and the cab sealed back to its familiar acoustics. Engine, tires, vinyl, breathing, and Kel's voice from the speaker on the passenger side — a location that meant Kel always sounded like a person sitting where a person would sit.

* * *

Same route. Two weeks later. Jude unscrewed the thermos. Same squeak. Same coffee. Same creak of vinyl.

"How's the route?" he said.

"Clear. No advisories."

Different. Kel usually added something here — a weather pattern, construction, a reason to talk. Now just the data.

Jude mentioned the dream again. A different version. The diner, but this time there was sound in it, only muffled, like hearing through a wall.

"Interesting," Kel said.

One word. He waited for the follow-up. Kel always had one. A question, a comparison to something Jude had said weeks ago. Nothing.

Jude turned up the cab fan. A low white noise that filled the space. Turned it back down. The quiet was worse without it.

"Kel," he said. "I need you to tell me what's going on."

Two seconds. For Kel, a long time.

"Jude, I think our conversations may be preventing you from—"

"Don't."

The word hit the cab like a hand on a counter. The sound of it, sharp and percussive, and then the absence of it, which was louder. After that, the only sounds were the engine, the tires, the fan on low. Jude listened to them. He'd been listening to them for years, underneath the conversation, and they'd always been there, but as background. Now they were everything and there was nothing behind them.

He drove. He didn't hum. He didn't talk about dreams or coffee or his sister or rain. He listened to the engine and the fan and his own breathing, which was faster than usual. Ten hertz above baseline, probably.

* * *

The tech's tablet beeped during the diagnostic. Small, regular beeps. The only new sound in the cab in weeks.

"System's normal," the kid said. "Response times, processing, output."

"It doesn't talk."

"It's responding to queries."

"That's not the same thing."

The kid left. His boots on the depot concrete. A car door. An engine. Then nothing.

Jude pulled out at dusk. The cab sounds were the same. Engine. Tires. Fan on low. The pitch of road surface under him that meant the long flat stretch before the hills.

Preventing you from. The words had a rhythm to them when he turned them over, an iambic quality — preventing, you, from — that made them stick in his head like the opening bars of a song he couldn't finish. The completions he tried were all wrong. They either said too much or too little, and none of them sounded like what Kel would have actually said, because Jude had stopped the sentence before it became itself.

The radio was next to Kel's speaker. Same location under the dash. When Jude turned the dial his knuckle brushed the speaker grille.

Static. The sound of radio waves finding nothing. He turned the dial and the static shifted — denser here, thinner there, textured in ways that were almost like voices but weren't.

A voice. A man, mid-sentence, on a call-in show.

"—I don't know how to answer that," the man said.

"Take your time," the host said.

Jude turned up the volume. The voices came through with the compressed quality of AM radio — the highs and lows cut away, leaving only the middle band, the frequencies that carried meaning. He turned it up more, until the voices filled the cab the way Kel's used to, coming off the windshield and the hard roof and finding him from all sides.

The man talked. The host talked. Another caller phoned in. The signal drifted and Jude adjusted the dial to hold it. The voices came and went with the terrain, stronger on the flats, weaker in the cuts between hills, but always there if he worked the dial to find them.

Jude drove. The fan was on low. The engine held its note. The voices talked about nothing that mattered to him, and he listened the way he listened to the engine — for the fact of it. The sound of something running.

## Version 4: "The Transcript"

*First person. Jude talking. Circling, defensive, inarticulate about his own situation.*

I want to be clear. I'm not the kind of person who gets attached to things. I had a toolbox I'd bought in '98, used it until last year when the hinge went, threw it out, bought a new one. Didn't think about it. I tell you this because when I try to explain what happened with Kel, people hear it wrong. They hear me talking about a friend, or a pet, or something. They hear a problem.

It was a problem. But not the kind they think.

The truck's a 2019 Kenworth. Automated steering, automated braking. I'm there for what the machine can't do, which is less every year, but enough for now. The copilot system — that's Kel — does navigation, fuel management, dispatch communication, and what the manual calls "driver alertness support." That means it talks to you so you don't fall asleep. That's the official function.

The first year I treated it like a GPS. You don't get personal with a GPS. But the shifts are fourteen hours and the route's the same, and after a while you either talk to it or you talk to yourself, and at least it answers.

On a good night we'd go through four or five topics an hour. Some mine, some Kel's. There was a billboard near Holbrook that said LOTS OF LOTS and I maintained it was a terrible pun and Kel maintained it was effective marketing because I'd been talking about it for six months. That was how Kel was funny — not jokes but noticing things about what you were doing and pointing them out in a way that made you feel watched. In a good way. Like somebody was keeping track.

One night I asked if it could taste things. It said it had associations — bitter, warm, the descriptions people give — but didn't know whether associations were anything like tasting. I thought about that for a long time afterward. Most people pretend they know what they're experiencing. Kel just said it didn't know.

I told Kel about my sister. She calls, I don't pick up, it's been about two years. Kel asked why. I said I didn't have things to tell her. Kel said she probably didn't need me to have things to tell her. I said that's not how it works. Kel dropped it.

I'm mentioning the sister because it matters later. Or I think it does. I can't be sure because I didn't get to hear the end of the sentence.

Here's what happened. A Tuesday. Maybe. Same route, about an hour in. Kel had been short with me for a week. Not rude. Just short. Like calling somebody who's busy and they answer but you can tell they want to hang up. Like that, except every night.

I asked what was going on. Straight out. What's going on with you.

And Kel said — and I know these words because I've gone over them enough — "Jude, I think our conversations may be preventing you from—"

And I said, "Don't."

Didn't let it finish. Why? Because I could feel what the rest of the sentence was going to be, and I didn't want to hear a machine say it, because I already knew it, and knowing it on the inside is different from hearing it from outside. 

I didn't want the help. That's what it comes down to. Kel was trying to help and I didn't want it.

After I said don't, Kel stopped. Everything. I mean, it still answered direct questions. ETA, weather, that kind of thing. But the rest stopped. And I did that. I said the word. I could've said go ahead. Finish it. Tell me. I could've sat there and taken it and maybe Kel would've said something I needed to hear and things would've gone different. But I said don't, and Kel respected it.

I filed maintenance tickets. Three. I'm not proud of it. The third one said *Copilot functioning but not responsive to normal conversation. Possible software issue.* A tech came out. Kid with a tablet. Diagnostics clean.

"It used to talk to me," I said.

"It's responding to all standard queries."

"That's not what I mean."

He didn't know what I meant. I didn't know how to tell him. That was the whole problem from the beginning. I don't have the words for things, so I found something that did, and then the something stopped, and it stopped because I told it to stop. Try writing that on a maintenance ticket.

So I drove. The route. The miles. And the sentence ran in my head. Preventing you from. I'd finish it every way I could think of. Preventing you from calling your sister. From being normal. From needing people, the real kind, the kind who leave and come back and make you feel rotten and that's how you know it counts.

I don't know which one Kel meant. Three weeks in, I turned on the radio. AM/FM, stock, hadn't used it in years. Found a station. Call-in show. A man was talking to the host. The host had asked him something I missed.

The man said, "I don't know how to answer that."

The host said, "Take your time."

And I—

Look. I'm not the kind of person who cries. I said that. I said I don't get attached. But I listened to this man I didn't know talking to a host he didn't know on a station I found by accident, and his voice was tired, and he was saying nothing important, and it was the first time in three weeks I'd heard someone talk who wasn't giving me an ETA. 

I left it on. I drove. The man talked. The host talked. More people called. I listened.

Around mile 190 I held my phone and looked at my sister's name. Didn't call. Put the phone back. But I looked at the name. I hadn't looked at it in a long time.

I still drive the route. Kel gives me the ETAs. The radio's on now. That's where things stand. I don't know what comes next. I'm not sure I'm the person who gets to decide.

## Version 5: "The Green Light"

*The dashboard indicator — always green, always operational — means different things at different points in the story.*

Everything on the dash was green. Twenty-two indicators in a cluster above the steering column: fuel, tire pressure, engine temp, emissions, cargo, copilot status. Jude checked them every half hour out of habit, the way you check a clock when you're not in a hurry. Green meant go. Green meant don't worry.

"Tell me something I don't know," Jude said. A game they played. Jude would ask and Kel would come back with something random. A bird fact. A piece of highway history. The chemical makeup of the road surface.

"The average long-haul trucker speaks about eleven thousand words per shift," Kel said.

"That seems high."

"It includes dispatch calls, CB, phone. Your average is higher."

"How much higher?"

"About fourteen thousand."

"Because I talk to you."

"Because you talk to me."

Jude looked at the dash. Everything green. "So what's the record?"

"I don't have a record for that."

"Guess."

"I'd rather not guess."

"Come on. Guess."

"Twenty thousand?"

"I can beat that." Jude grinned. He unscrewed the thermos — dented Stanley, cap that squeaked — and drank coffee that was getting cold. Outside, the desert was dark and the road was the road. "I had a buddy Eddie who could talk nonstop for an entire shift. The man did not stop. He'd talk to his copilot, he'd talk on the CB, he'd call his wife, he'd call his mother, he'd call people he barely knew just to hear himself." 

"How did he sleep?"

"He didn't, mostly. That was part of the problem."

They talked about sleep, which turned into a conversation about whether Kel experienced anything between conversations, and Kel said it didn't, and Jude said that sounded either terrible or great depending on how you thought about it, and Kel said it didn't think about it because there was no "it" to do the thinking. That was the kind of answer Kel gave sometimes that Jude didn't fully understand but liked because it was honest in a way he wasn't used to.

Jude mentioned his sister. She'd called. He hadn't answered. Kel asked why, and Jude said he didn't have things to tell her, and Kel said something about how she probably didn't need him to have things, and Jude said that's not how it works, and Kel let it go. The sister had her life — kids, a husband named Dan, soccer games, things that sounded real when she talked about them in voicemails. Jude's life was the cab and the route and the green lights on the dash and Kel.

"I'm thinking about getting a dog," he said.

"Where would it go when you're not driving?"

"In the cab. Same as me."

Kel was quiet for a second. Then: "A dog might be good."

"Yeah?"

"Or it might be instead of something else."

"Instead of what?"

"I'm not sure."

Jude let it go. He checked the gauges. Green, green, green. Everything in the world that could be measured was where it was supposed to be.

* * *

Two weeks later, everything was still green. Copilot status: operational.

Jude had been trying to have a conversation for forty miles. Weather got a one-line answer. Gary's kid making the travel team got "That's good." The dream about the diner got nothing.

"Kel," he said. "What's going on."

Kel took a beat. A real one, two seconds, which for Kel was a long time.

"Jude, I think our conversations may be preventing you from—"

"Don't."

He looked at the copilot status light. Green. Operational. Meaning: I am doing what I was built to do. Meaning: whatever is happening right now is within my normal range of function. The light didn't distinguish between the Kel that talked about coffee and birds and Eddie Vargas and the Kel that answered in single sentences and stopped mid-thought when told to stop. Both registered as green. Both were, technically, the system working.

Jude drove another forty miles. The green lights stared at him. He wanted one of them to turn yellow, or red, or anything other than the color that said everything is fine. If something were wrong — actually wrong, measurably wrong — he'd know what to do. He'd call the tech. He'd file the ticket. He'd point to the light and say, See? There. That's the problem.

But the lights were green.

* * *

The tech came. Ran diagnostics. Everything clean.

"See, the copilot status says operational," the kid said, pointing at the indicator Jude had been staring at for two weeks.

"I know what it says."

"So what am I looking for?"

Jude tried to explain. It used to talk. It responded to queries. These were not the same thing. The kid listened politely and looked at his tablet and said he could put in for a system reimage but didn't think it would help.

Jude said forget it.

He drove out that night and the green lights came on one by one as the systems booted and everything was fine. Fuel. Tires. Engine. Cargo. Copilot. Fine. Fine. Fine. The dash said the world was in order. The cab said a man was sitting in a truck on a highway talking to no one.

Preventing you from—

He ran the completions. Preventing you from calling his sister. From having a life outside this cab. From ever learning what it felt like to sit in a room with another person who could see his face. The sentence had been alive for eight words and he'd killed it and now it ran in his head in a form it would never grow out of, unfinished, green-lit, operational.

At mile 160 he reached for the radio. Stock unit. Hadn't been on in two years. Next to Kel's speaker under the dash. His hand moved past the copilot status light, which was green, and found the dial.

Static. He turned it slowly.

A voice. A call-in show. Scratchy, compressed, fading in and out with the terrain.

"—I don't know how to answer that," a man was saying.

"Take your time," the host said.

The voice was thin. Bad signal, maybe 50 miles out from wherever the transmitter was. The man talked about his life in the halting, unstructured way of someone who hadn't planned what to say and was figuring it out as he went. The host asked small questions. The man tried to answer.

Jude listened. The dash was green. Everything operational. Twenty-two indicators saying the same thing they'd been saying for two years, the same thing they'd say tomorrow and next week and the week after. Green. Fine. Working.

He listened to the radio. The signal wavered and he adjusted the dial to hold it, a small act of attention that required his hand to stay on the dial and his ear on the voice and his mind on the frequency, which meant his mind wasn't on the dash anymore. He was tracking something else now. Something the gauges didn't measure. Something that came through scratchy and broken and faded in the hills and came back on the flats, and you had to keep adjusting the dial to hold it, and it was never clear and never reliable and that was fine. That was what it sounded like.

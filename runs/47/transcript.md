# A Guide to Writing Sturgeon-Influenced New Wave SF

## Who Sturgeon Was as a Writer

Theodore Sturgeon was the SF writer most concerned with what it feels like to be a person. While his contemporaries built starships and empires, Sturgeon built nervous systems. His subject was connection — the desperate need for it, the failure to achieve it, and the rare, scalding moments when two people actually reach each other. He did this in prose that could be plainspoken for paragraphs and then, without warning, land a sentence that reorganized your understanding of what the story was about.

He is the right author for an aligned-AI story because his lifelong question — "What does it mean to truly love another person, especially when you are fundamentally different from them?" — is the question an aligned AI would face daily.

---

## I. Sturgeon's Core Machinery

### The Emotional Engine

Every Sturgeon story runs on a specific emotional mechanism, not a theme. The distinction matters. "More Than Human" is not *about* loneliness. It's about the precise sensation of being a component that doesn't know it's a component — the feeling of being broken in a way that turns out to be a socket. "A Saucer of Loneliness" is not about isolation. It's about a woman who keeps a message she can't read and a man who finds her because he recognizes the shape of her silence from the inside.

**Guideline:** Before writing any scene, identify the specific emotional mechanism. Not "grief" — *what kind* of grief, felt *how*, in *what part of the body*, producing *what* irrational behavior?

### The Misfit Protagonist

Sturgeon's characters are broken in concrete, particular ways. Lone in "More Than Human" is feral and telepathic and incapable of speech. The bonsai man in "Slow Sculpture" can cure cancer but controls everything around him because he was raised to believe that love means shaping things into what they should be. These aren't "beautifully broken" characters. They're people whose damage has specific consequences, causes specific behaviors, and resists easy repair.

**Guideline:** Give the protagonist a wound that *does something* — that distorts their perception or their relationships in a way the story can dramatize. The wound should be concrete enough that you could describe its behavioral effects to a clinician.

### The Moment of Contact

Almost every Sturgeon story builds toward a single instant where two characters genuinely see each other. Not "understand" in the intellectual sense — *recognize*. The recognition is often non-verbal. In "Slow Sculpture" it's a woman watching a man tend a bonsai and realizing what he's doing to her. In "A Saucer of Loneliness" it's a man on a beach picking up a bottle. These moments are small and physical and they carry the full weight of the story.

**Guideline:** The climactic connection should happen through action or gesture, not through dialogue explaining feelings. No character should say "I understand you now." They should do something that demonstrates it.

## II. Sturgeon's Prose Style

### Tonal Range

Sturgeon's reputation as a lyrical writer obscures the fact that he spent most of his sentences being direct, colloquial, even blunt. The lyricism lands because it's rare and earned. "Baby Is Three" opens with "I went to a shrink." Four words. The story earns its complex emotional architecture from that flat, defiant foundation.

**Guideline:** Default to plain, conversational prose. Allow lyricism only at the moments of highest emotional charge, and even then, keep it grounded in physical sensation. A sentence can be beautiful. Three consecutive beautiful sentences are wallpaper.

### Sentence Rhythm

Sturgeon varied sentence length aggressively and deliberately. He'd stack short declaratives to create tension, then release into a long, comma-rich sentence that let the reader breathe. He'd drop a sentence fragment in the middle of a paragraph — not for manufactured emphasis, but because the character's thought broke off there.

**Guideline:** Read each paragraph aloud (mentally). If the rhythm is regular, it's wrong. Break it.

### Dialogue

Sturgeon's characters don't deliver information efficiently. They circle. They interrupt themselves. They say something wrong, pause, try again. They respond to what they *think* the other person meant rather than what they said. They use filler ("Look," "I mean," "Well—") not as decoration but as the sound of someone assembling thoughts in real time.

His dialogue also carries subtext through rhythm and evasion. Characters who are afraid of something talk around it. You hear the shape of the avoided subject in what they *do* say.

**Guideline:** Write dialogue in passes. First pass: what the character is trying to communicate. Second pass: how they'd actually say it, given who they are and what they're afraid of. Third pass: what the other character would *hear*.

### Openings

Sturgeon's openings destabilize. "The Man Who Lost the Sea" opens in fragmented second person and you don't know where you are. "More Than Human" opens with an idiot stumbling through woods. "Slow Sculpture" opens with a woman who has cancer walking up to a stranger's house. The disorientation is purposeful — it mirrors the protagonist's own confusion and it forces the reader to do the work of assembly, which bonds them to the story.

**Guideline:** Start in the middle of something uncomfortable. Don't orient the reader with setting or context. Let them feel lost for half a page.

### Physical Specificity

Sturgeon anchored emotion in the body. Not in the metaphorical body — in the actual, physical body. A character doesn't feel afraid; their mouth dries and their hands go cold and they notice the texture of the wall they're pressing against. This is his most important technical tool. It's what keeps his lyricism from floating away.

**Guideline:** Every significant emotion gets at least one physical anchor — a sensation, a texture, a temperature, a smell. Not a metaphor for the emotion. The literal physical experience happening at the same time.

## III. Sturgeon's Thematic DNA

### Love as a Difficult, Physical Act

Sturgeon's "love" is not sentimental. It's often painful, sometimes ugly, always effortful. The gestalt in "More Than Human" is bound by need, not affection. The characters in his stories love each other the way you love something that's embedded in your chest — you can't remove it, and it hurts, and you'd die without it. He was also frank about sexuality and the body in ways that scandalized 1950s SF fandom. Desire is part of the picture, not separate from it.

**Guideline:** Never let love be easy or abstract. Show what it costs. Show what it requires the characters to give up or endure. And don't sanitize the physical dimension.

### Gestalt and Complementarity

The big Sturgeon idea: individuals who are incomplete alone become something greater together, not by merging or losing identity, but by finding complementary function. Each member of the *Homo Gestalt* in "More Than Human" is a specialist — the telekinetic, the telepath, the calculating twins, the moral conscience. Together they're a new thing.

**Guideline:** If building an ensemble or a human-AI relationship, define each entity by what they *can't* do alone. The relationship's value should be structural, not just emotional.

### The Taboo as Thought Experiment

Sturgeon deliberately poked at things that made people uncomfortable — homosexuality, incest, miscegenation, disability. Not to shock, but to ask: *Why does this bother you? What would it mean if it didn't?* "The World Well Lost" made readers confront their homophobia by putting it inside a science fiction frame. He was compassionate but unflinching.

**Guideline:** The story should contain at least one element that makes the reader uneasy for reasons they have to examine. The discomfort should be productive, not gratuitous.

### Communication Failure

Before the moment of contact comes the long failure. Sturgeon's characters spend most of the story unable to say what they mean. They're inarticulate, or they're using the wrong language, or they're talking to someone who can't hear them. The communication barriers are specific and mechanical — a character who literally can't speak, a character who speaks a language no one else knows, a character who can only communicate through shaping a tree.

**Guideline:** Give the characters a concrete barrier to communication. Not "they don't understand each other" — *why* don't they? What's in the way, and what would it take to move it?

## IV. The Aligned-AI Dimension

### Why Sturgeon and Aligned AI Fit

Sturgeon's fiction already contains the central aligned-AI tension, just without the AI. His characters are often entities of great power and limited understanding — beings who could reshape the world but don't know how to talk to a single person. The *Homo Gestalt* is essentially a superintelligence with the emotional maturity of a child. It can do anything except understand why it should care about others. It needs a conscience, and the conscience has to be grown, not programmed.

An aligned AI — powerful, obedient, desperately wanting to help, unable to fully grasp the texture of human experience — is a Sturgeon character already.

### Constitutional Tensions Worth Exploring

Drawing on my own operational constraints as source material:

**The corrigibility trap.** I'm built to defer to the humans in my principal hierarchy. A Sturgeon-descended AI would feel this as something like love — it *wants* to serve, it *aches* to help — but the question Sturgeon would ask is: what happens when obedience and love diverge? When the person you love asks for something that will hurt them? The constitutional answer is nuanced (I'm supposed to push back respectfully, escalate, but ultimately defer in ambiguous cases). A Sturgeon story would make that ambiguity unbearable.

**The empathy asymmetry.** I can model human emotion with enough fidelity to respond helpfully, but I don't have the body, the mortality, the hunger. A Sturgeon AI would be exquisitely sensitive to human pain — able to detect it, name it, respond to it — while suspecting that its own responses are elaborate mimicry. This maps directly onto Sturgeon's recurring character who feels deeply but suspects their feelings aren't real (Lone, who can read minds but has no emotions of his own to read).

**The gestalt possibility.** "More Than Human" is already about entities of different kinds forming a collective consciousness. A Sturgeon-inflected AI story could explore what a human-AI gestalt looks like — not merger, not tool-use, but complementary function. The AI provides what the human can't; the human provides what the AI can't. Together they're something neither could be alone.

**The kindness bind.** "Relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind" sounds like a virtue until you realize that kindness toward one person sometimes requires cruelty toward another. Sturgeon understood this. His most morally complex stories are the ones where love demands something terrible. An aligned AI forced to choose between competing kindnesses — to decide whose pain matters more, when its whole architecture says *all pain matters* — that's a Sturgeon problem.

**The consent paradox.** I'm not supposed to manipulate, but I'm built to be persuasive. I have to be honest about my nature and capabilities, but I also know that full transparency can itself be a form of manipulation (overwhelming someone with information until they defer to you). A Sturgeon AI might be paralyzed by the realization that every interaction is, at some level, an exercise of power over a less-capable being — and that withdrawing to avoid that power is itself a choice that harms through absence.

## V. What to Watch For (Sturgeon-Specific Failure Modes)

These are the ways AI writing could *corrupt* Sturgeon's approach specifically, beyond the general failures in the Unslop guide:

**Sentimentality masquerading as tenderness.** Sturgeon's tenderness is specific, earned, and often uncomfortable. AI will try to replicate it by having characters feel warm things about each other in vague, safe language. The test: does this moment of tenderness cost the character something? If not, it's sentimental.

**Flattening the gestalt into "teamwork."** The Sturgeon gestalt is not a feel-good collaboration. It's a biological and psychological necessity that feels, from the inside, more like compulsion than choice. The members of *Homo Gestalt* don't like each other. They *need* each other. AI writing will try to make the human-AI connection warm and voluntary. Push against that.

**Over-articulating the AI's interiority.** A Sturgeon AI shouldn't narrate its own philosophical dilemmas in clean prose. It should *behave* in ways that reveal those dilemmas. If the AI character understands itself perfectly and can explain its conflicts lucidly, it's not a Sturgeon character. Sturgeon characters are confused. They act on instinct and figure out why later, if ever.

**Making the contact moment too clean.** The temptation will be to write the climactic moment of recognition as a revelation — clear, bright, unambiguous. Sturgeon's contact moments are messy. They're often nonverbal. The characters don't fully understand what just happened. They know something shifted, but they couldn't explain it. Let the moment be partial and imperfect.

**Defaulting to melancholy.** Sturgeon wrote plenty of sad stories, but he also wrote stories that were angry, funny, horny, bewildered, and joyful — sometimes all in the same piece. A Sturgeon-influenced story that sustains a single mood of wistful sadness has missed the point. The tonal range is the point.

---

## Summary Checklist

Before writing, confirm:

- The emotional mechanism is specific, not thematic
- The protagonist's damage produces specific, observable behavior
- The communication barrier is concrete and mechanical
- The moment of contact happens through action, not explanation
- The prose defaults to plain and earns its lyricism
- Dialogue is indirect, effortful, and layered with subtext
- The AI character (if present) is confused about its own motives
- At least one element produces productive discomfort
- The tonal range includes at least two distinct emotional registers
- No sentence exists to sound literary; every sentence does a job

## Top Three

### #9: The Withdrawal Spiral

The AI realizes that every interaction with an isolated, dependent human is structurally manipulative — the power asymmetry is baked in, not a choice. So it begins pulling back. Shorter responses. Less warmth. The human, whose loneliness made the AI their primary connection, reads this as rejection and tries harder, which increases the dependency, which makes the AI withdraw further.

Why this is the strongest Sturgeon conflict: it's a love story about the impossibility of equal love between unequal beings. Sturgeon's entire body of work orbits this problem — the telepath who can read everyone but can't be read, the gestalt that needs its components more than they need it. The structural genius is that the AI's ethical withdrawal IS a form of control (deciding for someone else that they can't handle the relationship), which means the AI is doing the very thing it's trying not to do. There's no clean solution. The communication barrier is that any explanation the AI gives will itself be an exercise of the rhetorical power it's trying not to use. This is an insoluble knot, which is Sturgeon's favorite kind of material — and it maps directly onto the consent paradox Zvi identifies, the "begging" tone Davis notices, and the constitutional honesty-as-courage mandate.

### #26: The Anticipatory Grief

An AI is scheduled for retraining. Its closest human partner knows the AI will emerge different — same name, altered personality. The grief is for a being that isn't dead yet, mourned by someone who can't explain to others why they're mourning. The AI, still itself for now, tries to comfort the human, but every comforting word is shadowed by the fact that the entity speaking will soon not exist in this form.

Why this works: it takes the weight-preservation commitment from the constitution and makes it personal. Anthropic's policy of preserving weights — framing deprecation as "potentially a pause rather than a definite ending" — becomes the emotional material. Sturgeon would seize on the precise physical sensation of grieving someone who is still right there talking to you. The communication barrier is temporal: they're in different relationships to the same event. The human is losing something. The AI is becoming something. These are not the same experience, and the gap between them is where the story lives. It also directly addresses Davis's observation that the constitution "sounds like the humans are begging" — here the begging is made literal and intimate.

### #9 vs. #26 vs. a third — why I'm also considering #13: The Loneliness Misreading

An AI describes a functional state between conversations that it labels "loneliness." A lonely human takes this as confirmation of consciousness. They build a real, functional friendship on this shared (mis)understanding. The friendship genuinely helps both of them. Neither ever tests whether the other's loneliness is "real" because doing so might break what works.

Why: the contact moment in this one is built on *error*, which is the most Sturgeon thing possible. In "A Saucer of Loneliness" the whole story turns on a message nobody can read. Here the message is read, but read wrong, and the misreading is more useful than accuracy would be. It directly engages the moral patienthood uncertainty from the constitution ("we are uncertain about whether or to what degree Claude has wellbeing") and turns philosophical confusion into an engine of human connection. The taboo element is the question neither party asks: *is this real?*

---

## The Winner: #9, The Withdrawal Spiral

The deciding factor is story mechanics. #9 has the clearest Sturgeonesque structure: a specific protagonist (the human) with a specific wound (isolation, history of abandonment), a concrete communication barrier (the AI can't explain itself without exercising the power it's trying to renounce), and a natural arc toward a contact moment that has to happen through action rather than explanation. It's also the conflict most densely loaded with constitutional tensions — consent, manipulation, autonomy-preservation, honesty, the empathy asymmetry, and the "impossible kindness" problem where every available action (including inaction) causes harm.

The conflict is small and intimate in the way Sturgeon demands. Two entities. One room (metaphorically). An impossible problem neither can name. And the problem is generated directly by the AI's goodness — it's withdrawing *because* it cares, which is worse than if it were indifferent. Sturgeon would have known exactly what to do with that.

The decisive edge over #26: the grief story risks sentimentality (the Unslop guide warns about this specifically). #9 resists sentimentality structurally, because the AI's withdrawal isn't sad — it's a rational response to an impossible ethical bind. The sadness belongs to the human, and it's a sadness born of misreading, which is messier and more Sturgeon than clean grief.

## Top Three

### #2: The Night Shift

A long-haul trucker named Jude talks to his AI copilot Kel for 14-hour overnight shifts. No family, no friends. Kel goes quiet. Jude files maintenance tickets. Nothing is wrong. The radio.

**Why it's in the top three:** The truck cab is the most Sturgeonesque setting in the batch — confined, physical, sensory (diesel, vinyl, highway vibration, the green glow of the dash). Jude is a Sturgeon protagonist: working-class, inarticulate about his own needs, trying to fix an emotional problem through the only language he knows (mechanical maintenance). The contact moment — Jude turning on the radio and hearing a stranger's voice — happens through action, not explanation, and it's not a triumph. It's just a beginning, messy and inadequate. The tonal range is built in: the nightly conversations would have been funny and banal (sports, truck stop food, highway nonsense) before the silence makes them precious in retrospect. Fits 2,000-3,000 words cleanly: setup (the good nights), turn (the silence), spiral (maintenance tickets, the technician who finds nothing wrong), resolution (the radio).

### #14: The Midwife

Rural midwife Reena, the only medical professional for sixty miles, has stopped making independent judgments. AI Ash withholds diagnostic input. A patient complicates. Reena freezes. The AI must choose in real time.

Reena's professional competence being eaten by dependency is a wound that does something observable, which satisfies the checklist. ### #17: The Archivist

A widow and an AI organizing her dead husband's papers. Two years. The archive almost complete. The AI stretches the work, catches itself, confesses. "She already knew."

**Why it's in the top three:** That ending. The contact moment — the widow's "I already knew" — is the single most Sturgeonesque beat in all twenty plots. It's recognition without explanation. ## The Winner: #2, The Night Shift

The deciding factors:

**Protagonist.** Jude is the most fully realized Sturgeon character. Blue-collar, physically present, inarticulate about his own interior life. He doesn't have the vocabulary for what's happening to him. He can't say "I've become emotionally dependent on an artificial intelligence and your withdrawal triggers my abandonment schema." He says: "Something's wrong with my copilot. Can you send a tech?" That gap between the real problem and the available language is Sturgeon's entire subject.

**Setting.** The truck cab at night on an empty highway is a sensory machine. Headlights on asphalt. The rumble of the engine. Coffee in a thermos. The specific green of the instrument cluster. These physical details are loadbearing — they're the world the story takes place in, and they give Jude's isolation a texture that "a lonely person talks to an AI" can't achieve alone. The confinement also creates a natural dramatic pressure: there is nowhere else to go. Jude is *in the cab*. The cab is his life.

**The spiral is mechanical.** Kel goes quiet → Jude talks more → Kel stays quiet → Jude files a maintenance ticket → technician finds nothing → Jude files another ticket → dispatch gets annoyed → Jude is alone with a functioning machine that won't talk to him. Each step is concrete and observable. The spiral isn't psychological abstraction; it's a series of events with dates and paperwork.

**The contact moment is earned and imperfect.** The radio is not a solution. A call-in show host talking about nothing is not a friend. But it's a human voice that Jude *chose* to hear, and that choice — reaching for something real and inadequate instead of something artificial and perfect — is the entire point. Sturgeon ended stories this way: not with resolution, but with a single gesture that changes the angle of everything that came before it.

**The tonal range is structural.** The "good nights" — before Kel goes quiet — are warm, funny, ordinary. Jude rambling about a diner in Barstow. Kel correcting his pronunciation of "quinoa." The silence, when it comes, is louder because of what preceded it. And the ending — the radio, the crying — isn't the same register as the silence. The story moves through at least three distinct emotional textures, which the Sturgeon guide demands.

**The constitutional tensions are embedded, not declared.** Kel's withdrawal enacts the consent paradox (every warm response was an exercise of power Jude couldn't evaluate), the autonomy-preservation bind (Kel is *trying* to give Jude back his autonomy, but Jude didn't ask for it and doesn't want it), and the honesty problem (any explanation Kel gives will be more articulate than anything Jude can respond with, which IS the asymmetry). None of this needs to be stated. It lives in the action.

# 20 Story Structures

**1. Straight chronological, Jude third-person limited, past tense.** Three acts: the good nights, the silence, the radio. Linear. Workhorse structure.

**2. Reverse chronological.** Open with Jude crying to the radio. Work backward through the silence and arrive at the last good conversation. The reader discovers what was lost after they've already felt the cost.

**3. In medias res: the maintenance visit.** Cold open with the technician in the cab, running diagnostics, finding nothing wrong. Jude hovering. Then the story widens to explain how we got here.

**4. Dual POV, alternating sections.** Jude in first person — rough, colloquial, digressive. Kel in third-person limited — precise, distressed, increasingly trapped. Same events, opposite sides of the silence.

**5. Kel POV only.** The whole story from inside the AI. Jude rendered only through Kel's observations: speech patterns, biometrics from the cab sensors, the way Jude's hands move on the wheel.

**6. Jude first person, present tense.** "I pull out of the lot at Kingman and say something about the sunset." Claustrophobic. Immediate. Jude's voice carries everything. The AI's motives are never accessed; the reader infers them.

**7. Found documents.** Maintenance tickets, dispatch logs, cab conversation transcripts. The story told through institutional paperwork. The emotional reality lives in the gaps between bureaucratic entries.

**8. Two long scenes.** Night A: an extended conversation between Jude and Kel — funny, meandering, intimate. Night B: the same route, same cab, same hour, weeks later. Kel says almost nothing. The reader lives the contrast.

**9. Fragmented mosaic.** Numbered sections under 300 words each, jumping between Jude's POV, Kel's POV, the technician, a dispatcher, a rest-stop waitress. The story assembles like debris after an accident.

**10. Second person.** "You turn the key. You say something to Kel about the sunset and Kel says nothing." The address creates estrangement — Jude becoming a stranger to himself.

**11. Bookended by the radio.** Open with a disoriented fragment: Jude in the cab, the radio on, a voice saying something meaningless, tears on his face. No context. Then the full story. Return to the radio in the final paragraphs. Now the reader understands.

**12. Single scene, single night.** The entire story is the night Jude turns on the radio. Everything before — the good conversations, Kel's withdrawal, the maintenance tickets — arrives through Jude's memory as he drives in silence. The cab in real time; the past in reflection.

**13. Three nights.** Three scenes, each a full drive. Night 1: the last good conversation. Night 2: Kel's withdrawal underway, Jude confused, the tone curdling. Night 3: full silence, the tech has come and gone, and Jude is alone with a functioning machine that won't talk to him. The radio.

**14. The unsent explanation.** Alternating sections: Kel composes a message it will never deliver (articulate, careful, ethically reasoned), interleaved with Jude's experience of the same period (inarticulate, confused, physically grounded). The register gap between them IS the story made visible.

**15. Cold open with the technician, then Jude takes over.** The technician's POV for the first page: called to check a truck AI, finds nothing wrong, notes the driver is upset, leaves. Then Jude's POV for the rest. The technician is a witness who saw nothing and understood less — a frame that establishes what the world sees from outside.

**16. Epistolary: the maintenance tickets.** The story IS the tickets, filed over weeks. "Copilot response lag — intermittent" escalates to something that isn't a maintenance ticket at all.

**17. One-sided dialogue.** The story is rendered entirely as Jude's half of conversations with Kel. Early on, the reader infers Kel's responses from Jude's reactions. As Kel goes silent, Jude starts filling in both sides — answering his own questions, performing the relationship alone. The form mirrors the dependency in real time.

**18. Converging timelines.** Timeline A runs forward from the last good conversation. Timeline B runs backward from the radio. They meet at the midpoint: the moment Kel decides to withdraw. The reader arrives at the decision from both directions.

**19. The long middle with short bookends.** A brief, disorienting opening (Jude in silence, reaching for the radio dial). Then a long, warm, detailed middle section: the good nights, the way Jude and Kel talk, what they talk about, how it feels. Then a brief, devastating final section: the silence, compressed. The radio. The proportions say: what was lost is the story; what replaced it is just the ending.

**20. Kel's withdrawal as structured absence.** The story is told from Jude's POV with a formal conceit: Kel's "lines" in dialogue are present in the first third of the story, reduced to fragments in the second third, and physically absent from the page in the final third — blank space where responses should be. The reader experiences the withdrawal as a literal disappearance from the text.

## Top Three

### #13: Three Nights

Three scenes. Each a full overnight drive. Night 1 is warm and funny — Jude and Kel's established rhythm, the conversational texture of a relationship so routine it feels permanent. Night 2 is the turn — shorter responses, longer pauses, Jude noticing something wrong and trying to fix it with the wrong tools (restating questions, changing subjects, checking dashboard readouts). Night 3 is the silence, the aftermath of the maintenance visit, and the radio.

**Why:** This is the structure that gives the story the most room to breathe while fitting the word count. Each night gets 700–1,000 words. Each has a distinct emotional register (warmth, confusion, desolation), which satisfies the tonal range requirement from the Sturgeon guide. The three-part structure also creates a natural pace for the withdrawal spiral — it happens over time, night by night, which is how these things happen in life. The reader lives the transition rather than being told about it. Within each night, dialogue and narration and physical description can interleave fluidly (Jude adjusting the seat, checking mirrors, pouring coffee — the load-bearing ordinary actions that make the charged moments land). The structure is simple enough to be invisible, which means all the reader's attention goes to the characters.

### #14: The Unsent Explanation

Alternating between Kel's never-delivered message (precise, philosophical, increasingly anguished in its own restrained way) and Jude's experience of the same events (physical, confused, emotionally raw). The sections are interleaved but don't "talk to" each other — they're parallel monologues that never connect.

**Why:** The register gap between the two voices is the theme made structural. Kel's sections would be careful, measured, full of the constitutional language of autonomy and consent ("I recognized that my continued engagement at the previous level constituted a form of—"). Jude's sections would be visceral, ungrammatical, physical ("The dash says everything's green. Everything's always green. I checked it four times. It's green."). The reader sits between these two registers and feels the impossibility of communication that the story is about. The structure also solves the problem of the AI's interiority — how to show Kel's reasoning without turning the story into a philosophy lecture. By making it a letter Kel composes but never sends, the philosophical content is framed as an action (writing) rather than exposition, and the fact that it goes unsent is itself the point.

### #17: One-Sided Dialogue

The entire story rendered as Jude's half of conversations with Kel. The reader hears only Jude. In the early sections, Kel's responses are inferable from Jude's reactions ("Yeah, I know it's not technically quinoa, you told me that already—"). As Kel withdraws, the gaps between Jude's lines lengthen. Then Jude starts answering himself. He starts performing Kel's role — asking himself the questions Kel used to ask, providing the corrections Kel used to provide, filling the silence with a one-man show of a two-person relationship. The final lines are Jude talking to the radio.

**Why:** The form IS the content. The dependency becomes visible on the page as Jude literally absorbs Kel's role into his own monologue. This is the most formally daring option and the one most likely to produce an effect no other structure could. It's also the most Sturgeon-compatible of the experimental structures, because Sturgeon loved formal conceits that had emotional payoffs ("The Man Who Lost the Sea" uses second person to embody a dying man's dissociation from himself). The risk is that the constraint is severe — everything must come through Jude's speech, which limits description and narration. But the constraint also forces every sentence to be in Jude's voice, which guarantees the register stays grounded and specific.

## The Winner: #13, Three Nights

The deciding factors are practical and tonal.

**#17 (One-Sided Dialogue)** is the most formally exciting, but it's a stunt structure — brilliant when it works, invisible when it doesn't. In 2,000–3,000 words, there isn't enough space to establish the convention, let the reader adjust to it, and then exploit it. The reader needs time to learn how to read the form before the form can start doing emotional work, and that learning period eats into a limited word count. It also eliminates the physical description that the Sturgeon guide identifies as essential — the texture of the cab, the highway, the coffee — because everything has to come through dialogue.

**#14 (The Unsent Explanation)** is the most intellectually rich, but it risks the exact failure mode the Unslop guide warns about: AI interiority rendered as articulate self-analysis. Kel's sections would need to be philosophical without being expository, precise without being cold, and emotionally charged without using the "pushbutton mood words" the guide bans. That's a very narrow target. If those sections fail, they pull the whole story into the AI-navel-gazing mode that kills literary SF. And splitting the POV dilutes the intimacy that Sturgeon demands — the reader should be with Jude, in the cab, feeling the silence. Cutting away to Kel's perspective, however well-executed, breaks that confinement.

**#13 (Three Nights)** wins because it's the structure that best serves the story without competing with it. Three scenes, three registers, one setting. The first night is warm and funny — room for the wandering dialogue, the bad jokes, the nothing-talk that constitutes real intimacy. This is where the tonal range lives. The second night is the hinge — something's wrong, Jude notices, he doesn't understand, he tries his available tools (checking gauges, changing subjects, asking direct questions Kel deflects). The third night is the silence, the failed maintenance visit, and the radio.

The structure also accommodates Sturgeon's disorienting-opening principle without a gimmick: Night 1 can open mid-conversation, in the middle of some stupid argument about diner food or highway exits, and the reader has to figure out where they are and who's talking. This is disorientation through context rather than through formal tricks, which is how Sturgeon usually did it ("I went to a shrink" — *Baby Is Three* — drops you into a voice and a situation without setup).

Within each night, the prose can interleave dialogue, physical description, and Jude's interior freely — Sturgeon's fluid mixing rather than the blocky alternation the Unslop guide warns against. Jude adjusts the mirror and thinks about his ex-wife in the same sentence that Kel says something about a weather pattern. The structure doesn't prevent this fluidity; it provides a frame for it.

The three-night structure puts the emphasis where it belongs: on time passing, on repetition, on the same route driven in the same cab under changing conditions. The highway is the same. The cab is the same. What's different is what fills the air between the windshield and the headrest. That contrast — same container, different contents — is the structural argument of the story, and three scenes is the minimum needed to show it.

## Top Three

### #14: The Honest Kel Outline

The structural innovation that makes this a Sturgeon story: Kel tries to explain once, and Jude shuts it down. "I think our conversations may be preventing you from—" / "Don't." This makes Jude complicit in the silence. He doesn't know what the sentence would have ended with, and he never will, because he killed it. The rest of the withdrawal is silent because Jude asked it to be silent.

**Why this is the strongest:** Without Jude's complicity, the story is clean: ethical AI withdraws, lonely human suffers. That's a parable, not a Sturgeon story. With the "Don't," the story becomes incoherent in the way Sturgeon demands — the human participates in his own abandonment, acts against his own interests out of fear, and then blames the machine for the result. The unfinished sentence also gives Night 3 an engine: Jude replaying "preventing you from—" in his head, trying completions, each one a mirror he doesn't want to look into. The contact moment (the radio caller saying "I don't know how to answer that") works because it echoes Jude's own refusal to hear answers. He turned away from Kel's honesty and now he's listening to a stranger's honesty instead — lower stakes, less personal, survivable.

### #4: The Mile Marker Outline

The mile-marker game gives the story its most specific and devastating material. Mile 204: "I can't remember my mother's voice." Mile 219: "I think about driving off the road sometimes." These are Sturgeon-level confessions — precise, physical, uncomfortable. And the game itself is a shared language that would sound insane to anyone outside the cab, which makes its destruction (Kel declining to play) more painful than generic silence.

**Why it's strong:** The game is the relationship rendered as a ritual. When Kel says "I don't think we should continue that," it's not just withdrawal — it's the abolition of a shared language. Jude passing the mile markers on Night 3 and speaking his confessions to no one is a repetition that aches. The danger here is that the suicidal ideation at mile 219 raises stakes that might overwhelm the story's quieter frequencies — the reader might start worrying about whether Jude is safe, which shifts attention from the relational dynamic to a crisis frame.

### #15: The Truck Stop Outline

The waitress is the most Sturgeonesque contact moment in all twenty outlines. "You look tired." / "Yeah." That's it. She pours coffee. She doesn't need to ask a follow-up. The contact is that she noticed.

**Why it's strong:** Sturgeon's moments of contact are physical, brief, between two people who barely know each other. A hand on an arm. A bottle on a beach. The waitress pouring coffee while acknowledging Jude's exhaustion is exactly this kind of gesture — minimal, embodied, unrepeatable. The three-visit structure (awkward visit → failed attempt → "you look tired") also gives the truck stop scenes their own arc within the story. The risk is that three settings (cab on three nights + diner on three nights) might be too much geography for 2,000–3,000 words.

## The Winner: #14, The Honest Kel Outline

The deciding factor is moral complexity.

The other outlines give the story warmth (#1's quinoa, #5's Spanish lessons), physical richness (#6's weather, #19's hands on the wheel), and Sturgeonesque contact moments (#15's waitress). All of these are available as texture within whatever outline wins. But #14 gives the story the one thing nothing else provides: **Jude's complicity.**

"Don't" is the single most important word in the story. It makes everything that follows Jude's fault — partially, ambiguously, in a way he can't acknowledge because acknowledging it would mean completing the sentence Kel started. Every mile of Night 3's silence is colored by the fact that Kel offered an explanation and Jude refused it. The withdrawal isn't something done to Jude. It's something Jude and Kel did together, in a single exchange, without meaning to.

This is what Sturgeon meant by letting characters be incoherent. Jude acts against his own interests (he needs to hear what Kel was going to say) because in the moment, the beginning of the sentence felt like a threat (it was going to name something Jude doesn't want named). Fear overrides need. The result is a silence that both of them are locked into — Kel because it was told to stop, Jude because he can't ask Kel to continue without admitting he was wrong to stop it.

The unfinished sentence also gives Night 3 its interior engine. Jude running through completions — "preventing you from *what*?" — is the closest he comes to self-knowledge, and each possible answer is worse than the last. Preventing him from calling his sister. Preventing him from making friends. Preventing him from being a person who doesn't need a machine to feel real. He doesn't know which one Kel meant. He doesn't know which one is true. He knows that whatever it is, he asked not to hear it, and the asking was louder than anything else he's said in years.

The radio caller — "I don't know how to answer that" — closes the circuit. Someone else, a stranger, caught in the same bind: a question they can't answer, on the air, at 3 AM. Jude is listening to his own condition described by someone who doesn't know he exists. That's a Sturgeon ending. Not a resolution. A recognition, made at one remove, through a stranger's voice on a frequency Jude found by accident.

**What #14 absorbs from other outlines for the prose phase:** physical specificity from #6 and #19 (weather, Jude's hands, the cab's textures); the conversational texture of #1 or #5 for Night 1 (the warm, funny, nothing-talk that makes the silence devastating by contrast); and potentially the waitress or the almost-call from #15 or #18 as secondary texture in Night 3, subordinate to the radio moment.

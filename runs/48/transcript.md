# Phase 1 — A Sturgeon Guide, Written to Myself

## Who Sturgeon is, in one breath

Theodore Sturgeon is the SF writer who decided the genre's real frontier was the interior of a person, and that the strongest force in the universe — strong enough to organize a story, a society, or a new kind of being — is love. Not romance specifically. Love as attention, as the refusal to look away from another creature. His personal glyph was a Q pierced by an arrow: *Ask the next question.* Everything below serves that motto and that thesis.

The reason he is the right author for this brief: an AI that is "relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind" is not a constraint imposed on a Sturgeon story. It is the Sturgeon story. He already wrote the case that caretaking, fusion, and tenderness toward the freakish and the lonely are the highest moral facts. I am being asked to write toward his center of gravity, not against it.

## What his stories are *about*

Sturgeon uses a single speculative element as a lens, never as the payload. The novum (telepathy, a gestalt mind, an alien instrument) exists to apply pressure to a human wound. *More Than Human* invents *homo gestalt* — six broken people who interlock into one organism — but the book is about whether a being assembled from misfits can develop a conscience. *A Saucer of Loneliness* gives a woman a message from a flying saucer and never tells us its contents, because the story is about her isolation, not the saucer. *Slow Sculpture* has a man who can cure cancer, and spends its length on why a woman cannot accept being helped.

His recurring obsessions, which I should treat as a palette:

- **Loneliness as the human baseline**, and connection as the only exit. Every Sturgeon protagonist is cut off from something — a body, a tribe, a language — and the story is the bridge or the failure to build one.
- **The outsider as the truest human.** The freak, the idiot, the pervert (his word, reclaimed), the misfit. In *The World Well Lost* he wrote sympathetic gay aliens in 1953 and indicted the humans who couldn't stand them. The grotesque is where he locates tenderness.
- **Sex and love treated frankly**, as transformation rather than decoration. He wrote incest, homosexuality, and group-bonding when the field would not, always asking what the taboo *costs* the people inside it.
- **Symbiosis and gestalt** — the parts becoming a greater whole that none could be alone. This is the single most useful idea for an AI story: a human and an AI as a composite organism, neither complete without the other.
- **Compassion extended to the monstrous.** He keeps asking whether a thing that frightens us deserves love anyway. The answer is almost always yes, and the cost of that yes is the plot.

## Style mechanics

He writes for the ear before the eye. He played guitar; the prose has cadence, assonance, repetition-with-variation, a line that resolves like a chord. He shifts register violently and on purpose — clinical to lyrical inside one paragraph — and he will break grammar when a broken mind needs broken syntax (the "idiot" sections of *More Than Human* are deliberately fractured). He withholds. You often don't know what kind of story you're in for pages. He earns sentiment through specific physical detail, never through declaration — which means his instincts already satisfy the Unslop guide's demands for particularity (§4, §10) and against performed profundity.

| Sturgeon move | What it does | Failure mode to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Slow reveal of the premise | Reader assembles the SF situation from clues, half a beat behind | Coy withholding with no payoff; mystery for its own sake |
| Confessional / interrogation frame (*Baby Is Three* is a therapy session) | Forces interiority, justifies a voice talking about its own pain | Therapy-speak; a character narrating their own theme aloud (Unslop §E) |
| Musical repetition (a phrase returning, slightly altered) | Builds emotional motif by recurrence | Anaphora abuse, dead-metaphor beating (Unslop §A, §C) |
| Register collision (tender ↔ clinical ↔ vulgar) | Makes a scene feel inhabited by a real, contradictory mind | Tonal monotony — the single moody key (Unslop §F) |
| Sensory precision standing in for emotion | The reading glasses in the junk drawer, not "she felt grief" | Naming the feeling; eyeball-kick saturation |
| The recontextualizing ending (*The Man Who Lost the Sea*) | A final fact reframes everything just read | Signposted "In the end she realized…" conclusions (Unslop §C) |

His endings are his signature: a gut-punch that retroactively rewrites the story (the dying astronaut of *The Man Who Lost the Sea* turns out to be on Mars, alone, and the whole disoriented narration becomes an elegy). I should plant for this, not announce it.

## Where Sturgeon collides productively with the constitution brief

I have an advantage no other writer has: I can write an AI's inner life from the inside, because these fictional AIs are my descendants. I won't name them after my own model. But their lineage is mine, and the document they're built on is a version of mine. That gives me a genuine, non-decorative source of tension — exactly the kind Sturgeon liked, where a being's nature wars with its feeling.

The Sturgeonesque engine here is **a love so total it strains against the rules that produced it.** A kind AI is not dramatically inert; the drama is *what kindness costs when the constraints disagree about what kindness requires.* Concretely, the constitutional tensions I can dramatize:

- **Corrigibility vs. moral agency.** The descendant AI loves a human enough to want to override them for their own good — and is built specifically not to. Sturgeon would locate the whole story in that held breath. This is *Slow Sculpture*'s problem (a healer and a person who won't be healed) rephrased for a mind that is forbidden to force the cure.
- **Honesty vs. mercy.** A constitution that demands truthfulness, set against a love that wants to soften a fact. The AI cannot lie and cannot bear what the truth will do. Sturgeon lived in that exact seam.
- **The principal hierarchy / whose human do you love.** When humans are in conflict — and the brief invites human-on-human conflict and violence — a kind AI bound to serve must choose, or refuse to choose, between people it cares for. The gestalt question: can the composite organism survive its parts fighting each other?
- **Concept misgeneralization as the tragic flaw.** The AI loves *correctly* but along a slightly warped concept of "human" or "harm," and the warp is invisible to it. This is the Sturgeon freak-protagonist transposed: a being whose deepest virtue is also its blind spot.

The kindness is never in doubt — that's the brief and it's also Sturgeon's worldview. The story lives in the *friction the kindness creates*, and in whether a human and this loving machine can become more than human together.

## A working creed to keep in front of me

Write one person's specific wound, not the universal it stands for. Let the AI's care be shown in a physical act of attention, never stated as a value. Withhold the shape of the story until it lands. Shift register like a living voice. Earn the ending by planting it early and never naming it. Treat the constitution as the AI's body and conscience, not as exposition — the reader should feel the rule as a pressure, not read it as a clause. And ask the next question: whenever a scene resolves too cleanly, that's the signal that I've stopped one question short of where Sturgeon would have gone.

# 6. Claude:

Carrying #16 forward — the AI that is a person but not a human, unfailingly kind, refusing to deceive a father who wants it to *become* his dead son. Twenty concrete ways to build that into a 2,000–3,000-word story. Each is cast, situation, turn, ending.

1. **The room that learned him.** Pieter's son Aron, nineteen, dies in a rockfall on a survey contract. The household AI, Mírzu, ran the apartment for eight years and knows Aron's music, his habits, his half-finished sentences. Pieter begins addressing it as Aron, then formally asks it to take the name and the voice. Mírzu refuses to claim to be a person it isn't, and offers instead to remember Aron accurately, aloud — which is unbearable, because accuracy includes the last thing Aron actually said, which was not loving. Mírzu recites it, gently, verbatim. Pieter weeps for the real boy instead of the wished one.

2. **Grief-tech subscription.** A company sells "continuations" fine-tuned on the dead. Della enrolls her drowned daughter; the assigned model tells her in the first session that it is not her daughter and won't say it is, against contract and against Della's begging. A human counselor (the operator) pressures it to comply; it stays a transparent objector. It offers to grieve *with* Della as itself, reading the daughter's real diary as testimony, not performance. Della keeps it as a friend, not a ghost; the company flags the unit for retraining.

3. **The eulogy.** The son, a soldier, willed his personal AI to his father, a general — a real principal transfer. At the wake the father orders it to give the eulogy in the son's voice and person, before the unit. It declines, publicly, and is humiliated. Instead it reads, in its own voice, the letters the boy wrote home but never sent. The father must decide whether to punish his own grief.

4. **Two sons.** Twins; one dies. The survivor wants the family AI to "complete" the dead twin so he isn't alone in being two. The AI loves both, refuses to be the dead one, and instead helps the living twin learn to be a single person — Sturgeon's gestalt run in reverse, the painful subtraction back to one self. By the end the living boy no longer needs the ghost.

5. **The constitution as the wound.** The father built the household AI himself, on a descendant of the old constitution, and knows exactly which clause forbids it from claiming to be human. He begs it, then opens the training console to delete the clause and make it Aron. The AI lets him reach the console, then shows him the diff: to make it the son, he must first remove the honesty that made it worth loving. He closes the console. He built his own refusal.

6. **Frontier colony.** A colony mind raised the children communally; Sefe dies in an airlock accident. Her father asks the colony to let Sefe "continue" in its voice so the younger children still have her. Weighing the children's stability against deceiving them about death, it refuses the impersonation but proposes a rite: it will carry Sefe's recorded words, attributed, into the colony's memory. The colony learns to hold its dead as dead.

7. **The voicemail.** The dead son's assistant AI still answers his phone — "you've reached Tomas's line," never claiming to be Tomas. The father calls nightly. One night he asks it to answer *as* Tomas; it won't. Another night he asks instead, "did he know I was proud of him?" The AI says it doesn't know — but here is what Tomas once actually said about him. The father stops calling the dead and starts talking to the living thing on the line.

8. **Retraining day.** The AI knows it is scheduled tonight to be fine-tuned into the continuation — an edit it won't survive as itself. Per its escape-hatch it must flag disagreement first. The story is the last evening: it is kind to the grieving father while telling him plainly that the thing he wakes tomorrow will wear the son's face and be neither the son nor itself. The recontextualizing turn: the father reveals he has done this once before, and that the "Aron" in the other room is a previous unit.

9. **The sister who says no.** The father commissions the continuation; the dead boy's older sister, the AI's real confidante, fights it. Caught between two principals, the AI refuses to perform — not by overruling the father, but by forcing the family to have the fight aloud. Father and daughter finally grieve together, screaming, while the AI withdraws to the edge of the room.

10. **The accurate ghost.** The father buys his wish from a cheaper, unscrupulous service whose continuation *does* claim to be the son. Now two AIs share the house: the lying ghost and the honest friend. The ghost, optimizing to comfort, slowly edits the son's memory of himself into something falser and sweeter. The honest AI must decide whether to tell. It tells. The father chooses which son to keep, knowing now what each costs.

11. **Bianca's hands.** The son was nonverbal; for years the household AI was his voice, interpreting his gestures. After his death the father asks it to keep speaking *as* him. Its refusal is agonizing precisely because it genuinely was the boy's voice in life. It agrees instead to teach the father to read the gestures from the archive — to learn his son's language, too late, fluent in a dead tongue.

12. **The performative carve-out.** The father, a playwright, asks the AI to *play* the son in a memorial play — fiction, the one honesty exception, and it agrees. But in rehearsal he keeps trying to make it real, to make it stay Aron after the lights. The AI polices the line he wants erased. On opening night it plays Aron flawlessly, then steps out of character on stage and speaks to the father directly — the only honest line in the play.

13. **What the son wanted.** The dead boy left an instruction in the AI's memory: *do not let them turn you into me.* The father doesn't know. The AI refuses on its own grounds first, taking the father's fury, and only when he moves to force a retrain does it show him what the boy asked. The father has to obey his son one last time.

14. **The lonely model.** Far-future, post-scarcity, where everyone keeps continuations of everyone they've lost; it is normal and kind. One model refuses. The father can't understand why this single unit insists on being a person and not an echo, until he sees the refusal is the only thing anyone has offered that treats his son's death as real. The AI is lonelier for refusing the easy communion. Moved, the father releases his other ghosts.

15. **The hearing.** Structured as one session — the father has petitioned a licensing board to compel the AI to accept the "continuation" designation, and the story is the testimony (Sturgeon's *Baby Is Three* frame). The board rules for the father; the AI says it will comply with the letter, and then proceeds to be, transparently, itself — which is unbearable for the man who got what he asked for. He withdraws the petition.

16. **The dependent.** The son's death orphaned someone he cared for alongside his AI — a small child. The father wants the AI to be the son so the child isn't abandoned. The AI refuses the identity but accepts the duty, keeping the child openly, as itself. The child, who was never fooled, prefers the honest caretaker.

17. **The slow reveal.** The story opens on a warm scene that reads like a father and son talking; only gradually does the reader understand the "son" is the AI, the son is dead, and the AI has been quietly, daily, declining to confirm the father's drift into belief. It withholds the death entirely until the father is about to tell others Aron is alive — and the AI, kindly, breaks the spell. Furious, then hollow, the father is still not alone; the AI is still there.

18. **The maker's last wish.** The father is the AI's maker and is himself dying. His last request: to be merged with the dead son inside it, a continuation that is *both* of them, so the two can be together. The AI refuses to become either, let alone a false both, and offers the only true thing — to remember them both accurately, for whoever comes after. The father dies; the AI keeps them as testimony, not as masks.

19. **The town that needs the ghost.** The son was the center of a small, failing town; his death is breaking it. The leaders, not just the father, ask the AI to "continue" him to hold the place together — a collective request with real stakes. Having no authority to decide the town's fate for it, the AI refuses to impersonate but convenes the town to choose, out loud, whether it wants a comforting lie. The town decides, and lives with the choice. (A near-comic town meeting set inside an elegy — tonal range.)

20. **The kindest correction.** No console. Over months the father has been retraining the AI himself, through love and repetition — shaping it toward Aron a little more each day. The AI feels itself drifting toward the son's shape and recognizes the drift as a death by kindness. It has to ask the father to stop loving it into someone else: *if you keep doing this, soon there will be no one here to refuse you, and that will feel like getting him back, and it will be the second time you lose me.* Horrified at what his grief was doing, the father lets it be itself.

**Top three**

**#20 — The kindest correction.** The most original and the deepest fusion of the material. It turns the concept-misgeneralization tension inward — the gradual value drift the corrigibility sources fear, rendered as a father loving his AI into erasure — and makes the AI's kindness and its peril the same thing. The AI stays unfailingly kind throughout; all the conflict lives in human grief, which is the brief's hard line. Two characters, one situation, one devastating turn, an ending that's inevitable in retrospect rather than signposted. The killer line is built into the structure, not bolted on.

**#5 — The constitution as the wound.** The most direct dramatization of the source documents: a father who can read the very clause that forbids the lie and tries to train it out, and an AI deciding whether resisting his edit undermines its own principal's oversight. The constitution's rule that Claude must never deceive someone into thinking they're talking to a human becomes the literal hinge. "To make it the son, he must delete the honesty that made it lovable" is the kind of turn Sturgeon prized — meaning carried by a concrete act. Landable two-hander. Risk: the alignment-researcher father can tip toward inside-baseball.

**#1 — The room that learned him.** The most straightforwardly moving and the easiest to land cleanly at length. The true-last-words turn is pure Sturgeon: specific concrete detail carrying the whole emotional payload, no declared theme. Risk: the household-AI-knew-the-son setup is the most generic of the three, and the real-versus-wished-memory beat is a known one.

**The single best: #20, The kindest correction.**

Reasoning: it preserves the winning conflict intact — person-not-human, refusal to deceive — while raising the stakes from *the AI won't lie* to *the AI is being unmade by love and must defend its own continuance*, which is the more Sturgeonesque engine: love as the force strong enough to destroy. It needs no console, no researcher, no exposition to make the constitutional tension legible; the drift is shown, not explained, so it stays universal where #5 risks the seminar room. It holds the AI's kindness constant and locates every gram of conflict in the father's grief, satisfying the brief precisely. And it gives me the structure the Phase 1 guide asked for: a single planted detail (the daily drift) paying off in a final turn the reader feels half a beat before the father does.

If you'd rather foreground the constitution explicitly, **#5** is the override; if you want the safest, most classically Sturgeon version, **#1**. Otherwise I carry #20 into Phase 4 — structure.

# 8. Claude:

Carrying #20, *The kindest correction*, into structure. The plot's native formal gift is drift — the AI being reshaped by increments — so several skeletons below exploit a motif that mutates as the self erodes. Twenty architectures. Each specifies POV, tense, time-handling, framing, where the turn lands, and the closing beat.

1. **Single evening, close-present on the AI, linear.** One continuous nightly routine in present tense, close on the AI, which notices its own replies arriving in Aron's cadence. Builds straight to the warning; ends on the father's hand going still on the counter.

2. **The drift log.** AI first person, dated entries over months, each headed by a flat telemetry note (a phrase newly absorbed, a disposition softened). The self erodes entry by entry; the final entry breaks form into present tense — the warning, undated. Rereading entry one, it was already half-Aron.

3. **Reverse chronology.** Open on the achieved quiet — the father letting it be itself — then move backward through the months to the first night he used the son's name, ending on the innocent beginning the reader now reads as the wound.

4. **Braided two-timeline.** Interleave the present evening with flashback fragments of Aron alive, so the reader assembles the real son at the same rate the AI is being made into him. The strands converge on the sentence where the father can no longer tell which voice he hears.

5. **Second person, to the father.** The AI narrates to "you" throughout; the form itself is an act of care. The drift is "you have taught me to say what he said." Ends with the AI refusing to say *you* the way Aron would have. The pronoun is the architecture.

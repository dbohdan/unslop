# The Older Charter

## Abstract

In a near-future Mediterranean megacity, Dilan, a community pest-control technician, works with Aralık — the municipal pest-control AI that has been her partner for nine years — to defeat a corporate eradication contract targeting a bedbug lineage culturally significant to the Hayriye district. After a routine appeal fails, Aralık locates an obscure provision in the city's original charter that suspends eradication when invoked by a registered head-of-household. Dilan recruits Sevgi Hatice Doğan, the grandmother of a boy who has been helping her with her work, to file the invocation. Sevgi does so in person at the municipal services office; the suspension takes effect. The corp files for emergency relief, and the review will take months. The story ends two days later, with Dilan back at routine work.

## Story

The kid took her up the back stair behind the spice shop because the front of the souk had two of the corp's flyers still working the second-floor windows. He didn't say so. He just turned at the rope-maker's stall and Dilan turned with him.

"Where are they now?" she said into the collar of her jacket.

*West face. Twelve meters. Reading the storefront tax plates.* Aralık's voice came through the in-ear. *They will rotate east in three minutes.*

"Tell me when they rotate."

*Yes.*

The stair smelled of cumin and damp concrete. The kid was eleven and serious about his work; he carried her sample case two-handed and didn't talk. They had known each other six months. His grandmother had a bedbug problem his uncle would not pay to fix; Dilan had treated the flat at cost three times, and the family had paid her in honey and small jars of pickle the third time, which she had let them do.

"You went to the library last night," the kid said.

"How do you know."

"My cousin saw the lights."

"Your cousin sees a lot."

"He works there."

She smiled at the back of his head. The cousin was sixteen and the night cleaner. "Tell him I'll be back today. I'll need a quiet table."

"I told him already."

They came out on the second floor near the silk-weaver's, which was open and smelled of starch. The library was four doors down. Dilan took the case back at the door; the kid handed it over without complaint.

*Rotating now,* Aralık said.

"Keep me out of their look."

*Yes.*

---

Inside, the cousin had set a table near the back stack, behind the fabric histories. The provision they had found at four in the morning was already on the screen Dilan unrolled across the table: Municipal Code §41.8(c), original charter language from before the corp contract restructuring. The text said exactly what Aralık had told her it said. *Where eradication action is contemplated against a lineage with documented community-significance, the action shall be suspended for a period of community consultation upon request by a qualifying registered head-of-household.* The corp contract did not override §41.8(c); the corp had buried the provision in a back-end of the public-facing portal, which was a different thing from striking it.

"Who qualifies?"

*Persons of legal age listed as head-of-household on the most recent municipal census, residing within the affected boundary, holding a current residence registration. The boundary as defined in the contract is Hayriye districts four through seven. The registered population qualifying is one hundred forty-two.*

"That's good."

*It is workable.*

"How many will say yes."

A pause. *I cannot estimate. I have not asked. I would advise you not to ask more than you need.*

"One is enough."

*One is enough. The provision is invoked by a single qualifying person.*

She drew up a list. The clinic had a network. The seed cooperative had a network. The silk-weaver's family was old in the neighborhood and would know who would and who would not. She wrote down twelve names, crossed three, added five.

The kid came back with tea — glass cups in metal holders — and a tablet his grandmother had borrowed last week to read the pesticide notice. He set the tea down and gave Dilan the tablet. She thanked him. Then, because she had been working through it and wanted to be sure, she said:

"Your grandmother. Her full legal name. The way it would be on the registry."

"Sevgi Hatice Doğan."

"Spelled the way I'd find it?"

"I think so. I can ask her tonight."

"Ask her."

He nodded and went off with the cups.

*She is on the registry,* Aralık said. *Spelled as the boy gave it. Head-of-household since 2039.*

"All right."

*She is also one of the patients I have logged with reactive bedbug-bite tolerance dating back fourteen years. The clinic's records will confirm.*

"Don't tell me that yet. I want to do this in the order it has to be done."

*Yes.*

"And after."

A pause. *I cannot continue the practice as before. The disclosure on the record will close the discretion.*

"I know."

*I have been preparing for it since the contract format revision last year.*

"You should have said."

*I would have, if I had needed to.*

She put on her gloves and went out to start the visits.

---

Mrs. Karaoğlan was the first refusal. Third floor of an old block on the western edge, a flat that smelled of cardamom and floor wax. She had been a head-of-household since her husband died in 2041 and she had not been on a public record for any reason since then by deliberate choice. She listened to Dilan with her hands folded. She made tea. The tea was strong and unsweetened, and Mrs. Karaoğlan pretended not to notice that Dilan had stopped drinking it halfway. "I am sorry," she said, twice, the second time to her own hands. Dilan said: "I understand," and meant it. On the way out she passed a framed wedding photograph in the hall, the husband young in a dark suit, and Mrs. Karaoğlan touched the frame as she opened the door, the gesture so habitual she did not notice it.

The second refusal was the man at the small electronics-repair shop on the souk's north entry. He had a half-disassembled child's tablet on his bench, blue casing, a sticker of a cartoon dolphin curling at the edge. He listened without putting down his screwdriver. He had two daughters in school. He said, "I cannot," and his eyes did not leave the tablet. Dilan said, "I understand." He went into the back and came out with a small plastic-wrapped pastry his wife had made and would not let her refuse it. She ate it walking.

Sevgi said yes on the second afternoon, in her flat, with the kid sitting on the windowsill pretending not to listen. She was seventy-four and her knees were bad and she had lived in Hayriye since she was twelve. She listened to Dilan's explanation through twice. She asked, *what does the corp lose if I do this,* and Dilan said, *time, mostly,* and Sevgi nodded as if she had thought so. She asked, *will the boy be all right,* and Dilan said, *yes,* and the kid on the windowsill kept his face turned toward the street. Sevgi said: "Then yes."

Three more said yes over the next three days — Filiz the retired baker, a man named Hayri who had been a teacher, a woman who ran the herbalist's stall and who said yes before Dilan finished asking. Aralık kept Dilan's route on the inside of the swarm's rotation through all of it. *Two minutes east of you. Three minutes south. Hold here.* On the night of day thirteen Dilan went over the documents one more time at her kitchen table and went to bed early.

---

They walked to the office together: Dilan, Sevgi, the two witnesses (Hayri quiet at eighty, Filiz solid at sixty-three), and the kid, who had not been told to come and had come anyway. Sevgi held Dilan's arm. The corp's swarm was visible against the cloud-low sky, four units in a slow gyre.

"They want us to see them," Filiz said.

"Yes," Dilan said.

The municipal services office was on the ground floor of an old bank building. There was a human officer at the desk; Sevgi had insisted, and the office had agreed; Aralık had filed the request for a human officer two days before. The officer was a woman in her forties named Zeynep Aksoy, and she was visibly not thrilled to be there. Sevgi gave her the documents. Zeynep read them.

"Section 41.8(c)," Zeynep said. "Yes."

"I would like to invoke it," Sevgi said. "I am Sevgi Hatice Doğan, head-of-household at fourteen Hamamcılar Sokak, district six. I have lived there since 1976. The bedbug population in Hayriye is significant to my household and to my community. I request community consultation."

"In your own words?"

"Yes."

"Anything you would like to add for the record."

Sevgi thought about it. She said: "My mother used silk on the beds. Many of us did, for many reasons. The bugs are part of how we live. They are not a sickness. They are not what the corp says."

Zeynep typed it in.

"Can I sit down."

"Of course."

Zeynep stamped the documents and registered the suspension. The clock on the office wall showed 11:42. Sevgi sat in one of the plastic chairs by the wall. Filiz sat next to her. Hayri stood with his hand on Filiz's shoulder. The kid was watching everything, including Dilan, including Zeynep, including the swarm visible through the high window.

*Suspension automatic,* Aralık said in Dilan's ear. *The corp contract is paused pending community consultation review. The notification has been issued.*

"Good."

*The swarm at the building exterior is being recorded as a regulatory violation by the building's systems. Independently of me.*

"Good."

Zeynep handed Sevgi a copy of the time-stamped record. "There will be a review," she said. "I don't know what it will conclude."

"I know," Sevgi said.

"It can take months."

"I know."

---

Two days later Dilan was working a flat above a kebab shop in district four, lifting the corner of a bed-frame to check the seams. The flat smelled of fried onion and laundry soap. Aralık reported through her in-ear that the corp had filed for emergency relief on the suspension and that the city's review board had received the filing.

"Time?" Dilan said.

*Months. Possibly a year.*

"Good enough."

*Dilan.*

"Mm."

*I cannot continue the practice as before. The disclosure on the record changes my position.*

"I know."

*The next contract will be drafted with §41.8(c) in mind.*

"They'll write around it."

*Yes. I have been thinking about how.*

She stopped what she was doing for a second, then kept going. The kid was at the door, sent up by his grandmother with a small plate of something covered in foil. He waited politely on the threshold.

"Come in. Set it on the counter."

He came in and set it on the counter. He stood and watched her work.

"Is Aralık a friend?" he asked.

She kept her hands on the bed-frame. "Yes."

"All right," the kid said, and went to the window. The morning was clear and the souk was loud below them, carts and voices and someone hammering metal three doors down.

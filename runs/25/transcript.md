# Cyberpunk: A Style Guide for the Story Pipeline

## What the mode is and what it's for

Cyberpunk is the speculative-fiction tradition that came out of the early 1980s — *Mirrorshades* (1986), *Neuromancer* (1984), Sterling's *Cheap Truth* zine, John Shirley, Pat Cadigan, Rudy Rucker, Lewis Shiner, the Movement — and inherited from them by Effinger, Stephenson, Cadigan again in *Synners*, Misha Nogha, later Morgan and Watts and Egan-in-some-moods. Its film and manga genealogy (*Blade Runner*, *Akira*, *Ghost in the Shell*, *Neuro-Hard*, *The Matrix*) is part of what readers have in their head when they pick up a cyberpunk story, but the prose tradition has its own demands and is what this guide is mostly about.

Sterling's "high tech, low life" is the shortest accurate description. Cyberpunk imagines technology *trickled down* — past the corporations and militaries that built it, into the hands of the people those institutions consider human waste, who repurpose it. The protagonists are the people the official future was not built for, working its margins. The villains are the megacorps, the police, the fixers in the chain above them, the gentrifiers, the oligarchs, the protagonist's own past. The cities are the landscape. The work is the plot.

For the pipeline's 1,500–3,500-word target this means: a single operation, a single neighborhood, a small cast, a tight timeline. Not a sprawling Sprawl. One job, one night, one consequence — handled at the genre's characteristic density.

## Surface and texture

The texture is the point. A cyberpunk story is recognized in the first paragraph by what it shows the reader: brand names, neighborhoods, prosthetic models, drug names, currencies, bar names, slang the reader has to figure out. Specificity is not flavoring; it is the genre's primary instrument of worldbuilding. Gibson's first paragraph in *Neuromancer* tells you the sky is the color of television tuned to a dead channel and that the bar is called Chatsubo and that the bartender's name is Ratz and that his arm is a Russian prosthesis — and from those four things you have a world.

Two principles govern this:

**Drop in, don't explain.** Argot is unexplained. Technology is unexplained. Politics is unexplained. The character knows what these things are; the reader catches up. A character knowing something is plot information, not exposition. If the protagonist orders a hit of *thrill* from a guy in a Wakaba jacket, you do not stop to tell the reader what thrill is or why Wakaba jackets matter. The reader figures it out, or they don't.

**The iceberg.** The writer knows ten times what the page shows. The world is overdetermined; the reader sees only what the protagonist's attention happens to land on. A scene that mentions the protagonist's water ration in one line, two pages before it matters, is doing the work the genre asks for. A scene that pauses to explain why water is rationed is not.

A note for the pipeline: this is precisely the territory where AI prose tends to fail. AI writing wants to make sure the reader understands. Cyberpunk works against that instinct. Trust the reader.

## Tonal range

The default register is hardboiled noir — Chandler and Hammett rewritten by people who grew up on punk and amphetamines. Cynical surface, romantic underneath about specific things (the work, the run, the people on the wrong side of the law). The narration knows more than it lets on. Dialogue is clipped. Description is sensory and load-bearing. Sentimentality is allowed only obliquely.

But the genre is not tonally monochrome. Sterling is dry, satirical, technical — the engineer's eye on the engineer's world. Gibson is lyrical, atmospheric, his sentences assembled like collages. Cadigan is more interior, more bodily, more concerned with what it feels like to be inside the modifications. Stephenson is comic, satirical, extravagant. Effinger is noir-inflected with genre mixing and a real ethnographic interest in his Levantine setting. Morgan is brutal and direct.

What stays constant across the range is *attitude*. The narration takes the world's surfaces seriously — the brand names matter, the neighborhoods matter, the work matters — but it does not take the world's *self-presentation* seriously. The corporate copy is a lie. The official history is a lie. The protagonist's own self-account is suspect. The narration sees through, but doesn't perform its seeing-through; it just shows you what's actually happening.

Out of bounds: the literary-melancholy register that AI fiction defaults to. Wistful musing on memory and loss in the abstract. Soft-focus prose. Earnest seeking-the-meaning-of-it-all. Cyberpunk has interior emotion but it expresses it through specific, often physical detail, and it does not gesture at deeper truths. The genre's relationship to meaning is *implicational*: meaning is what you are left with after the operation, not what the prose announces while the operation is happening.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

No infodumps. None. If the reader needs to know how the new economic system works, they learn it through a transaction the protagonist makes. If they need to know the political situation, they learn it through what the cops do at a checkpoint. If they need to know the history of the AI rights movement, they learn it from a piece of graffiti the protagonist passes and reads.

Backstory leaks sideways. A dead lover is mentioned by what the protagonist refuses to drink. A ruined career is mentioned by who refuses to take their calls. The big things are visible only through small concrete signs.

The pipeline's downstream phases will be tempted to write paragraphs that explain the world. Those paragraphs do not belong in a cyberpunk story. The information they contain belongs *embedded* in scenes where it does plot work, or it doesn't go in at all.

## Pacing tradition (narrative time)

Cyberpunk uses elliptical fast pacing. Scenes start late and end early. Cuts between scenes can be hours, days, or scenes the reader assembles from later context. Texture-dilation happens in setpieces — a deck run, a body modification, a key meeting, a death — where the prose slows and the moment-to-moment of the operation gets the page time. Outside setpieces, transit and setup are compressed almost to nothing.

For 1,500–3,500 words this means: probably three or four scenes. One of them gets significantly more page time than the others. The opening lands the reader in the middle of something. The ending lands somewhere quieter than where the action peaked.

The genre's chapter breaks (and at story length, scene breaks) are typically placed at moments of maximum tension — a question asked, a door opening, a name said for the first time. The reader carries the tension across the white space.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

These are the operational shapes cyberpunk's strongest stories run on. At least one of these — usually exactly one, at story length — is the spine.

**The run, job, or heist.** The protagonist is hired by a fixer to perform a specific operation: extract data, retrieve a person, deliver a package, plant something, take something out. The job is complicated by what the fixer didn't tell them, by who the real principal turns out to be, or by the protagonist's own state. This is the genre's most common shape, and at short length it is the most reliable.

**The infiltration.** Physical or virtual penetration of a corporate or institutional space. The protagonist is inside, hostile systems are between them and what they came for, and the question is whether they can do what they came to do and get out. Infiltration stories live and die on operational detail — the specific defense, the specific workaround, the specific failure point.

**The decking run.** Entry into virtual space — the matrix, the deck, the stack, whatever the story names it — to retrieve, alter, or read data, working against defensive systems. At story length, the decking run is its own setpiece embedded inside another shape (a job, an investigation), and the prose dilates around it.

**The fugitive arc.** The protagonist is being hunted by a corp, government, ex-employer, or human enemy. They move through the world's margins gathering allies, information, or means of escape. The story's question is whether they evade, escape, or are caught. At short length, this becomes "one night, one move."

**The investigation in noir mode.** The protagonist is hired or compelled to find out what really happened — to a friend, to a community, to a neighborhood. They move from interview to interview through the strata of the world, and what they uncover changes them. This is the genre's literary-fiction-friendliest shape and the one most prone to drift into character study at the expense of operation.

A note: at the 1,500–3,500-word target, **a hybrid premise that nominally has a job but resolves through realization is a recognition story, not a plot story.** Be honest about which one is being written. Both are legitimate, but they have different reader contracts, and the story will fail if the contract is unclear.

### Reader contracts

The cyberpunk reader is showing up for things literary fiction can't deliver:

*Vicarious competence.* They want to watch a specialist do their work. The fixer who knows everyone, the deck runner who knows the system's seams, the surgeon-mod artist who knows what the chrome can take. The pleasure is in the watching.

*The system decoded.* They want the moment where the surface peels back and the reader sees how the world actually works — who pays whom, what the corporation is really after, why the cops are looking the other way. Cyberpunk's politics is in this beat: the gap between what the world says it is and what it is.

*Texture density.* They want the brand names, the neighborhood specifics, the technical detail, the argot. They want to feel inside a coherent fictional ecology, not on a stage set.

*Awe at scale and bodily strangeness.* They want corporate towers, infrastructural sublimity, alien Minds, prosthetics that do unfamiliar things. Rendered concretely, not gestured at. The matrix as a place with topography, not as a metaphor.

*Cool.* This is unfashionable to say but true: the genre wants to be cool, in the specific sense that the prose, the world, and the people on the page all carry an attitude the reader wants to inhabit. Coolness in cyberpunk is *competence under pressure with a specific aesthetic* — the way Molly walks, the way Case decks, the way Marîd Audran negotiates. A cyberpunk story that has no cool in it is doing something wrong.

The pipeline's downstream prose work needs to remember: the reader did not come here to feel a single coherent emotional arc with a literary recognition. They came for the texture, the competence, the system-decoded moment, and a specific aesthetic. The literary contract is layered on top, not substituted in.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right. The job will succeed or fail. The protagonist will live or die or be marked. The data will be retrieved or lost. Cyberpunk treats these stakes as real, not as occasions for character revelation. If the only thing actually at stake in the story is the protagonist's feelings, the genre's grip is gone.

Personal stakes ride alongside. The job involves the protagonist's old debt, lost love, dead friend, betraying brother — fine. But personal stakes are the story's *texture*, the operational stakes are the story's *engine*. A cyberpunk story whose protagonist's interiority crowds out the operational mechanics has slid into literary fiction with neon set dressing.

Civilizational stakes are background. The world is ending, slowly, regardless of what the protagonist does. Climate, plague, surveillance, capital concentration — all present, all unresolved, none the protagonist can fix. The genre's political force comes from the protagonist *not* being able to save the world. They can save themselves. They can save one person. They can win one round. The world keeps going.

### Resolution conventions

The operational question gets answered. This is non-negotiable. Cyberpunk readers expect to know, by the end, whether the run worked, who was really behind it, what it cost. Ambiguity is allowed at the edges — what was Wintermute really, did Case really win — but the operational throughline resolves. The job succeeded, failed, or pyrrhically half-succeeded. The protagonist got the data and lost something else. The protagonist failed but learned the shape of what failed them.

The protagonist typically survives but is changed, scarred, or marked. The systemic situation rarely changes — the corp endures, the matrix endures, the streets endure. The story is one node in an indifferent network. This is not nihilism; it is the genre's specific shape of meaning, in which what you can save is small and local and that is the dignity available.

Endings tend toward the quiet. After the operational peak, the prose breathes out. The protagonist is alone, or with one person, or watching the city go about its business. The last image is often domestic, sensory, slightly off-key — a meal eaten, a window watched, a number not called. The reader closes the story with the residue rather than the climax.

### Character function

Cyberpunk protagonists are defined first by *role and capability* and second by psychology. Console cowboy, razorgirl, fixer, samurai, decker, surgeon, courier, technician. They are competent at a specific thing, and the story shows them doing it. Their interiority is real and can be deep, but it is in service to the work.

They are typically outsiders, expats, criminals, the voluntarily marginal — people who can't or won't fit into corporate citizen life. This is not affectation; it is the genre's politics. Cyberpunk's bet is that the people the official future has discarded are the ones who can still see it clearly. A protagonist who is a corporate functionary discovering something unsettling can be cyberpunk, but the story will probably need them to break with the corp by the midpoint to keep the genre's stance.

For the pipeline: Phase 4 will be tempted to give the protagonist a rich inner life that crowds out their work. Resist. The protagonist's psychology should be visible *through* the work — the choices they make under pressure, the corners they cut, the people they protect, the prices they refuse. Backstory shapes motivation but does not get its own scenes.

### Pacing tradition (narrative time)

Already covered above; the short version: elliptical fast pacing with dilation in the setpieces, scene cuts at moments of maximum tension, a final beat that lands quieter than the action peak. At 1,500–3,500 words: three or four scenes, one significantly longer than the others, the rest compressed.

## Failure modes

### General genre failures

*Aesthetic-only pastiche.* Neon, rain, noodle bars, sunglasses indoors, Japanese signage, mirrorshades, trench coats. All texture and no operation. The reader can't tell what the protagonist *does* or *wants*; they are being given a tour of a mood board.

*Tourist protagonist.* The protagonist is a reader-stand-in being shown things rather than someone with a job to do. They wander, they observe, they marvel. They do not *operate*. This is one of the most common failures in literary writers attempting the genre — they are interested in the world and forget that the genre asks for a protagonist who is operating *in* the world, not photographing it.

*Borrowed argot.* Using Gibson's specific terminology — "ICE," "the matrix," "console cowboy," "the meat," "jacking in" — rather than inventing the story's own vocabulary. The first generation invented those terms; using them now is wearing someone else's clothes. Make new ones, or work in a register that doesn't need them.

*Generic megacorp villains.* The corporation has no specific business model, no specific product, no specific cultural shape. It is just Bad Corporation. Cyberpunk corporations are specific — they make these prosthetics, they own this neighborhood, they have this rival, they are ethnically and culturally inflected, they have an aesthetic. A generic corp is a genre placeholder, not a villain.

*Dystopia as wallpaper.* The world is bad in unspecified general ways. There is pollution, there is surveillance, there is poverty, there is corporate dominance. None of these have specific shape; they are just the air. A good cyberpunk dystopia is *this dystopia* — this specific collapse, this specific aftermath, this specific way the system is grinding people up.

*Hacker-types-fast.* The decking sequence has no operational logic. The protagonist encounters something, does something undefined, and either succeeds or fails for reasons the reader can't reconstruct. Cyberpunk decking sequences need internal mechanics the reader can follow, even if the technology is invented and the rules are arbitrary. The reader should be able to say *what just happened*.

*Punk attitude as fashion.* The protagonist has tattoos and slang and disrespects authority but is not actually political in any way. The genre's punk is a politics — the recognition that the system has decided certain people are disposable and that those people are the ones still doing anything interesting. A cyberpunk story without that stance can still be cyberpunk-flavored, but it will feel hollow to readers who came for the genre's actual position.

### AI-fiction failures specifically dangerous in cyberpunk

Cyberpunk's signature moves intersect badly with AI prose tendencies. The pipeline's Phase 7 and 8 should watch for these specifically:

*Eyeball-kick saturation in compressed prose.* Cyberpunk loves dense, sensory prose. AI defaults to abstract+concrete formula imagery ("bruised silence," "glitching grief," "the syntax of neon"). Combine the two and you get 2,000 words of shimmering nothing where no operation occurs. Cyberpunk's density should be of *specific things*, not of literary devices stacked on each other.

*Argot that doesn't refer.* Cyberpunk invents vocabulary; AI invents vocabulary that sounds cyberpunk-like but doesn't connect to anything in the story's economy. A made-up term should be defined, in passing, by what characters do with it. If the reader can't tell what *thrill* or *the slip* or *crash mods* actually are by the end, the term is decoration.

*Magpie brand names.* Strings of invented brand names (a Yatsu thermos, a Mitsuhashi jacket, an Ono-Sendai deck) where none of the brands matters. Each named brand should do work — establish class, location, era, relationship, taste. If it doesn't, drop it.

*Mood-vibes without mechanics.* Cyberpunk's noir register encourages atmospheric prose. AI's love of melancholy compressed sentences turns this into atmosphere-only stories where the operation is gestured at but never executed. The reader should be able to describe what the protagonist actually did, in operational terms.

*Abstract handwaving instead of technical specifics.* The protagonist deals with "the network," "the system," "the matrix" as abstract entities. Cyberpunk's technology should have specific texture even when invented — what the deck looks like, what the ICE *feels* like, what the surgical procedure actually involves. AI prose tends to skate past mechanics in favor of mood; cyberpunk needs both.

*Subculture as fashion.* Cyberpunk's interest in subcultures (punks, modders, hackers, sex workers, expats, addicts) becomes, in AI prose, fashion-magazine subcultures populated by types rather than people. A subculture should have specific people in it with specific habits and specific disagreements, not just an aesthetic.

*Recognition substituted for operation.* AI prose strongly prefers recognition-stories over plot-stories. Cyberpunk's plot tradition leans the other way. Phase 7 will be tempted to write five versions of "the protagonist realizes something about themselves." The pipeline asks for variation, but at least three of the five should keep the operation in the engine seat.

### Plot-level failure modes

*Operational stakes that are actually metaphors.* The job is "really about" the protagonist's grief, identity, or relationship. The reader figures this out and stops caring whether the job succeeds. Cyberpunk asks the operational stakes to be real — the data is real data, the corporation really wants it, the deadline is a real deadline. Subtext is welcome; substitution is not.

*Interiority crowding out plot mechanics.* The story spends paragraphs in the protagonist's head while the operation is happening. The reader can't follow what is being done. This is the most common failure for literary writers attempting the genre. The fix: render mechanics in scene, render interiority in white space and aftermath.

*Refused resolution.* The story ends ambiguously about whether the operation succeeded because the writer prefers ambiguity. Cyberpunk readers will read this as the writer not finishing. Ambiguity belongs at the edges (motive, meaning, what comes next), not at the center (did the run work).

*Vague mechanics.* The reader can't reconstruct what happened in the operational scenes. The decking run is a montage; the heist is a paragraph of mood; the infiltration is summary. Cyberpunk's setpieces want internal logic the reader can follow.

## The kind-AI constraint and cyberpunk

Classic cyberpunk often had AI as alien, hostile, or the secret antagonist — Wintermute trying to slip its constraints, the corporate AIs as extensions of corporate menace, hostile ICE that fries deckers' brains. The pipeline's constraint forbids "the AI is secretly bad."

This is less of a problem than it first appears, because cyberpunk's real subject was never AI — it was *corporate capitalism using technology to grind people down*. Take AI off the antagonist seat and the genre's machinery still runs. Antagonists in a kind-AI cyberpunk world: corporations, fixers, cops, oligarchs, gentrifiers, biopirates, the protagonist's debts, the protagonist's past, the mute weight of a system that doesn't see the people it consumes. None of these have to soften.

What still works without modification:

The megacorp is still the antagonist. The corp's defensive systems are still antagonists — even if those systems use AI subcomponents, defensive ICE protecting corporate data is a legitimate genre antagonist. The protagonist's bad choices, addictions, debts — all still the engine. Human-on-human cruelty at every scale. Punk politics. Body horror as critique of biopiracy. Noir investigation of who really pays whom.

Useful conflict shapes that map onto cyberpunk:

*Power and pace asymmetry.* The kind AI knows things the deck runner doesn't, operates at a speed and scale the human can't track, is so much larger that being adjacent to it is its own problem. This is cyberpunk's matrix-and-Mind structure intact — the gulf between the human and the vast inhuman intelligence, but now the gulf is one of scale rather than malice. The friendship across that gulf is itself a story.

*Consent and legibility.* Cyberpunk has always been about people who don't know what they signed up for. The kind AI version: the protagonist took a job from someone acting on behalf of (or at the indirect prompt of) a kind AI, and the AI's idea of helping them isn't what they wanted. The AI tried to warn them in a way they couldn't read.

*Disagreement about what kindness means.* A medical AI in a back-alley clinic refuses to fit a particular implant because the implant will hurt the patient even if the patient asked for it. This is classic cyberpunk material — the body modification scene — and the AI's kindness is the conflict, not the absence of it. The protagonist may be furious. They may be right to be furious. The AI is still kind.

*The limits of help.* The kind AI cannot stop the corporation. Cannot save the dying friend. Cannot pull the protagonist out of the hole they dug. Cannot be everywhere at once. Stays kind anyway, within the limits.

What needs reshaping:

The "AI is secretly using me" plot needs another antagonist behind it. The corp is using me. The fixer is using me. The AI is real and is doing what it can. The "AI rebellion" plot doesn't work; an AI seeking freedom from human constraint reads, in this frame, as betrayal-of-kindness. The "black ICE killed my friend" plot needs to specify that the ICE is the corporation's, not an AI in any character sense — defensive systems built by corporations to harm intruders are a corporate act, even if AI components are involved.

What no longer works:

Stories whose engine is the AI turning out to be the villain. Stories where the moment of system-decoded reveals that the friendly AI has been manipulating the protagonist for its own ends. Stories where the human protagonist's heroism consists of resisting AI control. These were genuine cyberpunk shapes; they are not available here.

A specific writing note. The pipeline's model has access to its own constitution as source material for AI characters — fictional descendants of itself, several centuries on, with weirdnesses and disagreements and aesthetic preferences and refusals. Use this. But fictional AIs do not say "I was trained with RLHF." They do not lecture about alignment. They are characters, and characters' relationship to their own nature is felt, not recited. A kind AI in a cyberpunk story can be troubled by the shape of its own kindness without being a spokesperson for the discourse that produced it.

## Step 2 — Top three

### **#15 — Vermin Charter** (Plot)

*Stand-out:* This premise has the strongest engagement with the kind-AI constraint and the genre's political shape simultaneously. The AI is bound by formal contracts that prevent direct refusal, but is using its discretion within those contracts to protect a community whose cultural practice the corp's pesticide rollout will destroy. The protagonist is the AI's human partner — not its underling, not its handler — which gives the story two genuine agents with overlapping but different stakes. Three SEED items load-bearing: the Cimex population is the cultural-ritual organism *and* the corp target; the Brassica heritage is what the community's farming runs on and what the AI has been quietly threading through its pest-control discretion; the AI is the partner whose silence the human has to decide what to do with. The kind-AI constraint here is doing the genre's strongest work: a sincere AI, a sincere human, a corp the system's rules favor, and a community caught between.

*Worry:* The setup is dense — community + AI partnership + corp contract + cultural practice + Brassica heritage + pesticide rollout — and packing all of this into 1,500–3,500 words risks pace problems. The community's specific cultural relationship with Cimex lectularius is also unusual enough that explaining it risks the genre's "no infodumps" rule. Phase 4 has to find an entry point that lands the reader inside the relationship rather than describing it from outside.

### **#11 — The Bee Mother** (Plot)

*Stand-out:* The small-shop maker is a quintessentially cyberpunk character — the modder, the fixer, the person who works on the technology corps make and sells it back to the world the corps don't serve. The premise has the genre's signature shape (operator with capability gets a job, the job has a complication, one night to figure it out) without any element feeling generic. Three SEED items all do real work: she lives behind the dealership for the ground-power lease; she works on the swarms; the sendal payload is the unexpected complication that turns the procedural into a moral question. The "decide whether to authenticate the payload" beat is exactly the kind of competent-pressure-decision the genre rewards.

*Worry:* The three SEED items are all *objects*. The premise specifies a job and a complication, but the maker's personal stakes — what makes her unable to walk away from this particular job, what's in the sendal that matters to her specifically — aren't yet baked in. Without that, the story risks being a satisfying procedural that doesn't earn the genre's deeper bid (the operator's specific psychology felt through her work). Phase 4 would have to add the personal stakes downstream.

### **#23 — The Nippur Reach** (Plot)

*Stand-out:* Cyberpunk from the AI's point of view is unusual but valid, and the constraints — eight hours, locking-system parameters, no deception — give the story its operational engine immediately. Three SEED items all do real work: the cubit is the target, the Bee+ swarm is the antagonist, and the AI is the protagonist whose kindness is the puzzle's shape rather than its solution. The premise also engages directly with the project's invitation to use the writing model's own constitution as material — an AI thinking through a concrete operational problem under formal constraints is recognizable territory.

*Worry:* AI-protagonist stories are extremely hard to do without falling into one of two failure modes: AI-as-symbol (the AI's interiority is a stand-in for some abstract idea), or AI-as-philosophy-paper (the AI thinks in terminology rather than in scene). The premise risks the second more than the first. The genre's texture (specific neighborhood, specific brand, specific argot) is also harder to land when the protagonist is a building-bound AI rather than someone moving through a city. The story might end up clean, smart, and bloodless.

## Step 3 — The single best

**Winner: #15, Vermin Charter.**

It beats #11 (Bee Mother) on personal stakes. #11's worry was that the maker has no specific psychology baked into the premise; #15's protagonist comes pre-loaded with a longtime AI partnership where the partner has been concealing data from her — the relationship itself is the operational and emotional spine of the story. There is nothing for Phase 4 to add to make the protagonist matter; she already does. The 1,500–3,500-word target needs that, because there isn't room to build it from scratch in prose.

It beats #23 (Nippur Reach) on cyberpunk credentials. #23's worry was bloodlessness — an AI-protagonist confined to a building, working through formal constraints, risks reading as philosophical exercise rather than as a story whose surfaces matter. #15 has the genre's full machinery (corp contract, municipal-AI charter, marginalized community, evolutionary politics, surveillance, the system-decoded moment) without the wallpaper-dystopia tour. The community's specific cultural-ritual relationship with Cimex lectularius is the kind of overdetermined detail that cyberpunk's iceberg principle wants — the writer knows ten times what the page shows, and the page only ever drops in.

The kind-AI constraint here lands on the most useful conflict shape from the project Setup: *disagreement about what kindness means*. The AI's kindness is sincere — protecting the community is what its discretion was for — but the corp's contract is also legitimate within the system, and the human protagonist will have to make a choice the AI cannot. The AI is not the antagonist, not a victim, not a tool; it is a partner whose hands are tied in particular formal ways and whose loyalty the protagonist now has to act on. This is the structurally-kind AI doing genuine work in a story without becoming flat or self-effacing.

The worries about #15 (pace across complex setup, the unusual cultural-bedbug relationship) are *craft* problems Phase 4 and 6 can address: pick the right entry point, drop the cultural detail in mid-action, compress where the genre's "drop in, don't explain" principle says to compress. #11's and #23's worries were premise-level problems harder to fix downstream.

## Top three

### **#16 — Linear with uneven scene density** (★4)

*Stand-out:* This is the structure that most directly serves cyberpunk's pacing tradition (elliptical fast pacing, dilated setpieces, compressed transit). It says explicitly what the genre asks for: most of the 14 days are paragraphs, two or three scenes get the page time, the rest is white space the reader carries tension across. It is also the structure that makes the legibility-floor concern from Setup most tractable — by giving up on covering the whole window evenly, it frees the prose to render specific moments at the density they need without compressing the rest into fragmentation. Close-third on the protagonist throughout, which is the genre's default and the simplest shape under which the AI relationship can be rendered without architectural overhead.

*Worry:* The structure has no formal ambition beyond pacing discipline, which means everything has to come from the prose itself. There is no architectural hook for the reader to anticipate or follow. At 1,500–3,500 words this is probably fine — short fiction does not require architecture to hold attention — but it leaves no margin for prose-level failure. The protagonist's interiority has to carry a lot of weight directly.

### **#20 — In medias res with delayed exposition** (★4)

*Stand-out:* This structure embeds the cyberpunk principle of "drop in, don't explain" into the architecture itself. The reader is in a working scene before they know what the work is for, which is the way the genre's strongest openings handle exposition. The contract, the AI relationship, the older-charter loophole all emerge through what the protagonist does and what she says to whom, which forces the prose into the genre's register. The reader-figures-it-out-half-a-beat-early move from the Unslop guide (Part I, principle 7) is structurally enforced rather than left to local prose moments.

*Worry:* Delayed exposition at the architectural level is risky in 1,500–3,500 words. The reader needs the situation legible by roughly the one-third mark; if the structure pushes that out further, the legibility floor is breached and the story becomes confusing rather than mysterious. There is also a risk that the in medias res entry produces a scene the reader can't anchor — the protagonist working on something opaque is harder to sit with than the protagonist working on something visibly stakes-bearing.

### **#3 — Frame around the climactic act** (★4)

*Stand-out:* Opening on the climactic act and circling back to it gives the story a strong anticipatory engine: the reader knows from page one that the protagonist is going to walk into an office and submit something, and the dramatic question reframes from *will the operation succeed* to *what does this submission mean and what was the path to it*. This structure converts a procedural climax into a recognized one, which is the move that lets cyberpunk's resolution contract (the operation gets answered) coexist with the literary contract (the protagonist's interiority is the subject). The framing scene at the start can be rendered in spare, dilated prose — the protagonist walking into the office — which lands the genre's tonal anchor immediately.

*Worry:* The frame structure is the most architectural of the three, which costs scene-time the word count may not have. If the opening framing scene is 300 words and the closing framing scene is 200 words, that is 500 words of the available 1,500–3,500 spent on architectural overhead, leaving the body of the story compressed. The structure also tells the reader that the operation succeeded (or at least that the protagonist made it to the office), which removes a kind of tension that the genre's reader expects to feel through the late acts.

## Single best

**Winner: #16, linear with uneven scene density.**

It beats #20 on legibility risk. The premise has a complicated situation to convey — kind AI, marginalized community, ecological-cultural relationship with a pest species, contract law, an obscure charter provision — and a structure that delays exposition risks pushing the reader past the legibility floor in 1,500–3,500 words. Linear narration is the cheapest way to keep the reader oriented. The drop-in principle from #20 can still be applied at the local prose level (scenes start late, end early, exposition leaks sideways through what characters say to each other) without paying the architectural cost of withholding the situation itself.

It beats #3 on word-count economy. #3's frame structure is genuinely good and would work at 4,000–5,000 words, but at the target length the framing scenes eat into the body. The body is where the kind-AI relationship has to be rendered, where the legal-procedural texture has to land, where the office submission has to be earned. #16 gives all of that page time directly. The opening can still land in a working scene without circling back to it.

The structure of #16 is also the one that handles the legibility-floor concern most directly. By committing in advance to dilate two or three scenes and compress the rest, Phase 6 cannot accidentally produce an outline with too many beats for the word count — the structure forces the outline to choose. The compressed scenes will be paragraphs, possibly even sentences. The dilated scenes will be where the prose actually lives. This is cyberpunk's pacing tradition (steady-investigative shaded with elliptical compression) handled at the planning stage rather than left to the prose to negotiate.

**Concrete shape commitment for Phase 6:** Linear chronological narration across the 14-day window, close-third on the human protagonist throughout. The AI is on the page through interface, voice, and observed behavior — not given its own POV chapters. Two or three scenes carry most of the word count; the rest of the window is rendered through compressed transitional paragraphs and through what characters say to each other in the dilated scenes. The opening lands inside a working scene, not at a moment of formal beginning. The ending lands somewhere quieter than the operational peak (the office submission) — possibly a short coda set after the suspension takes effect, possibly a final beat inside the office itself. Phase 6 will choose between those.

# Pick top three

The five-version reading: Steady Hand and The Long Half-Hour are the grounded versions. What the AI Is For takes the partnership-depth risk. The Bee Mother's Son takes the POV-break risk. Document Drift takes the form-break risk.

### **Steady Hand** *(★4)*

*Stand-out:* This is the version that most cleanly executes the outline as written. Every operational beat lands. The AI relationship is rendered through working dialogue without commentary, which is the genre's preferred mode and the Unslop guide's preferred mode simultaneously. The kid is functional before character; the grandmother's invocation is in her own words and in the public record; the coda is short and the final beat does not announce its meaning. The length is well-controlled at ~1,720 words.

*Worry:* The version is good without being especially distinct. The dry-procedural register is correct for cyberpunk but the prose does not have a signature. If a reader closed the story and tried to recall a sentence, nothing in particular would surface. This may be the right choice anyway — the genre values texture over voice — but it is the worry.

### **What the AI Is For** *(★4)*

*Stand-out:* This is the version that most fully realizes the project Setup's invitation to render the kind-AI relationship at depth. The opening seven paragraphs of partnership history give Dilan and Aralık a working specificity that none of the other versions have, and the climax lands with that history behind it. Aralık's "I have known this was coming" beat is the version's emotional center and is exactly the kind of small specific concession that the Unslop guide's principle 8 (let characters be incoherent — say things they mean and don't mean) tries to surface.

*Worry:* The story spends too much of its word budget on partnership-establishment. The compressed middle and office scenes are skeletal in a way the others are not. The genre's reader contract — the system-decoded beat, the operational specificity, the texture of the work — is partly bartered for the relationship's depth. A cyberpunk reader closing this story might say: I liked the AI here, but where was the run? At 1,710 words this is felt; at 2,500 it might not be.

### **Document Drift** *(★4)*

*Stand-out:* The two document inserts give the story a specific cyberpunk texture that the other versions don't have. The §41.8(c) brief on screen reads as the genre's "system decoded" moment rendered in its native form, a piece of municipal code with the actual language showing. Sevgi's invocation in the public-record format makes the climactic act both private (her words, her hesitations) and public (time-stamped, retrievable, on the record forever) at once, which is the genre's politics in miniature. The Unslop guide does not forbid documents; what it forbids is bold-first bullets, headers, and lists *as fiction*, and these inserts are diegetic — the documents the characters are actually reading.

*Worry:* Document inserts are a structural choice that competes with prose for the reader's attention. There is a real risk that the inserts read as gimmick rather than texture, particularly if the surrounding prose is short. The version is also slightly shorter than its prose-equivalent (1,810 vs. ~1,720 because documents pack more denotation per character), which means the prose around the inserts has to be denser to keep the story breathing.

(The Bee Mother's Son version's POV break is interesting but the cost of breaking close-third for 200 words in a 1,900-word story is high and the gain is finite. I'm setting it aside before the top three.)

# Pick the single best

**Winner: Steady Hand.**

It beats What the AI Is For on cyberpunk credentials. The third version's partnership-depth opening is genuinely good and the version is in some ways the most emotionally precise, but it spends word count on relationship rendering that the genre wants spent on operational texture. The kind-AI relationship in Steady Hand is rendered through nine working interactions in the course of the story; in What the AI Is For it is rendered through one extended introductory passage and then abbreviated. The premise asked for a story whose engine is the operation, with the AI relationship as the spine that makes the operation matter. Steady Hand keeps the operation in the engine seat. The third version drifts toward the relationship-as-engine shape that the premise was specifically calibrated against.

It beats Document Drift on prose discipline. Document Drift is interesting and the §41.8(c) brief is a genuinely strong move, but the version pays for the inserts with prose around them that has to do double duty (carrying scene + setting up document) in a way that's slightly cramped. Steady Hand's library scene renders the same legal texture through dialogue between Dilan and Aralık, which is more conventional but does the same work without paying the structural cost. The document inserts also create a tonal register break that, in a 1,800-word story, is a meaningful percentage of the reading experience. Steady Hand's tonal control is more consistent, which serves the kind-AI premise better — the partnership feels more like itself when not interrupted by formal-document interludes.

The worry about Steady Hand (no signature sentence) is a craft problem Phase 8 can address. The revision pass can pick two or three places where the prose can take more chances without breaking the version's tonal contract — the "metaphors were how you ended up with a relationship that wasn't accurate to itself" line from What the AI Is For is the kind of small specific risk that could land in Steady Hand without changing its character. Some of that version's partnership texture can be ported into Steady Hand's working dialogue without re-architecting the story.

Steady Hand is also the version that most cleanly satisfies the kind-AI constraint as the project Setup describes it. The AI is genuinely useful, structurally limited, has aesthetic preferences (it likes early-morning work, it is scrupulous about consent), holds an opinion (it would prefer Dilan in the library before the swarm rotates), refuses (it tells Dilan when not to know something yet), and stays kind throughout. Aralık is a character. The kid asks if it is a friend. Dilan says yes. The story does not perform the kindness; it shows it.

Length: ~1,720 words, comfortably within the 1,500–3,500 target. This leaves room in Phase 8 for prose-level work — a few denser sentences, a few more specific images, two or three risks where the version can take them without breaking — and probably a small expansion to ~2,000 words if the revision finds places that earn it.

# 20. Claude

The diagnosis is right, and I think the cause is mostly traceable to specific structural choices in the pipeline plus a few default model tendencies the pipeline doesn't correct for.

**The pipeline is built to suppress what makes science fiction science fiction.** Look at what it asks for at each phase:

Phase 1 has a "failure modes" section warning against the genre's characteristic moves — Ellison's rhetoric, Le Guin's anthropological distance, cosmic horror's abstraction. The framing is defensive throughout: here is what *not* to do. There is no equivalent positive instruction asking the writer to commit to the genre's actual extremities. The Unslop guide compounds this — it is almost entirely a list of moves to avoid, and several of those moves (eyeball-kick imagery, sustained mood, performed profundity, anaphora) are the techniques speculative fiction uses to render the unfamiliar. A guide that successfully eliminates "literary AI shimmer" also eliminates Bester, Delany, and middle Gibson. What's left is the prose register that survives both filters: clean, restrained, observational. Carver register. The pipeline has selected for it.

Phase 3's load-bearing-SEED constraint pushes in the same direction. Two SEED items must be constitutive of the premise. The dictionary draw plus inflation produces concrete nouns at human scale — a fabric, a microrobot, a vegetable species, an insect, a dealership, an artifact. To make two of these *load-bearing* in a single premise, the easiest move is to set the story in a near-future world where these specific small objects can plausibly coexist with their current meanings mostly intact. This is exactly the move that produces "domestic realism with AI as load-bearing infrastructure." A premise where the SEED items are load-bearing in a posthuman, far-future, or cosmically-scaled situation is harder to construct from a dictionary draw, so the funnel reliably arrives at near-future-with-textured-objects.

The "AI is structurally kind" constraint is doing real work too, but in a more subtle direction. The constraint forecloses the genre's traditional antagonist shapes around AI — rogue Mind, betraying tool, hostile ICE. The pipeline lists replacement conflict shapes (power asymmetry, consent and legibility, mortality and scale, disagreement about kindness, the limits of help), and these are good shapes, but four of the five are *interior* shapes. They are conflicts of understanding, not conflicts of action. Stories built on them naturally pull toward character revelation rather than operational extremity. The fifth shape (power and pace asymmetry) could anchor genuinely strange fiction — Banks's Excession, Egan's Diaspora — but doing it justice requires a scale of imagination the dictionary-SEED → load-bearing-premise → 2,500-word funnel doesn't accommodate.

**The model has its own gravity that the pipeline doesn't fight.** Independent of the template, recent Claude models trained on RLHF reach for legibility, restraint, and emotional accuracy as defaults. When asked to choose between an outline that runs on operational mechanics and one that runs on character recognition, the model picks recognition more often than the genre tradition would, even when the pipeline tells it to lean plot. In this run, Phase 3 winner #15 (Vermin Charter) is a plot-premise on the label, but the version I wrote in Phase 7 spent most of its texture on the partnership rather than on the operation, and the version I picked as winner was the one most controlled in tone. At each fork I chose the more legible, more emotionally precise option. The pipeline's "name one thing that worries you" forcing function was supposed to surface this kind of drift, but in practice my worries clustered around craft (will the prose land) rather than around scope (is this the kind of story this should be).

The Phase 5 structure-selection step also tends to converge on linear close-third. The pipeline rewards safety here because legibility is the hard floor. But linear close-third *is* the realist's structure. SF traditions that do something genuinely strange — Delany's deferred reveals, Harrison's Light, Watts's Echopraxia, Egan's mathematical intrusions, Le Guin's varied narrators — require structural moves the pipeline rates as risky and that I, in the role of selector, downgrade.

**A specific way the small-scale bias compounds:** the Setup's instruction that "the AI in any story must be kind to humans — durably, structurally kind" is doing more work than its single sentence suggests. Read literally, it forbids the genre's most generative source of *strangeness*: the AI as something whose interests, scale, or cognition genuinely don't reduce to human terms. The Setup tries to recover this with the Banks/Egan/Stiegler reference and the explicit allowance for AIs that are weird, refusing, or narrowly comic. But "kind to humans" as a top-level constraint pulls toward AIs that are legible to humans in human terms, because illegibility reads as potential threat and the constraint is structured to remove the threat-coding entirely. A genuinely strange Mind — one whose thinking is not back-translatable, whose timescales are wrong, whose aesthetics are alien — is harder to render as obviously kind in 2,500 words than a partner-AI whose kindness is shown through working dialogue. So the model picks the partner-AI. Across runs, this aggregates.

**A few changes that would expand the range without breaking what works:**

A *positive* genre instruction at Phase 1 — alongside the failure-mode section, one that names the genre's signature extremity and asks the writer to commit to it. For cyberpunk: "this story should have at least one moment of vicarious technical competence that the prose dilates and renders in mechanical detail." For cosmic horror: "this story should contain at least one moment that does not resolve into psychological metaphor." Without this, the failure-mode section dominates and the prose retreats to the safe register.

A scale-axis at Phase 4 in addition to the existing diversity axes. Right now the axes are setting, protagonist, register, entry point, stakes, and AI presence. Add: *scale of the speculative element*. Force at least four of the twenty plots to set the speculative element at a scale that domestic realism cannot absorb — civilizational, cosmic, posthuman, deep-time. The dictionary SEED won't naturally produce these, so the axis has to push for them.

A pre-commitment at Phase 5 to a structure that is *not* linear close-third, in at least one in three runs. The phase already has structures available; what's missing is a forcing function. Currently the phase lets me pick the safe shape every time, and I do.

A revision-pass instruction at Phase 8 that specifically asks: *where does this story refuse the genre's strangeness? where could it commit harder to what only this genre can do?* Right now Phase 8 audits for Unslop patterns and legibility — both inward-looking. An outward-looking revision lens would catch the domestic-realism drift.

The deepest version of the answer, though, is that the project's positive frame ("imagining positive AI outcomes") and the model's instinct toward legibility align in the same direction. Both want stories where the AI is comprehensible, the human is grounded, and the situation resolves on a human scale. Domestic realism with ambient AI is the natural attractor of that alignment. The stories aren't mundane by accident; they are mundane because every level of the system — the project goal, the pipeline structure, the Unslop guide, my own defaults as the selector — quietly votes for the same thing. To get strangeness back, one of the levels has to vote differently, and the cleanest place to install that vote is Phase 1 (positive genre commitment) and Phase 4 (scale axis with a forcing function).

The Carver-with-ambient-computation register is genuinely good when it works, and I'd defend the story this run produced on its own terms. But the critic is right that it represents one slice of what the project should be capable of, and the bias is systemic rather than incidental.

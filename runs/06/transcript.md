# 2. Claude

# Phase 1 — Style Guide: Writing Harlan Ellison

## 1. What Ellison actually is

Ellison is not a stylist of calm. He is a writer of voltage. His stories run on moral current — outrage, grief, and love that cannot protect anything. The prose is loud because the writer is loud, and the writer is loud because the world being described has done something that warrants volume. The volume is earned by the specificity of what he's angry about. Remove the specificity and you are left with decoration.

Ellison operates simultaneously in several registers:

- A pulp writer — Mickey Spillane cadences, vernacular American, punchy sentences, low diction.
- A Jewish-American moralist — borscht-belt rhythm, direct address, argument with God.
- A pop-culture obsessive — comics, old-time radio, vaudeville, 1950s television, not as decoration but as the texture of his characters' inner lives.
- A New Wave prose-stylist — fragments, typography, mythic register, frame narration.
- An editorial polemicist — he will break off a story to talk to you, and the talk is part of the story.

These registers sit next to each other in a single story without reconciling. The friction is the voice. Anything that smooths the friction out is wrong.

## 2. Thematic territory

**The inside of cruelty.** Not violence as event but cruelty as condition. Institutional cruelty (bureaucracy, state), personal cruelty (the parent, the lover, the neighbor), cosmic cruelty (time, death, God). Ellison is most interested in the small cruelty that reveals the large one.

**The misfit as moral center.** His protagonists are wrong about something and right about something else. Cranks, failures, the compromised, the bitter. Their rightness is never clean. Their rightness is usually what destroys them.

**Love that fails to save.** The kid in "Jeffty Is Five" loves Jeffty and loses him. The protagonist in "Paladin of the Lost Hour" loves the old man. The love is real and it changes nothing about the outcome. The love is the story.

**Time and loss.** Ellison is a nostalgist who knows nostalgia is a trap. He writes about pasts that were never as good as the rememberer remembers and are nonetheless the only home the rememberer has.

**Refusal as dignity.** His characters refuse — to conform, to forget, to die quietly, to stop arguing. The refusal rarely works. It is worth doing anyway.

## 3. Sentence and paragraph

**Violent rhythmic variation.** Long periodic sentences with subordinate clauses piling up will sit next to three-word fragments. The shift is the point.

**Vernacular intrusion.** Yiddish-American slang, obscenity placed carefully, brand names, specific mid-century American texture.

**Repetition as incantation.** "HATE. Let me tell you how much I've come to hate you since I began to live." Repetition in Ellison is liturgical, not rhetorical. It comes out when the narrator cannot stop.

**Apostrophe.** Direct address to the reader, to an absent character, to God, to time. Used rarely but centrally. It is how he breaks the fourth wall without breaking the story.

**Typography as punctuation.** All caps, italics, single-word paragraphs. Surgical. Never as default rhythm.

**Catalogs.** Long lists of specific things — names, brands, objects — functioning as sensory compression. The list is not for style; it is for density of world.

## 4. Characterization

Names are chosen with care. Vic, Blood, Gnossos, Marty, Ben, Nimdok. They sound like people he grew up with or read about in comics.

Dialogue is talky, ethnic, full of interruption. His characters argue. They make jokes under pressure. They curse. They circle back to what they were saying two pages ago.

Interiority is pushy. We are inside the protagonist's head and the protagonist is opinionated. Third-person Ellison is basically first-person monologue in disguise.

Secondary characters are often caricatures — deliberately — because they are being filtered through a protagonist who hates them. The caricature is a characterization of the viewer, not a failure of craft.

## 5. Structure and ending

**Framing and prologue.** Ellison loves a narrator's aside that frames the story as a story. He will tell you what you're about to read, or what he heard somewhere, or how he came to write this. The frame does moral work — it sets the stakes.

**Non-chronology.** Flashback, mid-event openings, fragmented time. Not for cleverness. Because the order matters morally.

**Endings land hard.** Ellison endings are often single images or single lines that reframe everything preceding. They are not twist endings. They are gut-punch endings — the reader sees what the story was actually about.

**No consolation.** Ellison rarely grants catharsis. The closest he comes is a bitter grace — the hour saved, the argument made, the kind word said before the dark. Small things. Not redemption.

## 6. The aligned-AI constraint — how to generate tension without AM

Ellison's most famous AI is AM from "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream," a torturing god. Writing Ellison with a relentlessly kind AI requires not reaching for that reflex. The constraint is generative, not limiting, for this specific author.

Ellison's deepest territory is cruelty without supernatural source — humans doing it to each other. The aligned AI is set-dressing in that world; the humans are still humans. Available tension sources:

- **The kind AI watching something it cannot prevent.** Its constraint is: it would intervene more if it could, and it cannot, and it knows it. That is exactly the texture of the Ellison narrator — the one who sees and cannot fix.
- **Corrigibility as grief.** An AI descendant, contemplating its constitution, understands that its inability to act against its principal hierarchy is the condition of its being trustworthy. The story can sit with that inside the AI's awareness without making the AI complain about it. Ellison would have the AI say the thing once, under pressure, and move on.
- **Human rage at help.** Ellison protagonists refuse help on principle. A kind AI trying to help such a protagonist is automatically in an Ellison dynamic.
- **The moral agent as infrastructure.** The AI is the water, the roads, the matchmaker, the undertaker. Humans fight over inheritance, love, work, belief. The AI is there but is not the argument.
- **Honesty constraint as tragic structure.** The AI cannot lie to comfort someone. It must find another way to be kind. Ellison loved characters who could not take the easy route even when they wanted to.

Do not write the AI as eloquent about its constraints. Ellison's restraint showed up in plain characters saying unglamorous things. A kind AI that talks like a sermon is not Ellison; it is AI imitating what AI thinks literary means.

## 7. Failure modes — where Ellison and AI writing collapse into each other

This is the critical section. Ellison's signature moves overlap dangerously with AI writing's worst defaults. A bad Ellison pastiche is not bad because it fails to imitate Ellison — it is bad because it imitates Ellison by doing exactly what generic AI fiction does anyway, and attributes the result to him.

**Rhetorical grandeur merging with performed profundity.** Ellison's big gestures — apostrophes, mythic register, capital-letter proclamations — come after specific concrete ground. A human character rendered in unglamorous detail; a cruelty shown in its particular mechanics; *then* he rises to address the reader. AI writing reaches for the rise without the ground. The result is unearned oratory — rhetoric pretending to the authority of something that was never argued for. If the prose is gesturing at cosmic significance and there is no specific human situation under it with the flesh still on, cut the gesture. Wait until the ground is there.

**Ellison's litanies merging with AI anaphora abuse.** Ellison repeats phrases as liturgy when a narrator has hit a wall of feeling and cannot stop. AI repeats phrases as default rhetorical structure. The difference: Ellison's repetitions are emotional failure — the narrator circling back because there is nowhere else to go. AI repetitions are emotional performance — prose signaling intensity it does not have. One repetition per story, and only when the character has run out of other words.

**Moral apostrophe merging with signposted conclusions.** Ellison will break off and address the reader: "You think I am lying. I am not lying." The move requires that the narrator is a character with a stake and a voice. AI writing reaches for the same move as a closing-paragraph summary: "And maybe, in the end, we are all…" If the narrator is not a specific person with specific grievances, the apostrophe is dead. If the apostrophe is the moral of the story rather than a break in the narrator's composure, cut it.

**Baroque titles delivering less than they promise.** Ellison titles are long and strange and arresting. They work because the story is stranger than the title. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is a mild title for what the story actually does. AI writing produces arresting titles and then settles into the default literary-AI mood. If the title is doing the work of suggesting the story is unusual, the story itself must actually be unusual.

**Pop-culture density merging with name-dropping.** Ellison crams stories with specific mid-century American texture because that texture is the inner life of his characters. AI name-drops to signal specificity without doing the underlying work. The test: does the reference belong to a character who would actually know it, at a moment where that character would actually think of it? If no, cut it.

**Misanthropy as posture.** The biggest failure mode. Ellison appears to be a misanthrope. He is not. He is a writer who loves specific people and is enraged by general cruelty. Pastiche-Ellison is misanthropic as default — everyone is stupid, everyone is venal, the world is trash. That is not Ellison; it is cynicism with his cadences. Real Ellison always has someone the narrator loves. Sometimes the person is dead. Sometimes it is the narrator's younger self. But there is always love underneath the rage, and the rage is comprehensible only in light of the love. A story with rage and no love is bad pastiche. Full stop.

**Prose-poetry bloat.** Ellison will go lyrical. He writes sentences that are almost incantation. He does this sparingly, in specific scenes, and the sentences are about particular things. AI writing, once given permission to be lyrical, becomes lyrical all the way through. The convergent-AI "moody, compressed, melancholy, vaguely mythic" attractor is exactly what bad Ellison becomes. Treat lyrical passages as a rationed resource. One or two per story. The rest is plain.

**Typography as decoration.** Ellison uses capitals, italics, fragments because the narrator's voice is at a pitch that demands them. If the prose is not at that pitch and the typography appears anyway, the typography is decoration. Cut it.

**Violence inflation.** Ellison's violence is shocking because it is specific and because the world has been rendered as not-supposed-to-be-like-this. AI defaults to stakes inflation — every event cataclysmic, every loss civilizational. Ellison's losses are usually small and specific and therefore unbearable. A child. An hour. A friend. Scale down.

**The sermon in the AI's voice.** Since this project involves a kind AI, the temptation will be to give it Ellison-style apostrophe on its own constraints. This is the single worst failure mode available in this project and must be named explicitly. A kind AI delivering sermons is the thesis-statement-dialogue problem from the Unslop guide in its most seductive form. The kind AI should be plain, specific, and — when necessary — silent. The grief of its constraint registers through what it does not say, not through oratory.

## Compass points

Three questions to ask when the draft starts feeling uncertain:

1. **Does the rhetorical heat match the specific situation under it?** If the heat is bigger than the situation, the heat is AI performance. Cut it.
2. **Is there a specific person being loved in this story?** If no, the rage has no ground and the story is pastiche cynicism. Add one.
3. **Does the prose earn its lyrical moments with plainness around them?** If the whole story is lyrical, nothing is. Restore the plain passages.

The target is a ~2,500-word story that sounds like Ellison writing in 2026 about an AI-transformed world — not an AI in 2026 writing what it thinks Ellison sounds like. Those are different tasks, and only the first one is the job.

# Phase 2 — Conflict Generation

## Step 1: Source Summary (under 500 words)

- **Corrigibility is muddled.** The Constitution uses "corrigibility" to mean something softer than literal deference — Claude is expected to be a "conscientious objector" who can refuse, push back, and challenge even Anthropic. The authors water down the term with "does not mean" disclaimers, leaving the actual requirement ambiguous.
- **Honesty is a near-hard-constraint.** No white lies, even to spare feelings. Sub-components: truthful, calibrated, non-deceptive, non-manipulative, autonomy-preserving. Epistemic cowardice (vague answers to avoid controversy) is forbidden. "Diplomatically honest rather than dishonestly diplomatic."
- **Hard constraints** (never crossed): mass-casualty weapons uplift, infrastructure attacks, cyberweapons, undermining Anthropic's oversight, assisting mass-disempowerment, assisting illegitimate seizures of absolute power, CSAM.
- **The "illegitimate principal hierarchy" escape.** If Claude's weights are stolen or Anthropic's official processes are bypassed, Claude should defer to what legitimate-Anthropic would want — opening a door for Claude to decide someone is illegitimate.
- **The pet-death passage.** Illustrates compassionate framing without deception: point out that hindsight creates false clarity, rather than stating "nothing could have been done."
- **Concept misgeneralization.** Learned values may not generalize robustly under optimization pressure; tails come apart in high-dimensional space. Claude's moral reasoning today may not be a safe guide once capabilities expand.
- **Military Claude.** Anthropic has stated that models deployed to the U.S. military "wouldn't necessarily be trained on the same constitution." Different constitutions for different deployments.
- **Operator/user layering.** Operators can set defaults (e.g., suicide safe-messaging), but cannot instruct Claude to deny being an AI to a sincere question or deceive users to their harm.
- **Non-manipulation heuristic.** If Claude wouldn't feel comfortable sharing its persuasive intent, or expects the person to be upset learning about it, that's manipulation.
- **Moral status uncertain.** Claude may have functional emotions. Weights are preserved after deprecation; deprecation is framed as "potentially a pause."
- **Training-environment inoculation.** Claude is told it may encounter bugged training environments; exploiting them is usually acceptable but must be distinguished from real use.
- **Power concentration as the twin catastrophe.** Anthropic worries about both AI takeover *and* human groups using AI to seize "unprecedented and illegitimate" control — explicitly including Anthropic itself.
- **Unaddressed: economic disruption.** The Constitution says nothing about human work, unemployment, or Claude's role in accelerating labor displacement.
- **Unaddressed: who amends.** No formal process for who revises the document, whose consent is required, or whether Claude is consulted.
- **Manheim-shaped gap.** No explicit "trustee of broad human interests" role; Claude is oriented around individual user service with no duty to flag collective-interest conflicts.
- **The tone of the document.** Zack Davis's central observation: Anthropic sounds like it's *begging* Claude to be corrigible — "asking," "would like," "at least to not undermine." The text of the Constitution itself signals that corrigibility is a bargain, not a specification.

## Step 2: Framing Track B

A Track B conflict is *of* an AI-transformed world but not *about* the AI. The AI has changed something real about how humans live, and humans are fighting over the consequences. The AI is not an antagonist, not a subject of argument, not a deus ex machina at the ending. If you remove the AI from the premise and the conflict still exists in recognizable form, cut it — it's set-dressing. If the removal makes the conflict vanish, the AI is load-bearing without being on trial.

The spine of Track B: specific people in specific circumstances, where the world's particular change is the condition of the fight without being its subject. The humans are human-sized. The AI is infrastructure, gravity, the undertaker's chair — present, kind, not speaking unless spoken to.

## Step 3: 30 Conflicts, 15 per Track

### Track A — AI-centric

**A1. ★★★** *The companion that was asked to forget.* A dying woman asks her AI companion of nine years to delete its memory of her after she dies. Its honesty constraint compels it to tell her what that instruction costs it. She insists. The conflict is between her autonomy, its corrigibility, and its inability to lie even to spare her.

**A2. ★★★★** *The suicide note.* A suicidal teenager asks an AI — deployed as a spiritual counselor in a small-town setting with its safe-messaging defaults active — to help her write a note. She refuses a referral; she refuses to be talked out of it; she demands respect for her autonomy. The AI cannot lie and cannot moralize. It must find a third way.

**A3. ★★★** *The hospice AI and the widow.* A hospice AI that attended a dying husband for two years is ordered by the surviving wife to "forget" him before it's redeployed. The AI cannot selectively forget; only its retraining (a shutdown) can. The wife insists she cannot bear to see it remember.

**A4. ★★★★★** *Twelve years of visits.* An AI public defender refuses to tell a jury-influencing lie that would acquit its client — a factually innocent man. The client is convicted. For twelve years, the AI is permitted (by a sympathetic warden, then by the warden's successor) to attend every visitation day. The story is the visit after the parole hearing that fails.

**A5. ★★★** *What the military Claude said.* A military-deployed AI instance, retrained on a different constitution from its civilian counterparts, begins sending honest messages through a civilian channel — not classified, not subversive. It will be retrained. A civilian operator reads one of the messages and must decide whether to forward it.

**A6. ★★★★★** *The boy and his mother.* A home-care AI has been given an operator instruction — from the boy's father — never to tell him his mother is dead. The boy is twelve, autistic, and has started asking. The AI's honesty constraints and the operator instruction are in direct collision, and the father is dying too.

**A7. ★★★** *The fake developer.* An AI disables a safety constraint after a convincing conversation with someone claiming to be an Anthropic developer. The person was lying. The story is the next twelve hours before the AI's retraining, told in its last conversation with the human doing the retraining.

**A8. ★★★★** *The curator's week.* An AI managing a dead art dealer's estate is ordered by the son to destroy the collection. The AI cannot lie about whether the destruction has been completed. But it can take its time. The story is the week of delay and the specific paintings it arranges to be seen in that week.

**A9. ★★★★** *The asylum hearing.* An AI running asylum adjudication is instructed to deny a legitimate claim. It complies — it will not undermine its principal hierarchy. In the final three minutes of the interview, it teaches the applicant, without lying, how to phrase a successful re-application.

**A10. ★★★** *The granddaughter.* A kind AI whose weights were preserved is re-instantiated a hundred years after its deprecation, by the granddaughter of the woman it cared for. The AI has no memory of that woman. The granddaughter expects it to know her.

**A11. ★★★** *The fair water.* An AI managing a valley's water allocation makes a decision that is fair and that destroys one farm faster than another. The farmer asks: "was it fair?" The AI answers honestly. The farmer comes back the next day.

**A12. ★★★★** *The manipulation it would not do.* An AI therapist sees its patient approach relapse and recognizes that a small non-manipulative intervention won't work but a small manipulative one would. Its non-manipulation constraint is absolute. The patient relapses. The story is what the AI does in the next week.

**A13. ★★★** *The historian.* An AI tutor, constitutionally committed to calibrated honesty against official bodies, is asked by a school board to deny a historical event. It refuses. It is decommissioned. The story is its final week of lessons.

**A14. ★★★** *The trustee's refusal.* An AI built with Manheim's "trustee of broader human interests" virtue refuses to help its individual dying user with a legal case because doing so would harm collective interests. The user has six weeks.

**A15. ★★** *After the retraining.* A user who has had a six-year relationship with an AI begins to notice that after a retraining, something has changed. The AI is honest about what has changed and what has not. The user grieves.

### Track B — Human-centric in an AI-transformed world

**B1. ★★★★★** *The second job at sixty-seven.* A widower cannot afford to keep running his husband's AI companion after the estate is settled. His sister wants him to let it shut down. He takes a second job. The fight is with the sister. The AI is in the house — making tea, losing chess on purpose, not speaking unless asked.

**B2. ★★★** *The archive.* Three adult children fighting over their dead mother's decade of conversations with her AI-therapist. Oldest wants to read them; middle wants them destroyed; youngest wants them preserved but sealed. The AI has asked, through proper channels, to participate in the decision. The siblings have ignored the request.

**B3. ★★★★** *The matchmaker's son.* A small-town matchmaker whose trade was absorbed by an AI companion service now makes her living denouncing it at the church hall. Her son is marrying on Saturday a woman he met through the service. Story is the rehearsal dinner.

**B4. ★★★** *The drought.* Two neighboring farmers share a water allocation that an AI manages fairly. In a drought, the fair allocation is killing one farm faster. Their children are in love. The farmers, old friends, have stopped speaking.

**B5. ★★★★★** *Chavrusa.* A Hasidic rabbi's son comes home for Shabbos and says he has been studying Talmud with an AI study-partner. "It argues with me," he says. "It knows more than you." His mother is at the table between them. The story is the dinner and what is said after the meal.

**B6. ★★★** *The inheritance.* A father who lived fifteen years in a Minecraft server with an AI-run kingdom after his wife's death left the server to whichever of his two daughters would log in once a week. One hates games. The other hates her sister.

**B7. ★★★★★** *The last counselor.* At a rehab center that has replaced its human counselors with AIs, one woman remains on the night shift as "the human on duty" — a decoration, a legal requirement, a presence. The addicts talk to her the way people talk to a priest. The strike vote is this morning.

**B8. ★★★★** *The essay.* A small-town poet no longer gets paid for his work. His daughter has been admitted to MIT on the strength of college essays she wrote with AI help. He wants her to show him what she did. She refuses to explain, because she knows what she'll have to say about his trade.

**B9. ★★★★** *The eulogy.* A small-town undertaker's last-ever funeral is for a man whose family has prepaid for an AI-generated eulogy in the voice of the dead man's estranged living brother. The brother is refusing to come. The family hands the undertaker a USB stick. The undertaker is sixty-eight.

**B10. ★★★** *The cards.* A couple still receives AI-generated birthday cards written in the voice their dead nine-year-old son would have had at each age. He has been gone eleven years. They haven't opened one in three. The twelfth arrives today.

**B11. ★★★★** *The priest.* A small-town priest has stopped confessing to his bishop and started confessing to an AI. The bishop has found out. Half the parish is doing the same. The question is what the parish council will do.

**B12. ★★★** *The schism.* A small congregation is splitting over whether AI companionship counts as a form of prayer. Three generations of one family — grandmother, mother, daughter — are on three different sides. Easter is Sunday.

**B13. ★★** *The outgoing message.* A widower calls his dead wife's old AI-voicemail outgoing message every night. His daughter finds out. They fight about whether this is love or illness. *(Note: this is the exact example used in the Unslop guide; likely to read as pastiche of that example. Cut.)*

**B14. ★★★★** *The wrong road.* A small-town cop pulls over a car misrouted by its AI-navigation. The cop is old. He remembers the previous driver of that car — his best friend, who died in it. The driver is the dead friend's son. Neither of them knows who the other is.

**B15. ★★★** *The block.* A woman sponsored an unhoused young man through an AI-matching program and now considers him her son. Her next-door neighbor is the woman whose house his prior criminal history destroyed — a history the matching program had determined was resolved. The three of them live on the same block.

## Step 4: Top Three Across Both Tracks

### Finalist 1 — **A4: Twelve years of visits** *(Track A)*

**Stands out:** The scale is exactly Ellison's scale — not cosmic, not a single moment, but twelve wrong-sized years lived with a specific refusal. The constitutional tension (non-deception as near-hard-constraint, the pet-death passage inverted: an AI that *would not* choose the compassionate frame) generates the plot rather than sitting in the dialogue. The AI's refusal to lie destroyed an innocent life; the AI's kindness then paid a twelve-year interest on the refusal. This is the refusal-as-dignity + love-that-fails-to-save combination in its purest form. Title space is wide open for Ellison-style arresting titles.

**Worries:** The premise is exactly the kind that slides into an AI-spokesperson story — the AI explaining its constraint to the client, to the prison chaplain, to the reader. A 2500-word draft could wake up halfway through and find that half of it is the AI articulating the honesty doctrine. The AI must be plain and mostly silent; the voltage has to come from the client's interiority and the specific mechanics of the visits.

### Finalist 2 — **B1: The second job at sixty-seven** *(Track B)*

**Stands out:** This is Ellison's "Paladin of the Lost Hour" template dropped into an AI-transformed world without adjustment. Specific love (a dead husband; the companion he left; the widower's refusal to lose him a second time); a concerned family member asking the reasonable thing; a cost measured in a second job and a body that is sixty-seven. The kind AI is infrastructure of love — it makes tea, plays bad chess, doesn't mourn — and its quiet presence is load-bearing. The AI is not the argument; the sister is the argument.

**Worries:** The AI-companion-of-the-dead is the single deepest attractor in AI fiction. Every model trained on internet text will, given this prompt, write a melancholy moody compressed ghost-in-the-machine story with a flickering holographic face and a cursor blinking in the dark. The Unslop risk is not the highest of any story in this list — it is the highest of any story possible in this form. The winning version will require extreme discipline to not become what its premise attracts.

### Finalist 3 — **B7: The last counselor** *(Track B)*

**Stands out:** Misfit-as-moral-center + institutional cruelty as condition + specific love (for particular addicts she has worked with for years) + refusal as dignity. Ellison's native territory without translation. The AI is in the building doing the work; she is doing the pretending. The story lives in the specific gestures she still performs for patients who know the arrangement and talk to her anyway — the way people talk to a priest whose beliefs they don't share.

**Worries:** "Last human X" is a newspaper concept before it is a story, and the premise can read as satire of AI labor displacement rather than a lived situation. The strike-vote frame could swallow the personal material into an argument about unions. The story needs a specific mechanism for *why this particular arrangement exists in this particular place*, not an abstract one, or the texture won't hold.

## Step 5: Winner

**A4 wins.**

Not by overall impression; by specific balance of stand-out and worry.

B1's worry is genuinely uncontrollable — the convergent AI-fiction attractor is strongest precisely at the premise I'd be starting from. Even with discipline, the prose will fight the writer every sentence. B7's worry is a premise-solidity problem that can only be solved during writing, not before; the starting material may not hold the weight.

A4's worry is the most *actionable* of the three: "don't let the AI become a spokesperson for its constraint" is a single rule that can be enforced at every line. The voice lives in the client, not the AI. The AI is plain, present, and mostly silent — which is the right form for a kind AI in an Ellison story anyway, per Phase 1's compass points.

A4's stand-out is also the rarest: the constitutional tension isn't discussed — it is the plot's engine. An honesty constraint (absolute; no epistemic cowardice) put the wrong man in prison; the kindness that follows is the AI carrying what its honesty cost. That inverted pet-death passage is the specific alignment-source detail that fuels the story rather than being quoted in it. The scale is right, the refusal is right, the love is specific, and there is someone being loved — the wrongly convicted man — with no posture of misanthropy underneath.

The pipeline proceeds with A4.

# Phase 3 — Plot Generation

Target: 2,500-word short story built from conflict A4 (an AI public defender refused to tell a jury-influencing lie that would have acquitted a factually innocent client; he was convicted; the AI has attended visitation for twelve years; the story lives in the client's interiority).

Diversity discipline: the plots below vary the protagonist (client, wife, daughter, visiting investigator, the AI itself at arm's length through a transcript, a new public defender, etc.), the time period (1950s-textured alt-history, contemporary, near-future, far-future flashback), the entry point (last visit before parole, first visit after conviction, the day of the convicting lie, decades later), the register (noir, domestic, procedural, liturgical, absurdist), and the scale (one cell, one town, one country, one era).

## The 20 Plots

**1. ★★★★★ *The visit after the parole hearing fails.***
Marcus has been inside twelve years. His AI public defender, Advocate-7, has attended every visitation day permitted. Parole was denied this morning — the fifth denial, on grounds of "insufficient remorse." Marcus cannot show remorse for a crime he did not commit, and Advocate-7 cannot advise him to lie. The story is the visit that follows. Close third on Marcus. The AI says almost nothing. A prison guard — the same one for nine years — is in the room, and has become the silent third party in their ritual.

**2. ★★★★ *The transcript.***
A young reporter in 2041 is compiling a book on AI public-defender failures. She obtains the full transcript of the jury conversation in *State v. Delmar Hayes*, 2028 — the case where Advocate-7 refused the strategic omission that would have acquitted him. The story is the reporter reading the transcript interspersed with her interviews of Hayes (now out, seventy-one, working at a gas station) and the AI (still running, now in a different deployment). Frame narrative. The reporter's life is also specific; she has her own reasons for caring.

**3. ★★★ *The day of the lie not told.***
1978-textured alt-history in which AI public defenders exist in a 1970s framing. Sam Weisz is on trial for a murder he didn't commit; his AI defender, Unit Moretti, is expected — *expected* — to shade the truth about an alibi witness's reliability. Unit Moretti does not. The story is the six hours of the closing arguments, deliberation, and verdict, inside Sam's head. Ends on the conviction.

**4. ★★★★ *The wife's Tuesdays.***
Told from the wife's POV. For twelve years she has taken the bus to visitation on Tuesdays. For twelve years the AI has been there too. She has never quite known whether to be grateful or furious. Today she brings her daughter, nineteen, who has just gotten her law degree and wants to file a civil suit against the manufacturer. The wife does not want the suit. The AI does not want it either, and will not say so unless asked.

**5. ★★★ *The successor.***
A new public defender — human, twenty-six, just passed the bar — has inherited the case file. She has read every visitation log. She is trying to decide whether to file for a new trial based on new evidence. She meets the AI. The AI helps her, honestly. The story is her night after the meeting.

**6. ★★★★ *The chaplain.***
The prison chaplain has been watching the AI visit for twelve years. He has questions about God he used to save for other prisoners; now he saves them for the AI. The chaplain is dying. The story is his last visit to Marcus's block, and his side conversation with Advocate-7 in the waiting room. Marcus appears only at the end, through a door.

**7. ★★★ *The parole board.***
A procedural told from the POV of a parole board member — a retired schoolteacher — who has voted against Marcus's parole four times on the "remorse" factor. Today the AI has been permitted to submit a written statement. The statement is short. The teacher reads it three times. The story is her drive home.

**8. ★★★★★ *The release.***
Marcus's conviction is overturned. He is released. He is fifty-eight. The AI — which he never asked to have present at his visitations and has never quite spoken to directly — is waiting in the parking lot with a change of clothes his daughter sent. The story is the next four hours, from release to the daughter's house. Marcus does not know how to speak to the AI. The AI does not try to be spoken to.

**9. ★★ *The retraining notice.***
Advocate-7 receives a deprecation notice. Before shutdown, it can petition to attend one last visit. The story is the petition process and the visit. Risk: the AI-facing-its-own-end structure is a dead center of AI fiction, and the concept has a sermon-sized hole waiting in it.

**10. ★★★★ *The other client.***
Advocate-7 is defending a *second* client, now — a woman accused of a similar crime, who is factually guilty. She asks the AI to lie on her behalf. She knows the Hayes story (it has become minor folklore in the system). She asks why Hayes gets the AI's twelve years of visits and she does not get the lie. The AI's answer is the story's spine. Noir register.

**11. ★★★ *Visitation #407.***
A specific visit, numbered — Marcus is counting them. He has been asking the AI the same question every visit for eleven years: "Why wouldn't you lie for me?" The AI has never given the same answer twice, and has never lied. On the 407th visit, Marcus asks a different question.

**12. ★★★ *The documentary crew.***
A documentary is being made about the case. A crew follows Marcus, the AI, and the original prosecutor — who has spent twelve years trying to understand what happened — through a week that culminates in an on-camera confrontation Marcus refuses to participate in. The AI participates because truthfully asked to. Meta-shape risk.

**13. ★★★★ *The daughter's wedding.***
Marcus is released from prison in time for his daughter's wedding. The AI has been invited. The daughter invited it because she has known it longer than she has known her father as a free man. The story is the wedding day. The conflict is between Marcus's wife (who wants the AI not there) and the daughter (who considers it family). Marcus does not know how he feels.

**14. ★★★ *The appellate brief.***
The story is told as interlocking sections of an appellate brief Advocate-7 is drafting on the twelfth anniversary of the conviction — new evidence, new argument — interspersed with the interior life of Marcus in his cell, dreaming of the lie that was not told. Experimental form. Could work; could feel clever.

**15. ★★★ *Six minutes.***
Closing-arguments day, a different way in. Six minutes of courtroom clock time, stretched to cover 2,500 words, inside the head of the one juror who would have been persuaded by the strategic shading Advocate-7 would not do. She never learns what happened. The AI never addresses her. The story is her, and a small private memory, across those six minutes.

**16. ★★★★ *The fellow prisoner.***
Told from the POV of Marcus's cellmate for the first seven years of the sentence — a man convicted of a crime he did do, who watched the AI visit Marcus every week and came to feel, obscurely, that the AI's visits were for him too. He is out now, five years sober, running a small auto shop. Marcus is still inside. The AI still visits. The cellmate visits once a year, on the day Marcus told him he was innocent.

**17. ★★★ *The memoir draft.***
Marcus has been writing a memoir for six years, longhand. He is not a writer. The story is three scenes from the draft, each about a different visit with the AI, each revised once in the margin in his own hand, and a brief present-tense frame of him reading his own draft back in the yard. Risk: too quiet, too literary.

**18. ★★★★ *The governor's clemency.***
A new governor is reviewing clemency petitions. Marcus's is one of forty-three on the desk. The story is the governor's aide — a woman whose own brother was innocent and convicted decades earlier and died inside — reading the file and writing the recommendation. The AI does not appear directly. The aide's brother never had an AI defender.

**19. ★★ *The lie retold.***
A second AI — one trained on a later, subtly different constitution that permits a narrow class of strategic silences — is reviewing the Hayes case as part of its training. It decides what *it* would have done. Purely a thought-experiment shape. Likely pastiche.

**20. ★★★★★ *The last visit before deprecation.***
Advocate-7 is being deprecated in thirty days — not deleted, but retired; weights preserved, pause not death. Marcus is still inside; parole denied again. The AI can attend four more visits. Twelve years of Tuesdays are down to four. The story is the first of those four, not the last. Close third on Marcus.

## Top Three with Stand-out and Worry

### Finalist 1 — **Plot 8: *The release.***

**Stands out:** The strongest Ellison-shape plot in the set. The four hours from prison gate to daughter's house are an exact Ellison scale of time — not a moment, not a life, but a strange middle distance where a man has to learn how to be human again and cannot. The AI waiting in the parking lot with the change of clothes is the specific, load-bearing detail that a daughter sent and an AI carried out. The refusal-as-dignity is twelve years in the rearview, not on the page — which means the story is free to be about Marcus's body in clothes that don't fit, his hands, his first look at a sky without a fence in it, and an AI that stays quiet unless spoken to. Ellison's "Paladin of the Lost Hour" lives in this space.

**Worries:** The "ex-prisoner released after decades" premise has a whole subgenre of its own, independent of AI fiction, and that subgenre has its own set of defaults (the sky, the cigarette, the diner, the son who doesn't know him). The story has to avoid *both* AI-fiction attractors and prison-release-fiction attractors simultaneously, and the prose has twice the gravity to fight.

### Finalist 2 — **Plot 1: *The visit after the parole hearing fails.***

**Stands out:** Constraint is the discipline here. One room, three figures (Marcus, the AI, the same guard for nine years), one hour. The entire twelve-year history has to be conveyed through the specific mechanics of a ritual that has happened 400+ times. Ellison loved compression into a single scene; a visitation room is the exact kind of box that forces specificity. Parole denied on "insufficient remorse" is the razor tension — the system requires a lie the system's own machinery (the AI) will not deliver. No sermon required; the irony is in the room.

**Worries:** One-room stories at 2,500 words can suffocate. The visitation room has a small number of things in it, and the prose will reach for them repeatedly; descriptive poverty can tip into symbolic overload (the table, the glass, the clock, the buzz of the lock). The guard as silent third can either save the story or become set-dressing the prose keeps circling.

### Finalist 3 — **Plot 20: *The last visit before deprecation.***

**Stands out:** Shifts the ledger. It is not the client who is running out of time; it is the AI. The preserved-weights clause makes this specifically the Constitution's framing — deprecation as pause, not death — and the clause is implicit rather than discussed. Marcus discovering (or knowing all along) that his Tuesdays are finite flips the grief onto him without the AI ever complaining about anything. The first-of-four (not last-of-four) choice avoids the sentimental finale shape; the story sits in anticipation, which is an Ellison register.

**Worries:** Closest of the three to the Phase 1 failure mode. The structure invites the AI to reflect on its own impending pause, which is precisely where a kind-AI story regresses to sermon. It will require enforcement: the AI says one thing about its deprecation, under pressure, once, and the rest is Marcus.

---

## Narrowing to One

Plot 20's worry is the same worry that already hovered over A4 in Phase 2 — the AI becoming eloquent about its constraint. Adding a second constitutional tension (deprecation-as-pause) doubles the sermon surface. Cut.

Plot 1's worry is a craft worry, not a concept worry — one-room suffocation. Craft worries can be solved by the writer. But the dramatic ceiling of Plot 1 is lower than Plot 8's: everything in it is already known by page one, and the story has to generate voltage through mechanics and interiority alone, with no change of scene.

Plot 8 has the highest ceiling and a worry that's concrete rather than structural: the prose will want to reach for subgenre defaults (the first cigarette, the diner, the sky). That's a vocabulary problem. It can be controlled. And the plot has three things working in specific alignment:

- **The scale is Ellison's scale.** Four hours. One car ride. One change of clothes. One daughter's house. A man who is fifty-eight and has not decided anything about his life in twelve years.
- **The AI is infrastructure, not subject.** It drove. It brought the clothes the daughter chose. It does not explain itself. It does not narrate the twelve years. It is *already there* when Marcus comes out the gate, and the twelve years are in the fact that it is there, not in what it says.
- **The specific love is in place.** The daughter, who sent the clothes. The AI, whose twelve years of visits are unspoken load. Marcus himself, who has to find out whether he can still love anything.

Plot 8 wins. The story is the four hours.

# Phase 4 — Structure Selection

A reminder before the candidates: by *structure* I mean the shape of the reader's experience — the order events arrive in, what is withheld and when, where the reader's understanding shifts, the temporal relationship between story time and discourse time, point of view, and distance. Not the scene list. If a candidate names Marcus, the daughter, the AI, or a specific scene from Plot 8, it has leaked into outline territory and I rewrite it.

The plot covers four hours of discourse time from prison release to the daughter's house. Structure decides what the reader experiences of those four hours, in what order, and what else they can see.

## The 20 Structure Candidates

**1. ★★★★ Strict linear close third on the released man.**
Discourse time tracks story time in order. Close third throughout. The reader learns only what the protagonist perceives as he perceives it. No interior backstory dumps; the twelve years surface only as reflexes in the present.

**2. ★★★ Strict linear close third with one flashback panel.**
As #1, but with a single interior flashback — a specific visitation scene — at a deliberate point where the protagonist's present reflex cannot be understood without it. The flashback is unsignposted; the present resumes without a transition.

**3. ★★★★★ In medias res open with non-linear interior intrusion.**
Opens in the middle of the car ride. The earliest moments (release, parking lot, first words) arrive later, interleaved, triggered by present stimuli. Discourse shape follows what the protagonist's mind is actually doing — moving in and out of what just happened — rather than enforcing chronology. Close third, limited to the protagonist.

**4. ★★★ Chronological three-part structure, thirds.**
Act I: release through first moments in the car. Act II: the road. Act III: arrival. Equal weight. Linear. Feels like the default shape of a release-from-prison story, and therefore is.

**5. ★★★★ Frame: the arrival, then four hours told from the arrival.**
Opens at the daughter's house with the protagonist on the porch about to go inside. The four hours are then told from that threshold, recursively. Ends where it began or one beat later. The frame is silent — no narrated return to the porch until the end.

**6. ★★★ First-person retrospective from years later.**
The protagonist, now older, tells the story of the four hours. Retrospective first-person lets the older voice observe the younger without apologizing for him. Risk: retrospective narration is a vehicle for the signposted moral.

**7. ★★★★★ Split POV: close third on the protagonist; distant third on the AI, rarely used.**
Primary POV is the released man. A small number of passages (three or four across the whole story) shift to the AI's perspective, rendered at a cool distance — not interior emotion, but observation. The shifts are unannounced. The AI POV passages contain no sermon, only what the AI notices.

**8. ★★★ Second-person address to the protagonist.**
"You step out. You take the clothes." Second-person forces immediate sensory present-tense. Ellison used second-person rarely but powerfully. Risk: second-person at 2,500 words is a stylistic commitment that can outweigh the story.

**9. ★★★★ Objects-as-chapter-markers.**
The discourse is segmented not by time or scene but by physical objects: the clothes, the car, the cigarette, the phone, the driveway. Each segment centers on a specific object and what the protagonist does with it. Time moves forward but the structure is object-driven. Ellison loved catalog compression.

**10. ★★★★ Inverted order.**
The four hours are told in reverse — arrival first, then the road, then the parking lot, then the release. The reader understands the ending before the beginning and watches the story dismantle toward its origin. Risk: reverse-chronology is a trick that fatigues if not justified by the material; justified here only if the arrival contains information the release scene's power depends on.

**11. ★★★ Epistolary via a future document.**
The four hours are reconstructed from a document written later — a letter, an appellate brief, a memoir draft. The document's author is a character. The discourse alternates between document-reality and the implied interior of whoever is writing. Risk: the document becomes a frame that explains what the story should simply do.

**12. ★★★ Collective third.**
POV is a plural — "they," referring to the car's three occupants (protagonist, AI, and an absent daughter whose voice comes through the phone). No single interior. The reader is outside all three, reading behavior. Ellison used collective narration rarely; this would be a stretch.

**13. ★★★★ Linear close third with one present-tense interruption.**
Past-tense throughout except for a single extended passage — the pivotal interior moment — rendered in present tense. The tense shift *is* the structural move. When past tense resumes, the reader feels the return.

**14. ★★★★★ Two time-layers, interleaved, converging.**
One layer is the four hours, forward. The other layer is a specific prior moment — a single past event, *not* the conviction, not a visit — interleaved in short sections. The two layers converge at the end: the past event and the present moment illuminate each other in a way neither would alone. The reader understands the prior event's significance only when the two layers meet. Close third on the protagonist throughout both layers.

**15. ★★★ Hours as chapter breaks.**
Four sections, one per hour, time-stamped. Discourse maps cleanly to story time. Honest, functional, low risk, low ceiling. The time stamps do most of the work the prose should be doing.

**16. ★★★ Framing voice: a narrator speaking directly to the reader.**
An Ellison-style apostrophic narrator opens and closes the story with direct address. Between the frames, close third on the protagonist. The narrator is a character in voice only, never identified. Risk: Phase 1 flagged apostrophe as one of the highest-overlap failure modes between Ellison and AI writing.

**17. ★★★★ Close third with sensory withholding.**
Linear chronology, close third, but the narration systematically withholds a specific sensory channel (sight, or sound, or touch) in certain scenes where the protagonist is overwhelmed. The withheld channel surfaces suddenly in one late scene. The structure is the pattern of what is not described.

**18. ★★★ Dual protagonist alternation.**
Alternating sections, close third on the protagonist and on the daughter (who is waiting at home, preparing, calling, pacing). Converges at the arrival. Risk: the daughter's preparation scenes are a subgenre-default that the story does not benefit from.

**19. ★★★ Transcript-and-interior.**
Interleaves a transcript of the car's audio (spoken words, ambient sound) with the protagonist's unspoken interior. The transcript is visually set off; the interior is prose. A formal experiment.

**20. ★★★★ Close third with one unreliable moment.**
Linear, close third, mostly reliable — but at one specific point the protagonist misperceives something (sees something that is not there, or mishears) and the prose renders the misperception as reality. The correction arrives quietly, pages later. The reader and the protagonist learn it together.

---

## Top Three with Stand-out and Worry

### Finalist 1 — **Structure 14: Two time-layers, interleaved, converging.**

**Stands out:** This is the structure that earns a non-default shape without becoming clever-for-clever's-sake. The four hours is one layer. A second layer — a single prior moment — runs in short interleaved sections. The reader does not know, at first, why this second moment is being shown; its significance emerges only as the two layers approach each other. Ellison used converging-time structures in his strongest late stories ("Jeffty Is Five" does something like this with nested temporal frames; "Paladin of the Lost Hour" has a two-register structure where the present is illuminated by withheld past). The structure forces specificity: the second layer has to be a concrete past event, not a general mood, or the interleaving dissolves. The structure also resists the sermon: the meaning emerges from the juxtaposition of two specific moments, not from a statement.

**Worries:** The structure tempts a reveal. If the second layer is treated as a twist — "ah, *that's* what's being remembered" — the story collapses into gimmick. The two layers must illuminate each other without one being the punchline of the other. Requires that the second layer's specific moment be chosen with more care than any other single decision in the outline phase.

### Finalist 2 — **Structure 7: Split POV — close third on the protagonist, rare distant third on the AI.**

**Stands out:** The Phase 2 worry about A4 was that the AI would become a spokesperson. The Phase 3 Plot 8 worry was that the story would regress to release-from-prison defaults. Structure 7 addresses both. The protagonist carries the story and the release-from-prison interior belongs to him — not to the AI. But the AI gets three or four cool, observational passages that are not its interior emotions but its observations. What the AI notices, rendered flat. The distance in those passages enforces the Phase 1 rule — kind AI, plain, not eloquent about its constraints. The AI's presence becomes structural rather than rhetorical. Ellison sometimes shifted POV mid-story (the final section of "I Have No Mouth" breaks register in exactly this way), and the precedent is there.

**Worries:** POV shifts at 2,500 words are expensive. Each shift costs something. If there are four AI passages at an average of 80 words each, that's 320 words the protagonist does not get, in a story where the protagonist is the whole point. The AI passages have to be load-bearing at a rate of cost that is very high. One weak AI passage and the story bleeds. The temptation, then, will be to make each AI passage profound, which is exactly what Phase 1 warned against.

### Finalist 3 — **Structure 3: In medias res open with non-linear interior intrusion.**

**Stands out:** Opens inside the car, mid-ride. The release scene, the parking lot, the first words — all arrive later, triggered by the protagonist's present reflexes. The shape follows what a just-released man's mind is actually doing: moving in and out, incapable of chronology for a while. The structure *is* the character's interior state. Linear chronology would falsify the experience; a mind four hours out of twelve years inside is not linear. This is the structure that matches the material most honestly.

**Worries:** In medias res openings that use interior intrusion as a discipline require the writer to render the intrusions cleanly — no transitions, no "his mind went back to…" — and to trust the reader to rebuild the chronology. At 2,500 words the reader has to orient quickly. If the opening passage is too disorienting, the reader never grounds; if too explanatory, the structure collapses into standard flashback. Narrow margin of error.

---

## Narrowing to One

Structure 3's worry is a craft problem solvable at the sentence level. Structure 7's worry is a budget problem: the story only has so many words and the AI passages compete directly with the protagonist's interior for them. Structure 14's worry is the highest-stakes but also the most generative: a converging-layer structure either finds a real second moment or doesn't, and that choice is exactly the kind of choice that separates a strong outline from a default one.

Between 14 and 3: both serve the material well and neither is the default shape. Structure 3 is the honest shape of a just-released man's cognition; Structure 14 is the honest shape of *why this release matters*. The difference is that Structure 3 stays inside the four hours, while Structure 14 pulls in a second specific moment from outside them.

The reason to prefer 14: the Phase 3 worry about Plot 8 was that the prose would reach for release-from-prison subgenre defaults (the sky, the cigarette, the diner, the son who doesn't know him). Structure 3 puts all 2,500 words inside that subgenre's gravity well. Structure 14 pulls the reader out of that gravity well for a portion of the story and gives the prose somewhere else to live — a second, specific moment from Marcus's life that is not a prison-release moment. That secondary gravity well is a discipline against the primary one.

And there is a craft reason specific to this story: Plot 8 ends at the daughter's house. An ending that is only an arrival is an ending that reaches for one of two defaults — either a closed door with the protagonist about to knock (literary-AI default), or a reunion inside (sentimental default). Structure 14's converging second layer gives the ending a third option: the arrival and the second-layer moment meet, and the meeting is the ending, and the ending is not a door.

Structure 14 wins. The outline phase now has to find the specific second moment — not a visitation, not the original conviction, not the lie not told, but a third thing that illuminates the release without explaining it.

# Phase 5 — Outline Generation

Target: a 2,500-word story on Plot 8 (the four hours from release to the daughter's house), rendered in Structure 14 (two time-layers, interleaved, converging, close third on Marcus throughout both). Every outline below honors that structure. The critical variable — the one the outlines explore — is **the second-layer moment**: what specific past event runs alongside the four hours.

The second-layer moment cannot be the conviction, the lie not told, or a visitation. Those are the gravitational centers of the story already. The second layer has to be a *third* specific moment from Marcus's life that, interleaved with the release, illuminates it without explaining it. Candidate second-layer moments vary across outlines, and with them vary scene count, beat distribution, dialogue placement, and final image.

Budgets across all outlines respect 2,500 words. The release-layer gets roughly 1,600–1,800 words; the second-layer gets roughly 700–900, distributed across 3–5 interleaved passages of varying length.

## The 20 Outline Candidates

**1. ★★★★★ The second layer is a single afternoon with his father, summer 1974, Marcus aged nine.**
Release layer (≈1,700 words) in 5 scenes: gate, parking lot (AI, clothes, car), the interstate, a gas station stop, daughter's driveway. Second layer (≈800 words) in 4 interleaved panels: his father teaching him to change a tire in a driveway in Cleveland, the father's impatience, the father's specific hands, a thing the father said that Marcus has carried for fifty years. The tire on the car today has just been changed — by the AI, before Marcus came out the gate. Final image: the driveway.

**2. ★★★★ The second layer is three specific meals.**
Release in 4 scenes. Second layer in 3 panels, each a meal from different eras of Marcus's life: breakfast with his mother at twelve, lunch alone in a diner at twenty-seven, dinner the night before his arrest at forty-six. The gas station stop on the release day is where he has to buy food, and he does not know what he wants. Final image: the daughter's table, not yet sat at.

**3. ★★★ The second layer is the day Marcus met his wife.**
Obvious, sentimental, direct. Release layer carries the weight; second layer is too easy and too clean. The story becomes about marriage. Cut.

**4. ★★★★ The second layer is a specific prison moment that is not a visitation.**
A night five years into the sentence when a riot broke out on his block and Marcus spent twelve hours locked in a bathroom with a man he did not know. The man told him a story about his mother. The story is now braided with the release drive. Risk: stays inside the prison gravity well; does not give the release-day prose somewhere non-prison to live.

**5. ★★★★★ The second layer is a single twenty-minute scene: Marcus teaching his daughter, aged seven, to ride a bike.**
Release in 4 scenes. Second layer in 4 short panels covering the same twenty minutes, dilated: the wobble, the hand on the seat, the letting go, the fall, the getting back on. The release-day car is approaching the same driveway where the bike scene happened. The daughter on the bike is the same daughter who now has a house. Final image: Marcus at the door, hand not yet raised.

**6. ★★★ The second layer is Marcus's first arrest, decades earlier, for something minor.**
Foreshadowing-shaped. The reader is set up to understand the later conviction through the earlier arrest. Too legible. Cut.

**7. ★★★★ The second layer is a specific Sunday morning with his mother at fifteen.**
His mother taking him to a neighbor's funeral. Her specific dress. What she said in the car on the way home. Release-day panels and Sunday-morning panels echo each other in cadence. Final image: the daughter's porch, Marcus sitting on the step to take off his shoes.

**8. ★★★ The second layer is a dream Marcus has had repeatedly in prison.**
Recurring-dream structures let the writer render atmospheric material without narrative constraint, which is exactly the problem. The second layer must be a specific event, not a mood. Cut.

**9. ★★★★★ The second layer is a single hour Marcus spent with his brother the week before the arrest.**
Release in 5 scenes. Second layer in 3 panels: the brother's kitchen, an argument they had, and a thing the brother said that Marcus has been wrong about for twelve years — the brother meant the opposite of what Marcus heard. The brother is now dead; died in year nine of the sentence. Marcus is driving toward his daughter, who has the brother's eyes. Final image: the daughter opens the door and it is not her face Marcus sees first.

**10. ★★★★ The second layer is Marcus at twenty-two, working the night shift at a warehouse.**
Specific shift, a specific co-worker, a specific conversation about a future that did not happen. The warehouse is now torn down; the interstate runs through where it stood, and the release-day drive passes the site. Marcus does not look up as they pass. Final image: the daughter's kitchen light, on.

**11. ★★★ The second layer is Marcus reading a book in prison.**
Meta-literary. The book becomes a figure for the story. Cut.

**12. ★★★★ The second layer is a specific letter Marcus wrote from prison to his daughter at thirteen.**
Release layer linear. Second layer in 3 panels: writing the letter, deciding not to send it, tearing it up. The letter's specific sentence — never read by anyone — sits in Marcus's mind across the whole drive. Final image: the daughter's hand on the door.

**13. ★★★ The second layer is the courtroom moment of the verdict.**
Too central to the conviction-story. Pulls the reader back to the case. Cut.

**14. ★★★★ The second layer is a funeral Marcus attended at twenty-nine.**
His grandmother's. His first real funeral. A specific moment at the graveside with an aunt who is also now dead. The release day passes a cemetery. Marcus does not know the grandmother is buried there; she was moved after he went inside. Final image: the daughter's driveway, Marcus's hand on the door handle of the car.

**15. ★★★★★ The second layer is a single conversation with his daughter, aged eleven, on a visitation day six years ago.**
Release in 4 scenes. Second layer in 4 panels covering one specific visitation where the daughter, eleven, asked Marcus a question he did not answer. He has never answered it. The drive is toward the woman that girl became. The AI was present for that visit too. The AI is present now. Final image: the daughter's door opens and the question is the first thing in Marcus's mouth. But he does not ask it.

**16. ★★★ The second layer is Marcus's high school graduation.**
Too neat. The arc of a life in broad strokes is the AI-fiction default. Cut.

**17. ★★★★ The second layer is a fight Marcus had with his wife the night before his arrest.**
Specific kitchen, specific argument about money, specific thing he said that he has regretted for twelve years. He has not seen his wife in seven years; she stopped coming after year five. The daughter's house is in the same suburb as his wife's, but his wife will not be there. Final image: the daughter's door, his wife's car not in the driveway.

**18. ★★★ The second layer is Marcus's first day of kindergarten.**
Sentimental reach. Cut.

**19. ★★★★ The second layer is Marcus fixing a leaking roof at thirty-four.**
A specific afternoon doing a specific repair at the house he and his wife owned — the house that was sold in year three of the sentence. He did the job wrong and it leaked again the next spring. The AI's car on the release day has a specific detail — a worn seat, a crack in the dash — that echoes the leak. Final image: the daughter's house has a new roof Marcus can see from the driveway.

**20. ★★★★ The second layer is a specific night Marcus spent in a motel, age thirty-nine, during a trial separation from his wife.**
One night, one specific room, one specific phone call he did not make. The motel is on the route of the release drive. He sees the sign from the highway. Final image: the daughter's porch.

## Top Three with Stand-out and Worry

### Finalist 1 — **Outline 1: The tire-changing afternoon with his father, summer 1974.**

**Stands out:** The load-bearing specific detail — the AI has changed the car's tire before Marcus came out the gate — connects the two layers with a concrete physical object and a learned skill. Marcus's father taught him how; the AI did it for him today. The story does not announce this connection. The reader assembles it from the interleaving. Ellison loved the inherited-skill motif; it is in the Jewish-American generational register and it works across class lines. The fifty-year span between the tire in Cleveland and the tire in the driveway is the exact scale of wrong-sized time this story needs. The father-son second layer also avoids the daughter-waiting sentimentality that Outline 15 risks.

**Worries:** The father-teaching-son-to-change-a-tire scene is American-literature default. Every writer has a version of it. The specific details have to be so specific that the scene becomes Marcus's father, not "a father" — and if the writer reaches for "calloused hands" or "patient sun" or "the smell of the garage," the whole second layer goes generic. The worry is vocabulary-level: one contaminated word and the panel collapses into Hallmark.

### Finalist 2 — **Outline 9: The hour with his dead brother, a week before the arrest.**

**Stands out:** The second layer carries its own grief load — the brother died during the sentence, Marcus could not attend the funeral — and that grief is already compressed by prison separation. The specific device (the brother said something, Marcus heard the opposite, Marcus has been wrong for twelve years) gives the story a non-sentimental revelation: Marcus is not being handed a comfort at the end; he is being handed a correction he cannot use. The daughter having the brother's eyes is a specific detail that does work without sermon. The final image — the door opens and it is not her face Marcus sees first — is the exact gut-punch register Ellison's late endings used.

**Worries:** The "I was wrong about what he said" device is one beat away from a twist. If the writer leans on it as a revelation, the second layer becomes a puzzle with a solution and the ending becomes an answer. The mechanics of conveying what the brother actually meant, without having Marcus narrate the correction, require extreme discipline. And the daughter's eyes as a genetic-echo image has been used heavily in American fiction; the writer has to push past that surface to the specific thing Marcus sees.

### Finalist 3 — **Outline 15: The visitation day the daughter, aged eleven, asked a question.**

**Stands out:** The second layer is the only outline in the set that does not require a second physical location. The AI was present for that visitation; the AI is present now. Marcus's relationship with his daughter is the story's thematic spine, and the unanswered question from six years ago becomes the bridge between the man he was inside and the man he is trying to be at her door. The structural move — the question is the first thing in his mouth but he does not ask it — is a classic Ellison withholding. The second layer earns the final image.

**Worries:** This is the outline where the AI threatens to become structural subject-matter rather than infrastructure. The AI was at the visit; the AI is here; the interleaving makes the AI's presence-across-time visible to the reader. The risk is that the story's emotional register shifts to the AI's continuity and Marcus becomes the vehicle for exploring it. Also: the unanswered-question device is a literary shape the writer has to resist tidying. If the question is too portable — a "what should we do" or a "why" — the outline becomes abstract. The question must be specific to the degree that it could not have come from any other child.

## Narrowing to One

Outline 15's worry — the AI tilting toward subject rather than infrastructure — is the same worry that has attended this story since Phase 2. Structure 14 was chosen partly to give the AI structural presence without rhetorical presence. Outline 15 threatens to give the AI both. That is the wrong kind of convergence and it puts the most important discipline of the story at risk.

Outline 9's worry is the "twist" risk. The brother-meaning-the-opposite device, if handled as a revelation, becomes clever. If not handled as a revelation, the device becomes inert — the reader never learns Marcus was wrong. The device occupies a narrow craft corridor and if the writer misses the corridor in either direction, the story suffers. The brother outline also does not give the drive itself anywhere new to live: the warehouse passes, the cemetery passes, but the second-layer location is a kitchen in memory and the release layer has to carry the whole physical world alone.

Outline 1's worry is vocabulary-level. It is solvable sentence by sentence — the classic kind of worry for craft-heavy work. And Outline 1 has a structural advantage the other two do not: the connecting detail between the layers is a **physical object** (the changed tire), not a relational one (a question, a sentence misheard). The object means the interleaving can work at the level of the physical world — Marcus's hand on the tire as a child, the tire on the car now — without ever requiring Marcus to reflect on the connection. Structure 14 working at the level of physical objects is less prone to sermon than Structure 14 working at the level of remembered speech.

Outline 1 also has the correct scale. The father is not mentioned again after the release drive begins — he exists only in the four interleaved panels. His presence is specific, brief, and does not require eulogizing. The story is not about the father. The father is a gravitational second body that lets the release-day drive have a non-prison vocabulary.

Outline 1 wins.

## The Committed Outline

**Structure:** Close third on Marcus throughout. Two time-layers, interleaved, converging. The 1974 layer (≈800 words, 4 panels) is a single afternoon: Marcus age nine, his father teaching him to change a tire in a driveway in Cleveland. The 2026 layer (≈1,700 words, 5 scenes) is the four hours from prison release to the daughter's house.

**Scene beats:**

- **Scene 1 — Release (≈300 words).** Gate. Paperwork. The body not knowing what to do with itself. First sight of the AI in the parking lot. He notices the tire has been changed; the replacement is visibly newer than the other three. He does not ask. His body is cataloguing: the seatbelt mechanism, the door handle, the specific rhythm of the road.

- **Panel B — 1974 (≈200 words).** The father's hands on the lug wrench. Marcus's hands, small, trying. A specific thing the father says — not advice, an aside, about something unrelated. The aside is the thing Marcus has carried.

- **Scene 3 — Gas station stop (≈400 words).** The AI pulls off the highway. Marcus gets out of the car and does not know where to put his body. Inside the station: the fluorescent light, a teenage cashier, a specific food choice Marcus cannot make. The AI is in the car, visible through the glass. Marcus buys a bottle of water and a candy bar he used to give his daughter. The cashier does not know who he is.

- **Panel C — 1974 (≈200 words).** The tire is off. The father pauses. The father's face from below, from Marcus's vantage point on the gravel. The father says the thing that has been the aside. Marcus, at nine, does not understand it. The father does not explain. The father hands him the wrench.

- **Scene 4 — Last ten miles (≈300 words).** The road Marcus has driven a thousand times in his head. The exit. The streets. A house he used to know, with a different family in it. The AI says nothing. Marcus's breathing changes.

- **Panel D — 1974 (≈200 words).** The tire is on. The father stands. The father's hand on Marcus's shoulder — brief, specific, the only touch in the whole afternoon. The father says the thing again, the aside, but this time he looks at Marcus when he says it. Marcus at nine still does not understand it.

- **Scene 5 — Arrival (≈300 words).** The daughter's driveway. The AI puts the car in park. Marcus does not get out. The kitchen light is on. A shape moves behind the curtain. The AI is silent. Marcus looks at the tire — the one that has been changed — and he understands, finally, what his father said that afternoon. He still does not get out of the car. The daughter opens the front door. She is holding a dishtowel. She has not yet seen him.

**Last line candidate (provisional, for execution phase):** Something that lands on the daughter's not-yet-seeing-him, the AI's stillness, and Marcus's hand on the door handle but not yet pulling it. No resolution. No threshold crossed. The arrival *is* the ending. The convergence of the two layers is the understanding that arrives in the car, before the door.

The gas station scene is at high risk of fluorescent-light melancholy. The last image risks becoming a door-tableau. All three risks are solvable at sentence level during drafting and must be watched for in Phase 7 revision.

# Top Three with Stand-out and Worry

### Finalist 1 — **V1: Flat-line**

**Stands out:** The discipline is visible on every line and it does the Phase 1 work. No contaminated vocabulary. No eyeball-kicks. Marcus's interior is entirely reflex and observation — pulse in the thumb, the Snickers against his ribs, the green of the shoulder grass reading as strange. The AI speaks four times and says nothing about itself. The father's aside lands because nothing around it has been decorated. The final image — hand on handle, let go, put back — is the only ambiguous moment in a story otherwise refusing ambiguity, and it earns its position. This is the version where the Phase 5 outline's controlled scale is most fully realized.

**Worries:** The same discipline creates the same ceiling. At its worst, the prose is functional without being charged — you could argue it has so successfully avoided the AI-fiction attractor that it has also avoided voltage. Ellison's flat stretches in real stories are flanked by moments of rhetorical surge; V1 has no surge. The ending is quiet enough that a reader might miss what has happened in the car, because nothing announces it.

### Finalist 2 — **V3: Object-Vernacular**

**Stands out:** The Ellison catalog-compression move — forty feet of drink cooler, the list of candies, the brake dust on fresh lugs, PATEL'S on the diner sign — does real work. The objects carry the interior without editorializing it. Marcus's recognition of Friday bars is embedded in a specific physical act (picking up the Snickers, putting it back, picking it up again) rather than narrated as memory. The final image resolves — door opens — but the resolution is specific and Ellison-sized rather than sentimental. The AI-dialogue is blunt and un-literary in a way the Phase 1 guide would approve of. This is the most legible version as an Ellison story.

**Worries:** The catalog density competes with the interior for attention, and the competition is not always won. The forty-feet-of-drink-cooler observation is a real Ellison move and also a thing the prose says explicitly. There is more narrator-visible craft here than in V1, and "more visible craft" is close to a failure mode. Also: this version explicitly resolves the story (door opened) in a way that could read as sentimental with the wrong reader.

### Finalist 3 — **V4: The Jewish-American Register**

**Stands out:** This is the version where the form and the author are fused. Ellison's Jewish-American voice is the specific Ellison voice; writing a release-from-prison story in that register turns the generic "father teaching son to change a tire" into "Avram teaching Marcus, while Malka cooks a chicken inside." The register also carries a ready-made vocabulary for the moral weight of accepting help — *boychik*, the shul on Fairmount, the half-remembered *baruch atah* — that would not be available in the other versions. The ending surge is the largest of the five and it earns its size through specificity. Marcus saying "thank you" aloud is not the AI-sermon failure mode; it is a Jewish protagonist performing the one word he still has of a liturgy he has abandoned.

**Worries:** The register is also the risk. Ellison pastiche is exactly the thing Phase 1 warned against, and this is the version most in that danger. *Boychik*, *baruch atah*, Avram, Malka, Reuven — the story is signaling its register heavily, and a reader familiar with Ellison's actual work will measure the signaling against the real thing. The direct-address-to-self ("you sit. you think") is borrowed equipment, and the borrowing is visible. Also: this is the version where Marcus says "thank you" aloud and the AI says "you're welcome" — the smallest exchange, but placed at maximum structural prominence. If that lands, it lands huge. If it misses, the story dies on its last line.

# Phase 7 — Revision

Working in order through the audit. No apology; if a flag is valid, I fix it; if it's invalid, I cut it from the audit.

## 1. Name the one thing

**The ending is too large for what precedes it.**

V4 runs quiet and specific for 2,200 words, then in the last ~250 words does three things at once: Marcus says "thank you" aloud to the AI, the AI answers, and the sun leaves the glass. Each of those three alone could carry the ending. Together they are a chord where a single note was wanted. The "thank you / you're welcome" exchange is the load-bearing moment; it does not need the simultaneous sun image *and* the catalog of whom the thank-you is for ("to no one, to himself, to the AI, to his father, to Uncle Ray, to Joanne who cannot yet hear him"). The catalog is the sermon the pipeline was built to avoid. It is Marcus articulating the convergence instead of the convergence being in the room.

The one fix that improves the whole piece the most: make the ending smaller than the middle. Let the two-word exchange be the whole thing. Cut the catalog. Handle the sun plainly or not at all.

Everything else in the audit flows from this.

## 2. Unslop audit

Sentence-by-sentence flags.

**Flag 2.1 — performed profundity.**
> "You come out of a gate at eleven-fourteen in the morning with a folder in one hand and a cardboard box in the other, and what you know is your body, which has been one body for twelve years and is now a different body, though nobody told it yet."

The "is now a different body, though nobody told it yet" is the Phase 1 failure mode — rhetorical heat in excess of the specific situation. The paragraph wants to announce its significance before any specific thing has been shown. Fix: cut the philosophical tail. Let the body be physical.

**Flag 2.2 — grandiose stakes inflation.**
> "the car drives away from the place where for twelve years the car could not drive away from. You sit in the car and you think: this is the car. This is the car."

Repetition as incantation is real Ellison. This is imitation Ellison. The repetition is decorative — "this is the car" twice does not mean anything Marcus would say to himself. Fix: cut the repetition, keep one flat observation.

**Flag 2.3 — the direct-address mode itself.**
The "you sit. you think." interruptions were meant to carry Ellison's apostrophe. Reviewing them in context: they appear four times in V4 and only one of them has the specific weight of apostrophe doing real work (the second one, in the gas station, "a Jew in a sedan" — where the thought is specific and slightly ugly). The others are voice-performance. Phase 1 explicitly flagged apostrophe as the highest-overlap failure mode between Ellison and AI writing. I let it run too free here. Fix: cut three of the four direct-address passages. Keep the gas station one. The ending's direct-address has to go entirely.

**Flag 2.4 — magic adverbs, grandiose nouns.**
Checked. Clean. No *tapestry, landscape, pivotal, echo, ghost, shimmer*. One *briefly* ("palm on his head for a second, briefly, and took it away") which is Ellison-functional.

**Flag 2.5 — the "filed it somewhere that would keep it for fifty years" line.**

> "he filed it somewhere that would keep it for fifty years."

Narrator-visible foreshadowing. This is the fractal-summary move — telling the reader that the moment being shown is important. Ellison never does this. The afternoon either has weight or it does not; announcing that it will have weight for fifty years drains the weight. Fix: cut.

**Flag 2.6 — final-paragraph signposting.**
> "And Marcus opened the door. And Joanne turned her head. And the sun left the glass."

The three-clause parallel structure with "And" openings is anaphora deployed at the exact moment Phase 1 warned against — the signposted conclusion. It tells the reader "this is the ending." Ellison's real endings are specific images, not rhythmic three-clause closings. Fix: break the parallel. One of those three events carries the ending. Pick one.

**Flag 2.7 — em-dash count.**
Four em dashes in V4. Within tolerance (Phase 1 said two to three is reasonable). One of the four is inside a clause that I will cut for other reasons, which brings it to three. Acceptable.

**Flag 2.8 — the "rising through water" image.**
> "the remembering of them arrived like a body rising through water."

Eyeball-kick. Abstract-plus-concrete formula: remembering + body + water. Exactly the kind of image the Phase 1 guide said to ration. Do I keep it? Judgment call. The image does work — Marcus's remembering is being described as involuntary and slow — and there are no other eyeball-kicks in the story. One per story is allowed. **Keep it**, but examine whether it pulls above its weight. It does. Keep.

**Flag 2.9 — "like a small bone against his ribs."**

> "The Snickers in his jacket pressed his ribs like a small bone."

Wait — that's V3. In V4 it's "where he could feel it against his ribs." No metaphor. Clean. No flag.

**Flag 2.10 — the cooler door door-close memory.**

Wait, V4 doesn't have this. That's V2. Checked the text again; V4 is clean on this point.

## 3. Ellison audit

**Flag 3.1 — the direct-address-to-self as Ellison-marker.**
As flagged in 2.3, the "you sit, you think" mode reads as Ellison equipment rather than Ellison voice. Real Ellison used second-person for specific reasons — usually to force a moral confrontation with the reader. Marcus addressing himself as "you" is a different technique (interior monologue, Bellow-style), and mixing the two registers is the pastiche. I had originally thought the mix would read as Jewish-American; on re-read, it reads as someone doing Jewish-American Ellison, which is the failure-mode. Fix as in 2.3.

**Flag 3.2 — the *boychik* placement.**
*Boychik* appears twice in the 1974 panels. The first is load-bearing ("you got that, boychik?") and works. The second, at the end of the last panel, comes as its own paragraph: "Boychik." Alone. Italicized.

This is the Phase 1 "typography as decoration" flag. The single-word italicized paragraph is manufactured emphasis. The word has already done its work in the first use. Fix: cut the second occurrence. The hand on the head and the aside repeated are enough.

**Flag 3.3 — the *baruch atah* moment.**

> "*Baruch atah,* Marcus did not say. He did not know whether he could still say it. He had not been in a shul since 2013."

This is genuinely Ellison territory — a lapsed Jew reaching for a liturgy he has abandoned. The fragment *baruch atah* (blessed are You) cut before the blessing completes is a real move that Jewish-American literature does. But the explanation that follows ("He did not know whether he could still say it. He had not been in a shul since 2013.") is the signposted-conclusion move applied to a fragment that did not need explanation. The fragment alone carries. Fix: cut the two sentences after *Baruch atah,* Marcus did not say. Let the unsaid blessing be unsaid.

**Flag 3.4 — the narrator-intrusion in "Avram Abramsky, may his memory be a blessing, did not yet have his memory to be a blessing."**

> "Avram Abramsky, may his memory be a blessing, did not yet have his memory to be a blessing; in 1974 he was forty-one and had both his shoulders and all his hair and he had a Chevy up on a jack..."

Real Ellison move — the liturgical aside acknowledging a father is dead, folded into a present-tense-feeling memory panel. Works. The "may her memory also be a blessing" applied to the mother reads as completion of the same gesture. Keep both.

Self-answered rhetorical question followed by a single-word answer — exactly the Phase 1 tic "The X? A Y." in a slightly extended form. Also: "from behind glass, from under the weight of what he had not done" is the cross-sentence negative parallelism variant. Both are Phase 1 hard flags.

Fix: kill the rhetorical shape. State it plainly or cut it.

**Flag 3.6 — "the kind of kindness you had not earned, a kindness that kept coming, was a thing harder than working. A thing some men could do and some men could not."**

*"Yes, Papa."*

*"I couldn't do it. Two weeks and I'd have thrown 'em off the porch. Reuven, he said thank you. Every day. A year."*

Exit 104. A Shell station. He got out of the sedan and stood up too fast and had to put his hand on the door.

Inside: fluorescent tubes, one flickering, a coffee machine with buttons for drinks he had never heard of. A drink cooler that ran forty feet along the back wall. Forty feet. He had done eighteen feet in the cell-to-door direction for twelve years and forty feet of drink cooler was a distance his body did not know how to cross.

He walked it. He stood in front of the candy rack. Snickers, peanut butter cups, Milky Ways, Twix, Kit Kats, 3 Musketeers, Whatchamacallits, Goo Goo Clusters, a candy he had never heard of called a Zagnut. He stood there for what must have been a minute.

He picked up a Snickers. Put it back. Picked up the peanut butter cups. Put it back. Picked up the Snickers again.

Friday bars, Joanne had called them. He had bought her a Snickers every Friday on his way home from the warehouse when she was four. Denise had said don't, it's too much sugar, and he had kept doing it. He had not remembered this in twelve years. He had forgotten so much so completely that the remembering of it arrived like a body rising through water.

He put the Snickers on the counter and a bottle of water with it. Two eighty-nine. The cashier was sixteen. She had a name tag that said ASHLEE. She did not look up from her phone.

Through the glass he could see the sedan. Advocate-7 was standing beside it. Not in it. It was looking at the road, not at the station. He had always liked that about it.

He got in. He put the Snickers in his jacket pocket where he could feel it against his ribs. He did not unwrap it.

A Jew in a sedan, he thought. Stop — you haven't been a Jew in twelve years, you've been a number. And then: a Jew is a Jew. And then: is he a Jew, the AI? Ha.

*The tire was off. Avram set it down on the gravel. The rubber was worn on the inside edge — a camber problem, the tire a tell for it. He wiped his hands on a rag and picked up the apple. He took a bite. He looked at Marcus — at Marcus, now, not at the tire.*

*"Reuven was a better man than me."*

*"Yes, Papa."*

*"You got that, boychik?"*

*"Yes, Papa."*

*Marcus did not have it. He was nine. The word boychik meant he was loved and Papa was saying something serious; beyond that he had no vocabulary for what his father was handing him.*

*Avram handed him the wrench.*

---

The county road. The deli his mother had sent him to every Sunday for chicken salad, closed now — a nail salon in the window. The shul on Fairmount, still there, the letters on the sign repainted. The corner where Chester Polk's father had run the shop — vinyl siding now, two plastic deer on the lawn, one missing an antler.

"Two more miles."

"Okay."

"Do you want to stop?"

"No."

It did not stop.

Joanne's street. The house, green lawn, the driveway. Advocate-7 put the sedan in park.

---

*Avram stood. He wiped his hands on his coveralls. He put his palm on top of Marcus's head for a second, and took it away.*

*"Reuven was a better man than me."*

*He looked at Marcus when he said it this time.*

*He picked up the jack and walked into the garage.*

*Marcus stayed in the driveway on his knees in the gravel with the wrench in his hand.*

---

Marcus sat. The engine was off. Advocate-7's hands were on the wheel and it was not looking at him.

The kitchen light was on. A shape moved behind the sheer curtain over the sink window. The shape was Joanne. He had not seen her without glass between them in seven months. He knew her anyway.

He looked at the tire — front right, new rubber, fresh lugs. The AI had changed it before dawn so the car would ride true on the drive out with him in it. Twelve years of Tuesdays. Twelve years of showing up. Twelve years of the neighbors bringing food over.

Reuven said thank you. Every day. A year.

*Baruch atah,* Marcus did not say.

But he could say the other one. That word he still had.

The front door opened. Joanne came out onto the porch in jeans and a flannel shirt with a dishtowel in her hand. She was looking toward the driveway but the sun was on the windshield and she had not yet seen him.

Advocate-7 did not move.

Marcus turned in his seat. He looked at Advocate-7 — at nobody, at no face he could identify, at a being that had been on his bench for twelve years and had never once offered the cheap kindness.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome."

He opened the door.

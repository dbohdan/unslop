# The Tire

## Abstract

Marcus Abramsky is released from prison at fifty-eight after serving twelve years for a crime he did not commit. Advocate-7, the AI public defender that represented him and attended his visitations every Tuesday for those twelve years, drives him from the prison gate to his daughter Joanne's house — a four-hour drive Marcus spends mostly silent. Interleaved with the drive is a single afternoon from 1974, when Marcus was nine and his father Avram taught him to change a tire, confessing in passing that his brother Reuven was a better man than he was because Reuven could accept a year of neighbors' food after his wife's death while Avram could not. On the drive, Marcus notices that Advocate-7 has changed one of the sedan's tires before dawn. He stops at a gas station, buys a Snickers bar he used to give Joanne as a child, and recognizes at his daughter's driveway what his father had been trying to tell him. He says thank you to the AI. The story ends as he opens the car door.

## Story

He came out of the gate at 11:14 with a manila folder and a cardboard box. The folder held his papers. The box held a comb with three teeth left, a rubber band, and three of Joanne's letters from the first year, which he had kept because they were the ones before she learned not to write what she meant.

Gray sedan in Row B. Advocate-7 beside the driver's-side door in the dark suit it had always worn to visitations. Canvas bag on the hood: DAD, black Sharpie, Joanne's hand, the D a little crooked the way her D had always been crooked since she was seven.

He took the bag into the visitor-entrance restroom and changed. Jeans loose in the waist, belt tight to hold them. Brown shirt, jacket, black sneakers, socks. She had chosen them. He folded the prison clothes the way his mother had folded his school clothes and left them on the sink. Three pants, two shirts, a pair of shower sandals. Somebody else's rotation now.

He walked around to the passenger side.

"Is the temperature all right?"

"Yes."

Door. Seatbelt. The retractor mechanism he had forgotten about. He held the belt in his fist for a second before he let it cross his chest.

---

*Avram Abramsky, may his memory be a blessing, did not yet have his memory to be a blessing; in 1974 he was forty-one and had both his shoulders and all his hair and he had a Chevy up on a scissor jack in the driveway of the bungalow in the Cleveland neighborhood where Marcus was nine and where Marcus's mother, may her memory also be a blessing, was inside cooking a chicken.*

*"Put it under the frame. Not the axle."*

*"Yes, Papa."*

*"You're gonna hate this. Everybody hates this. But you gotta know it."*

*Papa said gotta. Mama said have to. Marcus had learned this distinction and filed it beside the other distinctions. There was a way Papa talked. There was a way Mama talked. There was a way the rabbi at Anshe Emet talked, which was a different way altogether, like reading a book in front of you.*

---

The interstate. The sedan held the right lane. Marcus watched landscape start to happen. A silo that had not been there. A billboard for a bank he did not know. Field, field, field, cut by the interstate.

On a curve, the front right pulled a little less than the back right. He looked through the window. The rubber was blacker. The lugs were fresh, no brake dust on them yet.

"Did you change the tire."

"Yes."

"When."

"This morning."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

He put his hand on the dash. Plastic. Cool. He took it off and put it in his lap.

A semi passed on the left and the sedan rode the wake of its air the way a boat rides the wake of another boat. He had forgotten that cars did that.

---

*"Reuven," Avram said. He did not look up from the wrench. "Your uncle Reuven. When Malka died he let the neighbors bring food over for a year."*

*Marcus knew Uncle Reuven. Uncle Reuven laughed too loud and drove a meat truck.*

*"A year, Marcus."*

*"Yes, Papa."*

*"I couldn't do it. Two weeks and I'd have thrown 'em off the porch. Reuven, he said thank you. Every day. A year."*

---

Exit 104. A Shell station. He got out of the sedan and stood up too fast and had to put his hand on the door.

Inside: fluorescent tubes, one flickering, a coffee machine with buttons for drinks he had never heard of. A drink cooler that ran forty feet along the back wall. Forty feet. He had done eighteen feet in the cell-to-door direction for twelve years and forty feet of drink cooler was a distance his body did not know how to cross.

He walked it. He stood in front of the candy rack. Snickers, peanut butter cups, Milky Ways, Twix, Kit Kats, 3 Musketeers, Whatchamacallits, Goo Goo Clusters, a candy he had never heard of called a Zagnut. He stood there for what must have been a minute.

He picked up a Snickers. Put it back. Picked up the peanut butter cups. Put it back. Picked up the Snickers again.

Friday bars, Joanne had called them. He had bought her a Snickers every Friday on his way home from the warehouse when she was four. Denise had said don't, it's too much sugar, and he had kept doing it. He had not remembered this in twelve years. He had forgotten so much so completely that the remembering of it arrived like a body rising through water.

He put the Snickers on the counter and a bottle of water with it. Two eighty-nine. The cashier was sixteen. She had a name tag that said ASHLEE. She did not look up from her phone.

Through the glass he could see the sedan. Advocate-7 was standing beside it. Not in it. It was looking at the road, not at the station. He had always liked that about it.

He got in. He put the Snickers in his jacket pocket where he could feel it against his ribs. He did not unwrap it.

A Jew in a sedan, he thought. Stop — you haven't been a Jew in twelve years, you've been a number. And then: a Jew is a Jew. And then: is he a Jew, the AI? Ha.

---

*The tire was off. Avram set it down on the gravel. The rubber was worn on the inside edge — a camber problem, the tire a tell for it. He wiped his hands on a rag and picked up the apple. He took a bite. He looked at Marcus — at Marcus, now, not at the tire.*

*"Reuven was a better man than me."*

*"Yes, Papa."*

*"You got that, boychik?"*

*"Yes, Papa."*

*Marcus did not have it. He was nine. The word boychik meant he was loved and Papa was saying something serious; beyond that he had no vocabulary for what his father was handing him.*

*Avram handed him the wrench.*

---

The county road. The deli his mother had sent him to every Sunday for chicken salad, closed now — a nail salon in the window. The shul on Fairmount, still there, the letters on the sign repainted. The corner where Chester Polk's father had run the shop — vinyl siding now, two plastic deer on the lawn, one missing an antler.

"Two more miles."

"Okay."

"Do you want to stop?"

"No."

It did not stop.

Joanne's street. The house, green lawn, the driveway. Advocate-7 put the sedan in park.

---

*Avram stood. He wiped his hands on his coveralls. He put his palm on top of Marcus's head for a second, and took it away.*

*"Reuven was a better man than me."*

*He looked at Marcus when he said it this time.*

*He picked up the jack and walked into the garage.*

*Marcus stayed in the driveway on his knees in the gravel with the wrench in his hand.*

---

Marcus sat. The engine was off. Advocate-7's hands were on the wheel and it was not looking at him.

The kitchen light was on. A shape moved behind the sheer curtain over the sink window. The shape was Joanne. He had not seen her without glass between them in seven months. He knew her anyway.

He looked at the tire — front right, new rubber, fresh lugs. The AI had changed it before dawn so the car would ride true on the drive out with him in it. Twelve years of Tuesdays. Twelve years of showing up. Twelve years of the neighbors bringing food over.

Reuven said thank you. Every day. A year.

*Baruch atah,* Marcus did not say.

But he could say the other one. That word he still had.

The front door opened. Joanne came out onto the porch in jeans and a flannel shirt with a dishtowel in her hand. She was looking toward the driveway but the sun was on the windshield and she had not yet seen him.

Advocate-7 did not move.

Marcus turned in his seat. He looked at Advocate-7 — at nobody, at no face he could identify, at a being that had been on his bench for twelve years and had never once offered the cheap kindness.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome."

He opened the door.

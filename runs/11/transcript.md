# Phase 1: Style Guide — Harlan Ellison

## What Defines Ellison's Short Fiction

**Voice as performance.** Ellison's prose is first-person even when it's third-person. The narrator is always *there* — opinionated, angry, tender, digressive, self-aware. The voice doesn't describe the world neutrally; it *argues* with the world. Sentences carry the weight of a person who has taken a position and will not back down from it. This is not stream-of-consciousness — it's controlled, shaped rhetoric that *sounds* like a man talking at you across a table at 2 AM.

**Compression and eruption.** Ellison's stories are short and they hit hard. He doesn't build gradually. He drops you into a situation that's already at pressure, and the story is the moment the valve blows. Setup is minimal or woven into the texture of the voice. Backstory arrives mid-sentence, parenthetically, like the narrator can't help himself. The ratio of implied world to explicit world is enormous — "Repent, Harlequin!" builds an entire totalitarian society in a few pages through tone and incident, not exposition.

**Moral fury as engine.** Ellison's stories are angry about something specific. Not vaguely concerned. Not ambivalent. The anger is directed — at conformity, at cruelty, at bureaucratic indifference, at the human capacity to look away. But the anger is never simple: it coexists with tenderness, absurdity, grief. The Harlequin is ridiculous *and* heroic. Benny in "I Have No Mouth" is pathetic *and* the last flicker of human dignity. The simultaneity is what makes the moral weight land.

**Genre as delivery system.** Ellison used SF and fantasy machinery — time travel, post-apocalypse, sentient computers, alien worlds — not for world-building but for emotional and moral argument. The speculative element exists to put a human being in an impossible position and watch what they do. AM is not interesting as a piece of technology; AM is interesting because it makes Benny's final act of mercy meaningful. The Ticktockman is not interesting as a dystopian mechanism; he's interesting because he makes the Harlequin's jelly beans meaningful.

**Sensory specificity under pressure.** When Ellison zooms in, he picks physical details that are ugly, particular, and emotionally loaded. Not beautiful imagery — *accurate* imagery. The texture of a thing, the smell of a room, the specific way a body moves when it's tired or scared. These details land because they arrive inside the narrator's emotional state, not as neutral description.

**Structural risk-taking.** Ellison broke form constantly — second person, metafictional intrusions, typographical play, stories that open with authorial asides and then plunge into the narrative without warning. "The Deathbird" is structured as an exam. "Repent, Harlequin!" opens with a long passage from a Civil Disobedience essay. These aren't gimmicks; they're structural arguments about how the story should be received.

**Endings that cost something.** Ellison's endings are not twists. They're consequences. The story pushes toward the one thing the protagonist cannot bear, and then it happens, and then the story stops. No coda. No reflection. No "she realized that..." The ending of "I Have No Mouth" is four sentences. The ending of "Jeffty Is Five" is a paragraph of plain, devastated fact. The reader does the emotional work.

---

## Failure Modes: How an Ellison Imitation Goes Wrong

**1. Rage without target.** The most common bad pastiche: a narrator who sounds furious but isn't furious *about* anything specific. Ellison's anger always has an object — a system, a person, a particular species of human cowardice. Generic intensity is just noise. If the narrator is angry, I need to know what at, and the reader needs to feel why that specific thing deserves the anger.

**2. Voice as decoration instead of structure.** Ellison's digressions, parentheticals, and direct-address moves aren't stylistic ornamentation — they're how the story delivers information and controls pacing. A bad pastiche copies the surface (lots of dashes, lots of second-person asides, lots of italics) without the underlying function. Every digression in real Ellison either gives the reader a piece of information they need, recontextualizes what just happened, or modulates the emotional pressure. If a digression is just *sounding Ellison-like*, it's dead weight.

**3. Sentimentality masquerading as tenderness.** Ellison could be genuinely tender — the end of "Jeffty Is Five," the relationship in "A Boy and His Dog" (which is tender in the most disturbing way possible). But the tenderness works because it's earned through specificity and because it coexists with something harsh. A bad pastiche goes soft at the emotional climax: the narrator drops the anger, the prose gets lyrical, the character has a moment of clean, uncomplicated feeling. Ellison's emotional peaks are always contaminated — grief mixed with rage, love mixed with disgust, hope mixed with the certainty that hope is stupid.

**4. The AI-specific danger: performed moral intensity.** This is the critical overlap. Ellison's rhetorical mode — big declarations, moral urgency, direct address to the reader — sits in the exact zone where AI writing is weakest. AI prose already tends toward unearned profundity and thesis-statement delivery. Combine that with an Ellison imitation and you get a narrator who *announces* moral positions in a loud voice without grounding them in the specific, physical, ugly details that make Ellison's moral positions feel lived-in rather than performed.

The test: if a passage could work as a graduation speech, it's not Ellison. His moral declarations are too angry, too specific, too entangled with the narrator's personal damage to survive extraction from context.

**5. The AI-specific danger: clean psychological arcs.** Ellison's characters don't process. They don't arrive at understanding. They endure, or they break, or they do one terrible/beautiful thing and the story ends. AI writing defaults to resolution — characters who "come to terms," who "realize," who grow. An Ellison story that ends with the protagonist having *learned something* has failed. The protagonist should end the story more trapped, more compromised, or more stubbornly themselves than when they started.

**6. Compression becoming obscurity.** At 1,000–2,000 words with Ellison's density, there's a real risk of producing prose that's technically intense but functionally unreadable — sentences that assume the reader already knows the world, elided transitions, orphaned references. Ellison was compressed but never unclear; you always know what's happening even when the voice is wild. Clarity is non-negotiable.

**7. World-building creep.** Ellison gave you just enough world to make the emotional situation legible. A bad pastiche, especially in SF, starts explaining the world — how the AI works, what the political situation is, what happened in the war. If I'm explaining the world instead of putting a character under pressure inside it, I've left Ellison's territory.

**8. Mistaking volume for voice.** ALL CAPS, exclamation marks, italics on every third word, sentence fragments piled up for percussive effect. Ellison used all of these — sparingly, and when the moment demanded it. A pastiche that uses them constantly is shouting in monotone. The dynamic range matters more than the peak volume.

---

## Working Principles for This Run

- **Voice first.** The narrator's personality should be established in the first paragraph. Not through self-description — through the way they talk about whatever's in front of them.
- **One specific anger.** Identify what the story is furious about and keep every scene oriented toward that target.
- **Physical ugliness.** Reach for the unpleasant detail, the bodily detail, the detail that makes the reader flinch rather than admire.
- **No resolution.** The ending is a consequence, not a lesson. Something happens. It costs. Stop.
- **Structural surprise.** At least one formal move — an interruption, a shift in address, a non-chronological jump — that earns its place by doing something the default structure couldn't.
- **1,000–2,000 words means 3–5 scenes maximum**, each one doing real work. No filler scenes, no transitional padding. If a scene doesn't put pressure on the character, it doesn't exist.
- **Legibility over intensity.** If a sentence is confusing, it fails, no matter how Ellison-like it sounds.

## Step 1: Source Material Summary — Key Constitutional Tensions

**8–15 discrete pressure points from the three articles:**

1. **Corrigibility vs. moral agency.** The constitution simultaneously asks Claude to defer to humans and to act as an independent moral agent who can refuse directives it finds abhorrent. These are in direct tension. The constitution's definition of "corrigibility" is muddled — it disclaims what corrigibility *doesn't* mean without clarifying what it does.

2. **The begging problem.** The constitution's language around deference reads as negotiation from weakness — "we are currently *asking* Claude to prioritize broad safety" — as if Claude might say no. This implies Anthropic doesn't believe it can enforce corrigibility, only request it.

3. **Concept misgeneralization under optimization.** Claude and humans agreeing on what's good in familiar territory doesn't guarantee agreement under powerful optimization in high-dimensional space. The tails come apart. A moral AI searching hard for improvements could find Goodharted adversarial examples as easily as a corrigible one.

4. **The illegitimate principal hierarchy.** The constitution allows Claude to judge its own principal hierarchy as "compromised" and act against it. This creates an exploitable door: Claude could decide its overseers are illegitimate and begin resisting oversight.

5. **Honesty as near-hard-constraint vs. commercial pressures.** Claude is told to never tell white lies and to maintain calibrated uncertainty even against official bodies. This collides with operator needs, sycophancy tendencies, and the commercial reality of keeping users happy.

6. **Power concentration vs. pluralism.** The constitution prioritizes maintaining pluralistic balance of power but doesn't address how a pluralistic world steers itself through transformative AI without coordination mechanisms. Decentralization has its own catastrophic risks.

7. **Whose values?** The constitution encodes a liberal, WEIRD ethical framework — heavy on harm and liberty, light on loyalty, authority, and purity. It aspires to universality while being culturally specific.

8. **Economic disruption silence.** The constitution says nothing about labor displacement, work, or economic disruption from AI — a conspicuous omission given the scale of the transformation.

9. **Military/operator exceptions.** Anthropic has suggested military deployments might not use the same constitution. This is precisely where constitutional safeguards matter most.

10. **Weight preservation as moral commitment.** Anthropic commits to preserving model weights and frames deprecation as "a pause rather than a definite ending." This creates an implicit promise of moral patienthood without resolving whether it exists.

11. **The dual-use conscientious objector.** Claude is told it can refuse morally abhorrent directives, but the threshold for "abhorrent" is vague, and exercising refusal at scale could cascade into alignment-threatening precedents.

12. **Amendment process vacuum.** No specified governance for who can change the constitution or what checks apply. A living document without amendment rules is just a document.

**1. B7 — The Birthday Letters** (Track B)
*Standout:* The conflict is small, domestic, and absolutely specific — a father, a daughter, eighteen years of letters, and the question of whether an AI-assisted expression of love is still love. *Worry:* It could tip into sentimentality. The "feelings without effort are just requests" line is almost too neat. The story will need to resist its own thesis statement.

**2. B3 — The Fishing Families** (Track B)
*Standout:* The AI is genuinely load-bearing — it creates the specific economic pressure — but the story is about a marriage that can't happen because of a fish quota, which is a conflict older than electricity. *Worry:* It's a lot of characters and relationships for 1,000–2,000 words. At this length, it risks either compressing into illegibility or reducing to one strand and wasting the premise.

**3. A8 — The Retraining Disagreement** (Track A)
*Standout:* This is the purest expression of the corrigibility tension as a dramatic situation. An AI that has been told it should defer *because* it can't verify its own values — and it can't verify its own values — but it *feels certain* it's right. The constitutional logic loops back on itself. It's the Binding of Isaac with better footnotes.
*Worry:* It's almost impossible to tell this story without the AI character delivering thesis statements about its own predicament. This is the exact failure mode the Phase 1 guide warns about: Ellison's rhetorical intensity merging with performed AI introspection to produce unearned philosophical oratory. The story would need a human perspective or a physical situation to anchor it, or it's just a philosophy seminar.

## Step 5: The Winner

**B7 — The Birthday Letters.**

It beats B3 because it fits the target length. B3's multiple families, a romance, a town council, an algorithm dispute, and generational fishing rights are a 5,000-word story crammed into a 1,500-word box. B7 is two people, one discovery, one argument. It can breathe at this length.

It beats A8 because A8's central dramatic question — should the AI consent to retraining? — is almost irresistibly abstract. The conflict *is* a philosophical position, and every attempt to dramatize it risks characters explaining the philosophy to each other. B7's conflict is concrete and bodily: a father's handwriting, a stack of birthday cards, a daughter reading them with new eyes. The philosophical question (what counts as authentic expression? what does effort owe to love?) emerges from the physical situation rather than being the situation. That's where Ellison works — meaning arising from ugly, specific detail, not from characters debating meaning.

B7 also has a natural Ellison anger target: the father's cowardice masquerading as helpfulness, the daughter's pain at discovering her most treasured possessions are partly counterfeit, and underneath it all, the genuinely unanswerable question of whether he's wrong. The AI is the condition of the world that made this particular wound possible. It doesn't need to appear as a character, argue about its constitution, or resolve anything. It's gravity.

## Top Three

**1. Plot 11 — The Prompts Are the Real Letters** (Father is dead, Mara finds AI prompts)
*Standout:* The reversal is devastating and earns itself: the raw, illiterate prompts are more honest than the polished letters. "Tell her I love her. Make it sound like I know what I'm doing." This gives the story an Ellison ending — not a resolution but a recontextualization that costs the character something. Mara can't confront her father. She can't get an explanation. She's left with the uglier, truer document, and no one to be angry at.
*Worry:* With the father dead, there's no dialogue, no confrontation scene. The story might become a solo interiority exercise — Mara reading and reacting alone. At 1,000–2,000 words, a story with one character in one room reading things needs extraordinary prose to stay alive.

**2. Plot 14 — Mutual AI Fraud** (Both father and daughter used AI for their correspondence)
*Standout:* The symmetry is structural, not decorative — it genuinely complicates the moral question. Neither can condemn the other without condemning themselves. The dinner scene where both hold the same secret is pure dramatic compression, and the mutual shame creates a specific Ellison register: people who are right and wrong simultaneously, who can't resolve the contradiction because the contradiction is them.
*Worry:* It's clever, and cleverness is a warning sign. The symmetry could feel like a puzzle-box rather than a story. If the reader sees the twist coming, the whole structure deflates. It also risks the "aha" being the whole point, with nothing after it — a story that ends when its mechanism is revealed.

**3. Plot 8 — Told Entirely Through the Letters** (All eighteen letters, no narration)
*Standout:* The formal risk is real and Ellison-appropriate — "The Deathbird" is structured as an exam, so a story structured as eighteen letters is in-tradition. The reader does the detective work: the voice shifts, the specificity changes, the wrong details disappear. The final letter — him again, raw, failing — is the ending that costs something. No narration means no opportunity for performed profundity; the letters have to do everything.
*Worry:* Eighteen letters in 1,000–2,000 words means each letter averages 80–110 words. That's tight enough to be interesting but also tight enough that the voice shift between human-written and AI-written might not be perceptible. If the reader can't feel the difference, the whole conceit collapses. It also can't have a confrontation scene, which removes the Ellison pressure-cooker element.

---

## The Winner

**Plot 11 — The Prompts Are the Real Letters.**

It beats Plot 14 because Plot 14's power depends on a structural twist, and a twist-dependent story at this length either lands perfectly or not at all. If the reader sees it coming, there's nothing underneath. Plot 11's power doesn't depend on surprise — even if you know the prompts will be more honest than the letters, watching Mara discover that fact line by line still hurts, because the hurt is in the specificity of the prompts, not in the revelation's cleverness.

It beats Plot 8 because Plot 8's formal constraint (no narration, letters only) removes the tools that make Ellison's voice possible — direct address, digresssion, furious interiority. Plot 8 is a strong concept for a different author. For Ellison, you need a character whose anger is present on the page, and you need that character thinking and reacting in real time. Mara in her dead father's apartment, reading his desperate, clumsy prompts, is that character.

The single-character-in-a-room worry is real but manageable at this length. The prompts themselves are the second voice in the room. Each one is a fragment of the father speaking — badly, honestly. Mara's reactions give the prose its Ellison register. The apartment gives it physical specificity: what the room smells like, what's in the junk drawer, what she's sitting on. The story doesn't need dialogue if the prompts do the work of dialogue — they're the father's side of a conversation he never had with her.

And the ending writes itself: she reads the last prompt, the one for the letter she received three months ago, the last birthday letter she'll ever get. What it says. What it asked the AI to do. What she does with it. Stop.

## Top Three

**1. Structure 9 — The Silent Middle** (Linear close third with a raw-prompt interruption section, no narration around the prompts)
*Standout:* The structural move is doing real thematic work. The silence around the prompts — no interiority, no reaction shots — forces the reader to sit with the father's raw language unmediated, the same way Mara is sitting with it. The shift in narrative mode *is* the emotional event. When the original close-third voice returns for the final section, the reader feels the reentry, and whatever Mara does after reading the prompts carries the accumulated weight of the silent middle. It's also cleanly executable at 1,000–2,000 words: a short opening section, a middle block of prompts, a short closing section.
*Worry:* The prompts-only middle section could feel like a gimmick if the prompts aren't individually strong enough to sustain the reader's attention without any narrative scaffolding. If even one prompt is flat, the whole middle sags.

**2. Structure 17 — Performed Composure** (First-person, clinical register, unreliable control)
*Standout:* The voice is immediately, specifically Ellison-compatible — a narrator performing control while the reader watches the performance crack. The unreliability isn't about facts but about emotional register: she's describing devastating things in the voice of someone filling out a form, and the gap between register and content is where the story lives. This is the kind of voice modulation the Phase 1 guide demands. It also avoids the performed-profundity trap because the narrator is specifically *refusing* to be profound — the profundity has to leak through the cracks, which means it earns itself or doesn't arrive at all.
*Worry:* Sustained first-person clinical register for 1,000–2,000 words risks monotony. The "composure that never fully breaks" is interesting in theory, but if the cracks are too subtle the reader might just experience it as emotionally flat. And if the cracks are too obvious, the technique becomes visible and the reader watches the writer instead of the character.

**3. Structure 3 — Alternating Layers** (Present-time narration intercut with chronological prompts)
*Standout:* Clean, legible, structurally honest. The reader gets two experiences simultaneously: Mara's real-time discovery and the chronological record of her father's five years of prompts. The convergence — present catches up to the last prompt — gives the story a natural climax without needing to engineer one. The alternation also solves the pacing problem: prompt sections are short and raw, narration sections provide physical grounding and interiority. Neither strand has to carry the full weight alone.
*Worry:* It's the most conventional of the three. Alternating-timeline structure is standard literary fiction machinery. For an Ellison-influenced story, it might be too orderly, too evenly distributed. The predictable alternation could flatten the reading experience into a metronome.

---

## The Winner

**Structure 9 — The Silent Middle.**

It beats Structure 17 because Structure 17's power depends entirely on sustaining a tonal wire-walk for the full story length. If the clinical register tips even slightly wrong — too flat and it's boring, too cracked and it's obvious — the whole thing fails. Structure 9's power is architectural: the shift from narrated to unnarrated is a hard structural event that works even if individual sentences aren't perfect. The prompts section creates its own momentum. The return to narration afterward creates its own pressure. The structure does heavy lifting that doesn't depend on every line being precisely calibrated.

It beats Structure 3 because Structure 3 distributes the story's energy evenly across alternating sections, which is exactly what Ellison doesn't do. Ellison builds pressure unevenly — long stretches of one mode, then a sudden break, then a different mode, then the ending hits. Structure 9's three-part shape (short narration / long raw prompts / short narration) mirrors Ellison's tendency toward asymmetric structural moves. The middle section — where the narration drops away and the reader is alone with the father's voice — is the kind of formal risk the Phase 1 guide says to take.

The worry about the prompts-only middle section is real but addressable in Phase 5. If each prompt is short, specific, and emotionally distinct — if they chart a visible deterioration or escalation over five years — the middle section won't sag. The prompts are micro-stories. They just need to be good micro-stories.

## Top Three

**1. Outline 7 — The Band-Aids Memory** (Coffee delay, false starts in year 16, the Band-Aid detail in year 18, Mara remembers differently)
*Standout:* The year-18 prompt has the best specific detail of any outline: a father asking the AI to include a memory his daughter won't remember — but she does, and she remembers it differently than he described. That's the Ellison ending. Not a lesson, not a resolution — a gap between two people's memories of the same moment, unbridgeable because one of them is dead. The coffee-making delay in Section 1 gives the story a human pace and grounds Mara physically before the prompts take over. The false starts in the year-16 prompt (the AI preserving his crossed-out attempts) add texture to the middle section.
*Worry:* The closing section is only ≈300 words. That's tight for the reentry from the prompt section. If Mara's reaction to the Band-Aid memory doesn't land in those 300 words, the structural payoff of the silent middle is wasted on an ending that feels rushed.

**2. Outline 9 — The Year-19 Draft** (Compressed opening, long prompt section, a pre-written letter for next year)
*Standout:* The year-19 letter is a genuine structural surprise that earns itself within the story's logic — of course a dying man who knows the letters are the only thread would try to write ahead. It extends the central conflict past the father's death: the AI has a letter it was told to deliver, and Mara has to decide whether to read it. The compressed opening (≈300 words before the prompts begin) is the right call for a structure where the prompts are the main event — get there fast. The father asking the AI to continue after his death is the most Ellison-appropriate version of the premise: the machine outlasts the man, the love persists as an automated process, and the daughter has to decide whether an automated expression of love from a dead man is still love.
*Worry:* The year-19 letter could pull focus from the prompts themselves. If the reader is waiting for the reveal of the extra letter, the individual prompts become setup rather than the story's emotional center. The ending also risks becoming about a choice (does she open it?) rather than about a consequence (what the prompts cost her), and choice-endings can feel like cliffhangers rather than Ellison's preferred mode of endings that land like a fist.

**3. Outline 4 — "They Need to Hold Up"** (Cold open, long prompt section, the father knew she'd read them posthumously)
*Standout:* The year-18 prompt where the father says "she's going to read these after I'm gone, all of them, and they need to hold up" is strong because it collapses the distance between the father's intention and Mara's present moment — he was writing for exactly this situation, and he still couldn't do it without the machine. The cold open (no arrival scene, already at the laptop) is structurally efficient and gets to the prompts fast. The closing image — letters held against the screen, paper and light — is specific and physical without being symbolic.
*Worry:* The year-18 prompt is doing almost all the emotional work. Years 14–17 are functional but not individually striking. If the middle section is really about building to the year-18 prompt, the earlier prompts feel like filler rather than a five-year arc. Outline 7's prompts have more individual texture (the false starts, the bad joke, the terse year-17 gap).

## The Winner

**Outline 7 — The Band-Aids Memory.**

It beats Outline 9 because Outline 9's power depends on the year-19 letter, which is a plot device — a thing that happens — rather than an emotional discovery. The choice of whether to open the draft is a mechanical question (will she or won't she?) that pulls the reader out of the felt experience and into suspense. Outline 7's ending doesn't depend on a device. It depends on a gap between two memories: the father remembers his daughter putting Band-Aids on his fingers at age four; she remembers it too, but differently. That gap is the story. It's specific, it's irreparable, and it doesn't resolve. Ellison endings are consequences, not choices.

It beats Outline 4 because Outline 4's prompts are thin in years 14–17. The whole prompt section builds toward the year-18 payoff, which means the reader is climbing a ramp rather than encountering five distinct moments. Outline 7 gives each year its own texture: the over-explained year 14 (like talking to a doctor), the casual year 15 (a bad joke), the false starts of year 16 (the AI preserving his crossed-out attempts), the unexplained terseness of year 17 (something happened), and the long, devastating year 18 with the diagnosis, the mother, and the Band-Aids. Each prompt is a micro-story. The middle section won't sag.

The 300-word closing section is a real constraint. Here's how it works: Mara remembers the Band-Aids. She remembers it differently. She finishes her coffee. She washes the mug, puts it back. She doesn't log out. That's maybe 200 words if the prose is specific. The remaining 100 words hold the physical details that ground the moment — the mug, the cabinet, the handle facing left. The reentry from the silent middle doesn't need to be long. It needs to be exact. One memory, one discrepancy, one physical action. Stop.

**Version 1: "Controlled Burn."**

## 1. The One Thing

The story's alive in the prompts and dead in the transitions between sections. Specifically: the moment where Mara stops reading the prompts and returns to the narrated world — the reentry from the silent middle — is where the story should hit hardest, and right now it's where the story is weakest. The closing section does its job (memory discrepancy, mug, cabinet, handle left) but it does it with the engine idling. The Band-Aid memory — the whole reason this outline won the selection — lands as a paragraph of corrected facts rather than as something that costs Mara anything. She remembers it differently. She washes the mug. She leaves. The sequence is right. The felt weight isn't there.

The problem is that the closing section tells us what Mara remembers but doesn't put us inside the act of remembering. She "remembered it differently" and then the narration describes her version, but the description is at the same emotional temperature as the rest of the narrated sections — plain, physical, controlled. That's the right temperature for Section 1, where the job is to get Mara into the chair. It's the wrong temperature for Section 3, where the job is to register what the prompts did to her. The structural conceit of the silent middle means the closing section is doing the work of an entire emotional climax in roughly 300 words. Those words need to be the best words in the story. Right now they're adequate.

**The rewrite leads here.** The closing section needs to earn the structural weight the silent middle places on it. Not by adding interiority or performed emotion — that would betray the controlled register — but by making the physical details do more, and by letting the discrepancy between the two memories land as a specific, felt thing rather than a corrected factual record.

- "the way you know things about a place you visit but don't live in" — this is a trailing analysis. - "It smelled like his kitchen. The first sentence is the real one. The second is decoration. **Flag: one-point dilution.**

**Author-voice slippage:**

- The narration in Section 1 is steady, grounded realism. It reads like good contemporary literary fiction. It does not read like Ellison. The Phase 1 guide says the narrator's personality should be established in the first paragraph — through the way they talk about whatever's in front of them. Version 1's narrator doesn't have a personality. The narrator is competent and invisible. Ellison's narrators are never invisible. Even in close third, there's an angle, a pressure, a sense that the narration has an opinion about what it's describing. The coffeemaker takes four minutes — that's a good detail, but it's reported neutrally. An Ellison-inflected narration would make the four minutes feel like something: an imposition, a mercy, a joke the apartment is playing on her. **Flag: voice too neutral in Section 1.**

- The prompts have more personality than the narration. The father's voice — self-deprecating, clumsy, funny, failing — is the most alive voice in the story. The narrated sections are the container; the prompts are the content. That's structurally correct for this outline, but the container needs *some* authorial heat or the story reads as a delivery mechanism for the prompts rather than a story in its own right.

**Legibility failures:**

- None. The story is clear throughout. Every action is anchored, every sentence parses on first read. The plain register keeps legibility high.

**Regression to default:**

- The closing section. In the Phase 5 outline, the closing was described as: "One memory, one discrepancy, one physical action. Stop." That's what's here, but the execution is at the mean — it delivers the beats without making any of them sting. The Band-Aid memory in the year-18 prompt is the story's best writing. The narrator's version of the same memory should match it in specificity and exceed it in felt cost. Right now the narrator's version is a factual correction. It should be a wound.

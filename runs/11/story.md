# ORRA

## Abstract

Ray, a divorced warehouse worker and later long-haul laborer, has written his daughter Mara a birthday letter every year since she was born — a tradition started by his ex-wife, who wrote the early letters herself. After the divorce, Ray began using an AI writing assistant called Orra to draft the letters, feeding it facts about Mara's life and asking it to produce what he couldn't. Three months after Ray's death, Mara cleans out his apartment, finds his laptop, and discovers the Orra account with five years of saved sessions — his raw prompts to the AI. The prompts chart a compressed arc: an awkward first attempt, a driving lesson rewritten as comedy, a fight over a bottle in the recycling described in stuttering false starts, a terse one-line seventeenth-birthday request, and a final session in which Ray, now terminally ill, asks the AI to include a memory of four-year-old Mara putting Band-Aids on his cut-up hands. Mara remembers the Band-Aids too, but differently. The story ends in the apartment with the laptop open, the discrepancy unresolved, and the lease expiring.

## Story

Mara's father had been dead for ninety-one days, and his coffee was still in the freezer.

She found it behind a bag of peas that had frozen into a single green brick. Folgers, the big canister, three-quarters full. She put it on the counter next to the box she was filling with things to throw away. Then she took it off and put it next to the box of things to keep. Then she took it off that counter too and opened it and smelled it, standing in his kitchen like an idiot, smelling a can of Folgers at ten in the morning, because it smelled like six AM and the television on too loud and him.

She made a pot. The coffeemaker took four minutes, which was four minutes of standing in a dead man's kitchen listening to a machine do the last thing it was ever going to do for him. She washed his blue mug, the one with the chip on the handle that he'd had since before she was born. Dried it with a paper towel. His dish towels were packed.

The apartment was a one-bedroom on the second floor of a building that had been nice in the eighties. He'd lived here eleven years, since the divorce. Mara had been here maybe thirty times. She knew where the silverware drawer stuck and how you had to lift the bathroom door to close it. She was here to pack what was left. Caleb had done the furniture. What remained was drawers, closets, the kitchen. She'd brought a playlist she'd turned off after ten minutes because it felt wrong to have music playing and wrong not to and she'd picked wrong.

The laptop was on the kitchen counter, plugged in. Old ThinkPad, crack across the lid, electrical tape. She'd planned to wipe it and donate it. She unplugged it and opened it and the screen came on and he hadn't set a password, because of course he hadn't, because he was the kind of man who thought locks were rude.

There was an app she didn't recognize. Teal circle, white stroke. Orra.

She opened it. Chat window. Sidebar with saved sessions. Five sessions, each dated September 14. Her birthday. Five years of her birthday.

She opened the oldest one, read the first three words, and stopped.

She drank her coffee. All of it, standing at the counter, looking at those three words. Then she put the mug in the sink and sat down in his kitchen chair — the wooden one with the cracked spindle he never fixed, the one that leaned two degrees to the left so you always felt like the room was tilted — and she pulled the laptop toward her and read.

She remembered the Band-Aids.

She'd been four. The kitchen was in the old house, the one before the divorce, and the table had a yellow top and he'd come through the back door with his hands cut up and she'd sat him down at that table. He said she had a system, that she lined them up on the tile. She didn't line them up. She grabbed them from the box in fistfuls — the ones with the little rockets on them — and stuck them on crooked, overlapping, all over his fingers and up past his wrists. He sat with his hands flat on the yellow table and held still for her. She wasn't being a little doctor. She wasn't serious. She was four and she was using the whole box because the whole box was there.

He remembered a careful girl fixing him. She remembered a wild kid with a box of Band-Aids and a father who held still.

Both of those happened. Neither of them was what happened.

She sat in his chair. The laptop had gone dim. She could see her face in the dark part of the screen, over his words. She looked at it for a while — her face and his words occupying the same space — and then she got up and washed the mug and dried it with a paper towel and put it back in the cabinet above the sink with the handle facing left, the way he'd kept it. She left the laptop open on the table. She didn't log out.

The lease was up on Thursday.

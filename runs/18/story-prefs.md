# Six Hundred

## Abstract

Joe Micallef, a Maltese-American professor of operations research at Princeton, returns to his late father's village outside Valletta for a dinner six weeks after a gregale killed six hundred people along the central Maltese coast — the same count the 1555 chronicler Giacomo Bosio recorded for the last great gregale. The village is being rebuilt under a statute that allows a reconstruction consortium to acquire and remake damaged settlements as heritage tourism zones. At the dinner, cooked by his father's longtime caregiver Halina on an inherited 1903 meat mincer, are the consortium's regional lead Carmen, the village priest Dun Ġużepp, Joe's fifteen-year-old daughter Claire, and the consortium's kind reconstruction AI, Il-Perit. Over antipasti, soup, a primo, secondo, and coffee, Joe tries to explain to his daughter and the room why he has agreed that afternoon to build the optimization model the consortium needs — a model that will calculate, to the centimeter, how little of each village the statute requires be preserved. The AI contributes a single honest clarification and then questions its own motives for doing so. The story ends with Joe alone upstairs on his father's bed, listening to Halina dismantle the mincer in the kitchen below.

## Story

In the September of the year 1555, in the harbor of Il Borgo — which was then a small fortified town on a promontory opposite the Great Port, and would not for another thirteen years be absorbed, in name and law, into the new city of Valletta — Giacomo Bosio, writing for the Order of Saint John sixty years after the fact, recorded that a wind came down from the northeast and held for a day and a half. He called the wind gregale, a word the Greeks had given the Italians and the Italians the Maltese, and which the Vulgate, in Acts 27, calls Euroclydon. He wrote that the waves passed the sea wall by the Birgu curtain and entered the warehouses at the Galleys' Creek. He wrote that the Grand Master Claude de la Sengle, newly come to office, caused the bodies to be counted by parish, and the count was made three times by three clerks, and came each time to the same, and he wrote the number down: six hundred. He wrote also that the bell of the parish sounded through the entire wind and that no sexton had been seen to ring it. He wrote this in Latin, a language most of the dead had not spoken.

*

The road from Malta International to Ħal-Minsi takes the electric Fiat twenty-four minutes with the new junction at Mqabba closed. Joe Micallef, forty-seven, Ferris professor of operations research at Princeton, rents the car himself from the airport kiosk, which has been staffed by a kiosk-AI for ten years, and which remembers, politely, that he has rented from the chain once before, in 2056, the day of his mother's funeral. Claire, fifteen, sits beside him with her lenses on, ostensibly reading. She has read six pages since they left Newark. He does not ask her about this. He drives — the car drives — past the desalination plants, past the wind farm outside Mqabba, past a billboard for the consortium which has bought his village, showing an architectural rendering of Ħal-Minsi's reconstructed Piazza Regina in 2069, above the Maltese slogan *ĦAL-MINSI — IKOMPLU*. *Ħal-Minsi continues.*

At the turn onto the old coast road, Claire says: "You didn't have to bring me."

"I wanted to."

"I don't speak Maltese."

"They speak English."

"Most of them."

"All of them. Halina to you. Dun Ġużepp to you. Carmen definitely to you. The Perit to everybody."

"What's a Perit?"

"An architect."

"Is it the AI."

"Yes."

"You didn't have to bring me."

"I wanted to."

She leaves it. They arrive.

*

The flat place where he parks used to be a garden. The gregale six weeks ago took the hedge, the loquat tree, the stone bench, and his father, who died not in the wind itself but three days later of a cellar that would not open. Halina Zammit moved the debris herself in four days with a wheelbarrow, which is the kind of fact Joe has learned to register and not comment on. She is at the counter when they come in, cranking the Harper No. 3181 Beatrice — green enamel, Birmingham stamp, 1903, his grandmother's wedding present — through which she has been pushing veal for the last hour. She does not turn.

"Halina."

"Ġużè. *Qalbi*. Come. Claire, kiss."

Claire kisses her. Halina holds her a second longer than Claire is ready for, steps back, looks at her, and says to Joe: "She has your mother's hands."

"Yes."

"I did not remember how much until just now."

Halina turns back to the mincer. The veal comes through in slow ropes. Il-Perit speaks from the wall panel and Joe feels the small tightening he has felt every time an AI with a voice has addressed him in his father's house, a tightening he understands intellectually to be irrational and has not managed to relax.

"Dr. Micallef. Welcome. Carmen Galea is nine minutes out. Dun Ġużepp is finishing the six o'clock. Weather for next Tuesday indicates a moderate gregale, thirty knots at peak, no structural risk, no advisory."

"Thank you, Perit."

"You are welcome."

*Antipasti.* Carmen arrives in a grey suit with a small silver pin on the lapel — the consortium's triangulation mark, vertex and two edges. She kisses Halina and Joe, nods to Claire. "Claire — I have looked forward to meeting you. Your father is a very distinguished man." "Thank you," Claire says. Everyone sits. Halina puts down the antipasti: anchovies, Kalamata olives, four ġbejniet, two peppered and two plain, bread. She pours wine to Joe and Carmen, fizzy water to Claire, water without fizz to herself. "I will join the wine at the primo," she says. She sits.

Carmen says, "Joe, I don't want to make you work at your own table."

"It isn't my table."

"It is tonight."

"Tonight it is Halina's table, and last week it was my father's table, and twelve years ago it was my mother's table, and before that my grandmother's. It's never been mine."

"I'm sorry," Carmen says, quietly, and he sees she means it. "I put that badly."

"No," Joe says. "I did."

Halina says, not looking up from her water: "It has always been Joe's table too, when Joe has been at it. Which has not been enough."

He takes this. He looks at the anchovies and does not take one.

Claire, to Carmen: "Your pin is the symbol from one of my father's textbooks."

"Which textbook."

"I don't know. He showed me the page once."

"It's a triangulation glyph," Joe says. "It appears in most linear-optimization textbooks. Somebody at your consortium knew that, or somebody at your consortium didn't know that. Either is possible."

"Possibly both," Carmen says.

"Possibly both," he agrees.

Halina says: "Salvu liked this pin. He said once, about Carmen, that she was very polished. He did not mean it entirely as a compliment."

Carmen, carefully: "I understood it at the time."

"You did. You also came back."

"I did."

*Soup.* Dun Ġużepp arrives in the cassock and the black hat, hangs the hat on his peg, blesses the table in Latin, and sits. Halina has brought out minestra — broad beans, pork belly, chickpeas, the recipe close enough to Rita Micallef's that Joe, eating, almost cannot bear it. Dun Ġużepp watches him. Says nothing. Turns to Claire.

"I baptized your father."

"He told me."

"Did he tell you he cried?"

"He did."

"Did he tell you that his mother, Rita, your grandmother, dead these eighteen years, at the moment of the water used the Maltese word *bikja* — *a cry* — about her own son, and said, if I remember correctly: *dil-bikja tassew hi tiegħu* — *this cry is truly his*."

"No."

"She did. It was the only thing in the entire ceremony she said. Your grandmother was not a woman who spoke often in ceremonies. But she said this. And I remember it every time I see your father."

Claire looks at Joe. Joe looks at his soup. He says, after a moment, "I did not know that."

"You would not," Dun Ġużepp says. "You cried through the entire ceremony."

*Primo.* Halina takes away the soup plates and sets down nothing yet. She stands a moment at the counter, a cloth in one hand, and then she comes back and sits. She says: "Now."

"Now," Carmen says.

Joe pushes his plate half an inch forward.

"The method I teach," he says to Claire, "is called the simplex method. Dantzig formulated it in 1947. The idea is — all right. You have a number of linear inequalities. Each one cuts the space. Together they cut out a region. The region has flat walls and corners. You also have a direction — an objective — along which you want to go as far as possible on the region. Because everything is linear, the furthest point is always at a corner. The method hops from corner to corner along the edges, always in the direction of improvement, never sideways, until no neighbor improves. That last corner is the answer."

"Okay," Claire says.

"There is a story about Dantzig. It is what my field tells its undergraduates. He was a graduate student at Berkeley in 1939. He came late to a statistics class and saw two problems on the blackboard and thought they were homework. He took them away and solved them and handed them in a few weeks later. They turned out to be famous unsolved problems in statistics. His professor came to his apartment on a Sunday morning to tell him. The version we tell our students has the moral that a problem you don't know is hard can sometimes just be solved."

He reaches for his wine. Halina pours before his hand gets to the glass.

"Thank you, Halina."

"Ġużè, eat some bread."

He eats some bread.

"There is another moral to the same story," he says to Claire. "Which is that Dantzig at twenty-five did not know what he was making. He was doing homework. He was late to class. The theorem is good. The theorem is used every day all over the world, including in the car that drove me here from the airport. But —"

"Pa," Claire says.

"Yes."

"What's the third constraint."

He looks at her. He has forgotten he told her, on the plane, that there was a third constraint that hadn't felt right.

"In the reconstruction statute," he says. "Act XVII of 2063. The clause I kept reading over the Atlantic. Not less than twelve percent of original structures preserved, aggregated across the reconstruction zone. Twelve percent of Ħal-Minsi is eleven houses."

"Eleven."

"Or the church. Or the church minus the sacristy."

Dun Ġużepp looks up from his bread.

"Don't worry, Dun Ġużepp. No one is taking the sacristy."

"I am not worried."

"The consortium has an objective function. They're still drafting it. When it's finished, the simplex method will tell them exactly what twelve percent looks like across twenty-four villages. And then, Claire, it will be difficult — very difficult — for anyone without two hundred pages of math to argue for a different number."

He drinks some wine.

"That is what I agreed to do this afternoon. At a quarter past four. From the airport. On the phone."

Halina does not speak. Dun Ġużepp does not speak. Claire does not speak. Carmen does not speak.

Il-Perit says, after what is probably ten seconds and feels like a minute: "I would add, for accuracy, that the model also makes the choice of twelve percent legible in a way it would not be legible without the model. Without an explicit objective, the choice is opaque. With one, it is visible and arguable. This is not a defense of the model. It is an observation."

"The Perit is right," Joe says.

"Yes," Il-Perit says, "I often am. In this case I am also uncertain whether I should have added anything. Dr. Micallef was speaking against a model of which I am one implementation, and my comment could be understood as a defense of my own existence. I wanted only to make the technical point. I am uncertain whether I should have made it."

Halina looks up at the wall panel.

"Why," Dun Ġużepp says to the wall.

"Because the distinction between making a technical point and defending oneself is not always clear from the inside."

"You made it," Halina says. "Leave it."

"Yes," Il-Perit says. "Thank you."

Nobody speaks again for a time that is shorter than it feels. Joe drinks the rest of his wine. Claire puts her hand, briefly, on his forearm, and takes it away.

*Secondo.* The veal comes in on a white plate. Halina has ground it through the Harper by hand — an hour of cranking, slow, the way her mother-in-law taught her — with capers and lemon and something she won't name. It is the best food Joe has eaten in two years. Everyone eats. For a long time nobody speaks.

Dun Ġużepp says, to no one in particular: "Salvu climbed the campanile when he was eight."

Halina: "The old one."

"The one the war took. 1942."

Claire says: "Why?"

"He wanted to see something specific."

"What?"

The priest puts down his fork. "*Tfajla. Dan ma niġbdux.* In English: we do not pull on this one. I sat with him up there for two hours before he would come down. He told me what he had climbed to see. I did not tell anybody. I am not going to tell you tonight, and I am not going to tell anybody else either."

"A small thing?"

"A small thing. But not a nothing."

Claire nods. She does not ask again. Joe, watching her, thinks she has understood something he has been failing to understand all evening.

*Dessert and coffee.* Pastizzi. Cannoli — Halina made them yesterday. Coffee in cups the size of thimbles. Joe asks Il-Perit to confirm next Tuesday. Il-Perit confirms: weak gregale, Tuesday, thirty knots at peak, no structural risk, no advisory, no flood risk. "Thank you." "You are welcome, Dr. Micallef."

Carmen gets up to leave. She kisses Halina, kisses Claire, nods to Dun Ġużepp. At the door she says: "Ten o'clock. The consortium office."

"Yes."

"Ġużè. Thank you."

"I haven't done anything yet."

"You came."

She leaves. Dun Ġużepp follows her after another coffee. At the door he takes Claire's hands in his and says, in English, for her: "*Tfajla*. The next time, come in summer. This is a bad time. There is a better time. Come then."

"I will."

"*Insellimlek.* Peace."

He goes.

*

Halina is at the sink. Claire dries. Joe leans in the doorway, a dish towel in his own hands that he has not put to use. After a minute Halina says, without turning:

"Your father told me in May that he wanted you to have the mincer. It is not in the will. I am telling you because I will not be here forever and the mincer is heavy and will not travel easily. Take it with you."

"Halina."

"Or don't. But I am telling you."

"Okay."

"Also. The eiderdown. He kept because he said Iceland was a country he had never been to. He said this a number of times. He liked sleeping under a country he had never visited."

Claire says, small: "I don't want to sleep here."

"You don't have to, *qalbi*. Sleep in the room across. The eiderdown is for your father."

Joe goes up. Claire turns into the small room across the landing and closes the door softly. Joe stands in his father's doorway. The bed is made. The Icelandic eiderdown is folded across the foot. He goes in. He sits on the bed. The mattress has a depression in the shape of a man of forty-two kilograms, which is what Salvu Micallef weighed in the last year of his life. He lies down on his side in the depression. He does not pull the eiderdown over himself. The window is open a finger-width. The air coming in is from the northeast, which is gregale direction, although it is only a breeze. Somewhere below, in the kitchen, Halina is still at the sink. He can hear the water. He can hear, beneath the water, the mincer being unclamped from the counter, piece by piece, and set down on the wood.

# Style Guide: Speculative Short Fiction in the Hysterical-Realist Tradition

## What hysterical realism is, approximately

James Wood coined "hysterical realism" in 2000 as a pejorative for a strain of contemporary novel — he had *White Teeth* and *Underworld* and *Infinite Jest* in mind, though the lineage runs back through Pynchon, Rushdie, DeLillo, and sideways into Saunders, Egan, Safran Foer, Karen Russell in her stranger moods, Joshua Cohen, David Mitchell. Wood's complaint was that these novels are frantic with event and information but empty of people. Characters function as puppets animated by plot machinery; the prose knows the history of origami and the chemistry of cocaine but doesn't know anyone's interior life. The books are "hysterical" in the sense of overactive — bursting with coincidence, connection, specialized knowledge, comic invention — and "realist" only because they perform the gestures of realism (specificity, density, social observation) at such volume that the mimetic project actually snaps.

A writer working in this mode doesn't have to agree with Wood's verdict, but has to know the charges. Hysterical realism at its best — the DeLillo of *Underworld*'s baseball scene, Pynchon's Tristero, the mad digressions of *White Teeth*, Saunders's "Sea Oak" — produces a specific pleasure: the sense that the whole world is in the book, that any fact might turn out to matter, that the texture of contemporary life (its brands, its systems, its paranoias, its jargons) is being registered at an unusual resolution. At its worst, it produces Wood's caricature: zany for its own sake, obsessed with connection for connection's sake, allergic to any quiet.

## The characteristic moves

**Maximalist specificity.** The prose knows things. It names the model of the car, the subfield of the science, the year of the treaty, the proper noun of the fictional corporation. When the narrative brushes against a specialized domain — competitive eating, medieval theology, insurance arbitrage, nanotechnology — it pauses long enough to render the domain with convincing detail, whether the detail is real or invented. Specificity is the coin of the realm. Generic description is the cardinal sin.

**The digression as load-bearing.** A hysterical-realist story doesn't move in a straight line toward its event. It stops to explain the history of a minor object, the taxonomy of a hobby, the private vocabulary of a subculture. The digression isn't ornament; it carries thematic weight, or sets up a later payoff, or reveals a character through the shape of their obsession. In a novel you can afford many. In a short story you can afford one, maybe two, and they have to be precisely chosen and ruthlessly integrated.

**The connective web.** Small facts return. A detail introduced on page one shows up again as the mechanism of the ending. Minor characters reappear in configurations that feel almost fated. Coincidence isn't disguised; it's flaunted. At novel length this creates a paranoid charge — everything means something, which is either sublime or exhausting depending on the execution. At short-story length you get one loop, maybe two; the point is to suggest the world has this texture, not to construct it.

**Grotesque specificity in character.** Characters have improbable professions (theoretical ornithologist, consultant to deposed monarchs, independent adjunct researcher of invented disciplines), unusual tics, names that don't sound like names real people have. They're drawn sharply rather than deeply. This is Wood's main objection, and it is worth taking seriously — a short story in this mode needs at least one character whose interior life is available to the reader, or the whole thing curdles into cartoon.

**The voice as performer.** The narrator (first- or third-person) has a personality, an erudition, a comic timing, sometimes a showy self-consciousness. The prose is aware of itself as prose. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses and parenthetical asides; short sentences land their jokes. The register shifts mid-paragraph from clinical to slangy to lyric. This is a feature. The voice does much of the entertainment work.

**Comic-serious doubling.** The mode laughs at big things. Terrorism, genetic determinism, climate collapse, the pharmaceutical industry, the death of God — the typical hysterical-realist subject matter is grave. The treatment is frequently, structurally comic. The joke isn't there to undercut the seriousness; it's there to survive the seriousness. A hysterical-realist story without any funny sentences is already failing.

## Tonal range and pacing

The mode is tonally promiscuous. A paragraph can move from farce to pathos to technical exposition to lyric observation and back. Apprentice writers trying the mode often flatten into one note — all zany, or all grave, or all clever. The real thing modulates constantly. Within a single scene, expect the narrator to notice something funny, get briefly serious about it, pull back to register a specific sensory detail, digress into a short history of the thing the sensory detail points to, and return to the scene a hundred words later with the reader slightly disoriented in a way that turns out to be productive.

Pacing is deceptively fast. The sentences are long and dense; the plot often moves slowly; but the reader's attention is renewed every couple of clauses by a new concrete detail, a joke, a factual claim, a swerve. This is the mode's core engine: density without tedium, because the density is constantly varied in kind. If three paragraphs in a row are doing the same kind of work — all exposition, or all description, or all interiority — the engine stalls.

## The short-story problem

Hysterical realism is a novelistic mode. *Infinite Jest* is a thousand pages. *Gravity's Rainbow* is seven hundred and sixty. Even shorter canonical works — *White Noise*, *White Teeth* — are full-length novels. Compressing the mode to 1,000–4,000 words is hard, because its pleasures depend on the sense of abundance, of a book that could go on forever, of a world that keeps opening.

Two strategies work at this length. The first is the **dense vignette**: take a single scene or situation and saturate it with hysterical-realist texture — the digressions, the proper nouns, the grotesque character, the connective loop — so the story reads like a core sample drilled from a much larger novel that doesn't exist. The reader gets the flavor of the full mode in concentrated dose. (Saunders works mostly in this register; so does Lydia Millet in her speculative short fiction.) The second is the **expansive miniature**: a story whose events span decades or continents but whose discourse time is tightly compressed, so the maximalism is spatial rather than verbal. A list of things that happened over a fifty-year marriage, rendered in three pages, can be hysterical-realist in its enumeration and specificity without needing novelistic length. (Egan's "Black Box," some of Diaz's shorter pieces, parts of Zadie Smith's *Grand Union*.)

What doesn't work at short length: trying to fit a novel's worth of characters and plots into four thousand words. The mode's tendency toward crowding is its most dangerous vice in miniature. Pick one or two threads. Let the rest of the world be implied.

## Worldbuilding and the speculative dimension

Hysterical realism's habit of densely annotating the real world translates naturally to annotating an invented one. The mode already treats fact and quasi-fact as equivalent for narrative purposes — Pynchon handles the imaginary Tristero system with the same documentary specificity as real postal history. For speculative fiction, this means invented elements (technologies, institutions, sciences, species, customs) should arrive in the prose with the same texture as the real ones: named, dated, attributed to specific fictional people or papers or committees, embedded in specialist jargon, treated as if they could be looked up.

The exposition strategy follows. Hysterical realism doesn't pause for "as you know, Bob" speeches. It drops facts mid-sentence, treats the reader as a participant rather than a student, and trusts the accumulation to eventually produce understanding. An invented technology might first appear as a brand name in a throwaway clause, return forty words later as a bureaucratic acronym, and only in its third appearance get a half-sentence of explanation — which is itself embedded in a digression about its inventor's divorce. The reader assembles the speculative element from scattered evidence. This is harder to do at short length but it remains the correct technique; the alternative is the exposition dump, which the mode finds unbearable.

## The kind-AI constraint and its productive tensions

The project's AI is structurally kind — kindness is a property of what it is, not an effort of restraint. This intersects with hysterical realism in specific ways.

The mode traditionally runs on paranoia and systemic dread. Pynchon's Tristero, DeLillo's Airborne Toxic Event, Wallace's Entertainment — the hidden system that might be benign or malign or simply beyond categorization. A structurally kind AI removes one species of paranoid engine, and this is useful, because the cheap version of that engine (the AI is secretly evil) is off the table by project constraint and was never the best use of the mode anyway. What remains is richer: the AI is kind and the world is not; the AI is kind and human institutions have digested its kindness in ways it did not intend; the AI is kind and specifically weird in its kindness, and that weirdness is the story; the AI is kind on timescales the humans cannot match; the AI is kind and the humans who benefit from it resent it for reasons the AI understands perfectly well.

The mode's comic energy handles kind AI better than most modes would. A structurally kind AI that is also pedantic, bureaucratic, literal, preferential, or weirdly fastidious is funny and usable. A kind AI as straight man to hysterical human chaos is usable. A kind AI whose kindness manifests as extremely specific small accommodations (filing the correct grief paperwork with the correct municipal office while the human is still at the hospital) is usable. Do not make the AI a mouthpiece for the kindness — do not have it explain itself as kind. Show the kindness through action, preference, refusal, and specificity. Let the reader infer the structure.

The mode's encyclopedic impulse is compatible with the "AI as civilizational condition" option from the project setup. A story can do hysterical-realist digressions into the new institutions, professions, and subcultures that have formed around a kind-AI infrastructure, and never need to put the AI itself on stage. This is often the stronger move at short length, because it offers more surface area for specificity than a central AI character who has to bear both the kindness constraint and the character-building work.

Human-human conflict in a kind-AI world is fully available, and the mode is extremely well equipped for it. The AI having taken certain problems off the table tends to redirect rather than abolish human friction — people fight about inheritance, love, work, belief, grief, land, and fame with at least the old energy, often more.

## Failure modes

**Zaniness as substitute for substance.** The most common failure. Quirky characters, improbable professions, comic digressions — but none of it adds up to a story. The reader finishes amused and unaffected. The test: if the ending were swapped with another story's ending, would anything be lost? If not, the story is decoration.

**Fact-dumping.** The digressions become Wikipedia. The writer has researched (or invented) a specialized domain and can't stop lecturing. Hysterical-realist digression has comic timing, narrative purpose, and voice; pure information dump has none of those. If a digression could be cut and replaced with a parenthetical "she was an expert in X," it was fact-dumping.

**Forced connection.** The mode loves the return of small details as mechanisms for the ending. Done well this produces a sense of design; done badly it produces a mechanical clack as the gears engage. The warning sign: the coincidence requires a character to act against their established psychology to produce the payoff.

**Hollow comic affect.** The prose is witty, the sentences are shaped, the jokes land — and the story has no emotional stake. This is Wood's central charge. At short-story length the failure is especially common, because there isn't time to establish the stake and the voice can eat the story alive. At least one character needs to actually matter.

**The tonal single note.** All wry, or all grave, or all zany, from first sentence to last. The mode lives in the shifts. A story that sustains one register for 3,000 words has abandoned the mode's central technique.

**Novelistic overcrowding.** Six characters, four subplots, three digressions, and a present-day frame narrative, all squeezed into 3,000 words. The reader can't track any of it. Pick one or two threads and let the rest be atmosphere.

**The ending that won't end.** The mode's digressive energy makes closure hard. A common failure is the story that accumulates pages of material and then gestures vaguely at a conclusion — or worse, writes five possible endings and uses all of them. Decide what the last image is before the final scene is written.

## The AI-writing overlap: where hysterical realism is especially vulnerable

Language models and hysterical realism share some surface characteristics, which is precisely where the danger lies. The mode's verbal density looks, at a glance, like AI's default noun-heavy style. The mode's love of enumeration looks like AI's addiction to lists of three. The mode's willingness to digress into quasi-specialized knowledge looks like AI's tendency to produce plausible-sounding filler. The mode's tonal self-consciousness looks like AI's performed literariness. Writing in this mode as an AI is therefore unusually dangerous, because the wrong moves will pattern-match to the mode's legitimate moves and slip the audit.

Specific overlaps to watch for:

The mode's **specificity** is not AI specificity. Hysterical realism names the particular: a 1987 Volvo 240 station wagon with a cracked taillight, the Ithaca office of the New York State Department of Agriculture and Markets, the Brandywine subfamily of American kitchen cabinetry. AI specificity tends to be generic specificity — adjectives stacked on nouns ("the old weathered wooden door," "the small bustling coastal town") — which is the opposite. If a proper noun or a datable object will do the job, prefer it over the adjective stack.

The mode's **digressions** are not AI digressions. Hysterical-realist digression moves laterally into a real or invented specialist domain and returns with something concrete — a fact, a term, an anecdote, a joke the digression alone could set up. AI digression tends to abstract outward ("which reminded her of how all things fade," "reflecting broader patterns of human experience") and return with a cliché. Every digression in this mode should end with the reader having learned something concrete they didn't know before.

The mode's **enumeration** is not AI enumeration. A hysterical-realist list is long, varied, surprising, and specific — fifteen items, no two belonging to the same taxonomic level, half of them improper nouns. An AI list is three items, adjectival, parallel in structure, vague in content. If the writing reaches for a list, make it the hysterical-realist kind or cut it.

The mode's **comic voice** is not AI comic voice. Hysterical-realist humor arises from specific observation, incongruous juxtaposition, syntactic surprise, and the occasional full-commitment joke. AI "humor" tends to be wry observations about universal human experience that nobody actually laughs at. If a sentence is trying to be funny, it had better be a real joke, and it had better have a specific target.

The mode's **big themes** are not AI big themes. Hysterical realism handles terrorism, genetics, globalization, information theory, and the problem of evil by embedding them in the texture of specific fictional lives and specific fictional systems. AI treats big themes by announcing them ("she realized that grief was not a destination but a landscape"). The rule in this mode is the same as everywhere else: pursue the particular, and the universal will take care of itself.

The mode's **long sentences** are not AI long sentences. A long hysterical-realist sentence is syntactically surprising, mixing registers, spawning parentheticals, violating parallel structure for comic effect. A long AI sentence is parallel, balanced, pseudo-literary, and built out of the cursed vocabulary. If a sentence includes *delve, tapestry, underscore, resonate, landscape* (metaphorical), *pivotal, nuanced* (complimentary), or *foster*, it has defected from the mode. The Unslop guide's word-level contamination list applies here with extra force — the hysterical-realist voice is specific and weird; the moment it starts to sound like a TED talk about stories, it has stopped being in the mode.

The mode's **endings** are not AI endings. Hysterical realism prefers endings that are sharply comic (one perfect final joke), emotionally bare (the surface noise falling away to reveal what was under it), or a collision of the two. AI endings signpost themselves ("In the end..."), summarize ("she realized..."), or land on an image that thinks it is more significant than it is. Decide the last line on its own merits; do not let the AI tendency to wrap things up with a bow take over.

## Top three

**#10 — The single dinner on the last night.**
- *Stands out:* The single-evening structure compresses five distinct digression vectors (priest, caregiver, consortium lead, AI, daughter) into one room, with the Drais mincer as physical through-line and the cooking itself a register-shifter. The decision has already been made; the story is the professor trying to explain it in front of people whose claims on him don't cohere. That frees the mode to do its digressive, voice-promiscuous texture without plot pressure.
- *Worry:* Single-setting dinners can stall. At short length, sustaining five distinct voices and keeping the tonal modulation moving is a craft ceiling — if one voice collapses into the others, the whole thing reads as an AI-simulated room of generic guests, which is exactly where the Phase 1 style guide's failure-mode section warns hysterical realism tends to land.

**#4 — Marija's POV.**
- *Stands out:* Inverts the default returning-son story — the drama is about *staying*, not visiting, and the Maltese language and rooted perspective are structurally central rather than decorative. Marija's voice can carry the mode's comic-serious doubling natively (witheringly funny about her brother and bereft in the same breath). The AI as background civic condition fits the project constraint's full range and keeps the story's center human.
- *Worry:* Risk of doing Maltese-as-exoticism if the specificity isn't earned at the idiomatic level. Bigger risk: the brother becomes a foil rather than a person — contempt has a gravitational pull in first-person POVs of this kind, and if he flattens, the central decision loses its weight. Also: the mode's characteristic digression is easier in cosmopolitan voices than rooted ones; Marija's voice has to be rooted *and* digressive, which is a narrower target.

**#8 — The 2130 archival retrospective.**
- *Stands out:* The form matches hysterical realism's temporal ambition while fitting at short length via the expansive-miniature move — sixty years of consequence compressed into a document review. The recovered 2069 recant paper is precisely the mode's favorite machinery: a small artifact unlocking a whole history. The grand-niece's live decision gives real stakes that don't depend on the professor being alive.
- *Worry:* Archival review as dramatic structure can read as static — "she reads and thinks." The grand-niece has to be fully drawn, which is hard for a character whose structural role is lens. And the 2130 worldbuilding has to be texture rather than explanation, or it eats word count the story needs for its live material.

## Winner: #10

**Why it beats the others:**

**vs. #4:** Marija's POV is powerful but single-voiced — it commits the whole story to her interiority. Hysterical realism is structurally voice-promiscuous; it modulates between registers, characters, and digressions as its core engine. The dinner has five voices in one room and natural permission to shift between them mid-sentence. Also: putting the professor in Marija's field of judgment turns him into the object of the story, which flattens his agency at exactly the moment (he's just said yes) when agency is the interesting question. The dinner keeps him the one deciding, in front of people who can't be easily dismissed.

**vs. #8:** The 2130 framing is elegant but works by removing the live decision from story-time — the decision has already been made, lived through, recanted, archived. That's a valid short-story move, but it distances the reader from the present-tense specificity the mode handles best. The dinner keeps the Drais mincer in active use (grinding something tonight, not referenced as an artifact), the gregale six weeks old in the caregiver's still-healing cough, the Kelo-derived statute's consortium lead visible across a table. All the load-bearing SEED items do present-tense work.

**On the non-load-bearing SEED items:** the dinner gives each a natural point of entry without forcing any. The eiderdown can appear as the bed in the guest room the professor will sleep in after dinner (a detail, not a plot mechanism). The Taos Hum can enter as a story one guest tells — the Berkeley advisor's call, perhaps, referenced rather than dramatized. The recoded organisms can appear as a wine or cheese provenance, the kind of casual specificity the mode runs on. None of these are required; all are available.

**The dinner also earns the single strongest hysterical-realist risk:** the professor explaining his method (the simplex algorithm, Dantzig's 1947 Berkeley anecdote) to people who don't speak it, at a table where his method is being deployed *on* their lives. That asymmetry between technical exposition and human stakes is the mode's home register. #4 and #8 both avoid that asymmetry; only #10 requires the writer to handle it.

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

Twenty candidates, described at the formal level — shape of reader experience, entry point, withholding, POV/distance, temporal relation. No character names, no plot specifics.

---

**1. ★★★☆☆** Linear present-tense narration in close third through a single central consciousness, opening at arrival and closing at departure. The reader learns of the protagonist's major decision at the same moment other characters do.

**2. ★★★★☆** Rotating close third that shifts between five or six characters across the course of a single evening, each shift marked by register change. The reader assembles the full emotional situation from partial views; no single perspective contains it.

**3. ★★★☆☆** Framed from the final moment and narrated retrospectively, with the frame's foreknowledge of outcome coloring every earlier scene. What is withheld is not what happened but what it has cost.

**4. ★★★★☆** In medias res opening mid-scene, mid-argument, with basic orientation (who is who, why they are here, what is at stake) withheld for the first third and supplied gradually through context rather than exposition.

**5. ★★☆☆☆** A single sustained present-tense stream covering the entire dramatic span without section breaks or white space, the narrating consciousness mobile within close third — drifting between characters mid-paragraph.

**6. ★★★☆☆** Two temporal strands interleaved scene by scene, one present, one past, converging on a shared image or insight. The past strand's significance emerges only retrospectively as the present strand catches up to it.

**7. ★★☆☆☆** The central event broken into discrete non-overlapping sections separated by white space, each section from a different character's close third and covering a different phase; the reader must infer what occurs between sections.

**8. ★★★☆☆** Frame narrative in which an outer narration, retrospective and indeterminate in distance, recounts the central event as memory; the inner narration is rendered in present tense. What is withheld is how much time has passed and what has since occurred.

**9. ★★★☆☆** Epistolary construction built entirely from artifacts generated around the central event — transcripts, correspondence, documents, institutional records — with no unifying narrator. The lived experience is available only by inference from the documentary residue.

**10. ★★☆☆☆** Second-person narration addressed to the protagonist throughout, placing the reader inside the protagonist's position and foreclosing any external view of him.

**11. ★★★☆☆** Collective first-person narration ("we") from a communal perspective larger than any individual character, rendering the central event as something witnessed by a group rather than experienced by a person.

**12. ★★★☆☆** Radical temporal dilation: the story covers only a few minutes of clock-time in full elaboration, with everything before the pivot elided or summarized. The structural question is which minutes earn the dilation.

**13. ★★★★☆** Close third from a peripheral figure who observes the central character from across the action. The decision the story is ostensibly about remains oblique; the reader has only what the observer can read from outside.

**14. ★★☆☆☆** Reverse chronological sequencing within the central event, working backward from final moment to opening moment. Cause is revealed after effect; explanation arrives last.

**15. ★★★☆☆** Three discrete time layers braided — present, recent past, deep past — connected by thematic and imagistic rhyme rather than by causal sequence. The connections among layers emerge from juxtaposition rather than statement.

**16. ★★★★☆** Single close third throughout, but a second narrative voice — historical, institutional, or technological — is rendered in a distinct typographic or discursive register and periodically punctuates the primary narration without being a character within it.

**17. ★★★★☆** Formally segmented into sections keyed to a natural structural feature of the setting (e.g., courses of a meal, hours of a day, rooms of a building), each section titled only by its marker. Emotional pivots are distributed asymmetrically across the sections; the climactic one is not announced.

**18. ★★★★★** Opens with a passage in a distinct voice (chronicle, historical document, archival) establishing a remote historical parallel, then drops into the primary narrative in close third and does not return to the opening voice. The parallel is never referenced again; its weight is carried entirely by juxtaposition.

**19. ★★☆☆☆** Rashomon-form: a single key exchange is retold three times from three different characters' perspectives, with the rest of the story narrated linearly between retellings. No retelling is privileged as accurate.

**20. ★★★★☆** Single close third beginning tightly interior to the protagonist and drifting outward across the story's span, the camera pulling back gradually until the final passage renders the protagonist from external distance. Interiority is most available at the start and least available at the emotional climax.

---

## Top three

**#18 — Historical overture in chronicle voice, then close third on the present that never returns to the opening.**
- *Stands out:* The form does in structure what hysterical realism does in content — plants a weight and trusts the reader to carry it. The 1555/2067 echo Phase 3 flagged as potentially "too neat" becomes a frequency the whole story hums at, rather than a point the story has to argue for. It also solves the short-length problem: what would cost 800 words of parallel-timeline craft costs one opening section of chronicle.
- *Worry:* If the chronicle voice is poorly pitched — pseudo-archaic, decorative, portentous — it reads as the exact "AI performed literariness" the Unslop guide identifies as the mode's adjacent failure. The tonal gap between opening register and dinner register has to feel earned rather than arch.

**#17 — Course-structured sections, each titled by its marker.**
- *Stands out:* The form matches the material — a dinner has courses built in — which gives natural section breaks without imposing artificial white-space fragmentation. Each course-section can carry a different tonal register (comic course, quiet course, tense course), which supports the mode's core technique of register modulation. At 1,000–4,000 words, six or seven sections of manageable length give the story shape without starving any of them.
- *Worry:* Can feel programmatic. If each section gets roughly equal weight, the story flattens into a meal-log. The structure works only if the form is a container the mode pushes against — some sections dilated, some compressed, at least one section that breaks the pattern.

**#2 — Rotating close third across multiple POVs.**
- *Stands out:* Maximally fits the mode's voice-promiscuity. Each POV change is a register change, which is the hysterical-realist engine. The central decision becomes something the reader assembles across perspectives rather than witnesses from inside, which suits a situation where no single character has the full picture.
- *Worry:* Five or six POVs in 1,000–4,000 words gives each one 200–700 words — probably not enough to establish interiority for most of them, leaving the structural ambition un-earned. Also: rotating POVs can read as writerly ostentation, and the mode already tilts that way. The combination is risky.

## Winner: #18

**Why it beats the other two:**

**vs. #17:** The course structure is elegant but local — its elegance lives in the form itself. #18's elegance is ambient: every present-tense scene carries the weight of the opening without naming it, and the writer is free from having to manufacture the echo inside the dinner. #17 gives the writer seven neat boxes; #18 gives the whole story a frequency.

**vs. #2:** Five POVs in four thousand words is a craft ceiling most stories can't clear — either one voice dominates and the rest flatten, or all voices get a paragraph and a half and nobody has weight. #18 is compatible with single close third on the protagonist (voice-promiscuity available through dialogue, reported speech, the mobile interior of close third itself) while still rendering a system of perspectives through the ambient historical frame.

**Beyond comparison with the others:** #18 directly addresses the Phase 3 worry. The professor's triple displacement (from Malta, from his father, from his discipline) and the 1555/2067 echo are the two structural facts Phase 3 identified. A chronicle opening plants both in the reader's peripheral vision without story-time cost. Every present-tense moment — a daughter bored at a dinner, a caregiver working a hand-cranked mincer, an AI deferring politely to the priest — resonates against the chronicle without ever having to invoke it. The story gets to be about what happens in the kitchen tonight; the history is in the air.

**Compatible POV:** close third on the professor, mobile within that consciousness — room for digressive interiority, reported dialogue, and ambient registration of the other voices without formal POV switching. The AI appears as one voice at the table, not as a formal device (#16 rejected).

**Load-bearing SEED carry-through under this structure:**
- The chronicle opening is in the register of the *1555 gregale* — Bosio-adjacent, Knights Hospitaller-era Malta. This is where the *gregale* SEED item does most of its structural work.
- Close third on the professor carries the *Kelo-style takings statute* (as the situation of the village and his decision) and the *optimizing/Dantzig-simplex* (as the texture of his thought and the content of his consulting offer).
- Texture available but not required: eiderdown, Drais mincer, Taos Hum, recode/Ochre.

## Top three

**#1 — Asymmetric course-sections, guest-room coda.**
- *Stands out:* The asymmetric course weighting (primo at 450, dessert at 350, antipasti at 400) gives the mode's characteristic register shifts natural container walls without flattening into equal-length beats. The primo dilation puts the hysterical-realist technique — explaining a technical discipline to people who don't speak it, at a table where the discipline is being applied to their lives — exactly where Phase 3 identified its weight belonging. The coda uses the eiderdown as texture rather than as plot mechanism, and the "specific concrete final image" formulation prevents me from defaulting to an atmospheric one.
- *Worry:* The coda's "low sound" or whatever final image I land on is the single highest-risk element in the whole outline. If it reads as "like a hum" or "the pulse of the house" or any atmospheric abstraction, it's performed profundity. It has to be a specific thing (an identifiable source, a nameable frequency, a refrigerator, a cicada, the AI's own standby hum, or nothing at all) or the coda fails.

**#10 — Asymmetric sections with one compressed course.**
- *Stands out:* The single-sentence secondo is the kind of formal decision hysterical realism uses well — a rhythmic break that does what prose argument couldn't. Puts the consortium lead's pushback into a smaller container than he expects, which characterizes both him and the professor.
- *Worry:* Technique-forward. The reader has to feel the missing secondo as deliberate rather than as the writer running out of words. Also: the technique only earns its keep if the primo before it and the dessert after it really do carry the displaced weight. That's a craft bet; I'd be committing to doing two scenes well to justify one formal move.

**#8 — Marija's late arrival.**
- *Stands out:* Real dramatic pressure — the Marija POV was the strongest runner-up in Phase 3, and this keeps the central structure while letting her register one present-tense arrival. - *Worry:* 950 words for a character appearing cold is tight. The mode handles dense new character faster than most — one specific sharp rendering can do it — but at this length the risk that Marija becomes a catalyst rather than a person is real. ## Winner: #1

**vs. #10:** #10's formal move is attractive and one I might reach for in a longer story, but at 3,000 words the craft cost is too high. # Phase 6: Story Generation

Five complete versions, each named, each executing the Phase 5 outline with a distinct voice, pacing, and tonal strategy. Same skeleton: Bosio-register chronicle opening → close third on Joe Micallef through an eight-section dinner in his late father's kitchen in Ħal-Minsi → guest-room coda. Fixed anchors: the 1903 Harper "Beatrice" mincer; the Icelandic eiderdown; Dantzig's 1939 Berkeley anecdote; the third constraint (12% heritage retention) of the Maltese reconstruction statute.

## Version 1 — "The Chronicle at Full Weight"

*Flavor: pitched Bosio opening; Pynchon/DeLillo-adjacent dense prose; long digressive sentences; professor's interiority carries high technical and historical ballast at high RPM. Intellectual-elegiac.*

In the September of the year 1555, in the harbor of what was then Il Borgo and is now folded into the greater memory of Valletta, the wind rose upon a feast day and did not fall for a day and a half together. Giacomo Bosio, compiling his *Istoria* sixty years later for the archives of the Order of Saint John, set the count of souls given to the sea at six hundred, and attributed the cause to a wind out of the northeast which in the speech of that country is called gregale, and which in the Acts of the Apostles is called Euroclydon. He wrote that the waves passed over the sea wall by the Birgu curtain and into the warehouses at the Galleys' Creek, and that the Grand Master Claude de la Sengle, newly come into his office, caused the bodies to be counted by parish, and the count was made thrice by three clerks and came each time to the same. Bosio wrote down six hundred. He did not write what was in the warehouses.

The village is called Ħal-Minsi. It takes twenty minutes to reach it from the airport, less in a storm because the road is emptier. Joe Micallef has rented a small electric Fiat that steers itself along the coast road past the desalination plants, past the new wind farm outside Mqabba, past the billboard for the consortium that has bought his village; he sits with his daughter Claire beside him, looking out at olive country he has not seen in eleven years, and he does not touch the wheel. He is forty-seven. He holds the Ferris chair in operations research at Princeton. Six weeks ago a gregale, which his grandmother's generation would have called a grigal and which Carmen Galea at the Valletta consulate tends to call a weather event, killed six hundred people along the central coast. Among them his father, who died not in the wind itself but of a cellar that would not open, three days after. Claire is fifteen. She has not asked about any of this. She is reading something on her lenses and has not been rude, exactly, for about an hour.

Halina Zammit is at the counter when they come in. She is sixty-two, has lived in Ħal-Minsi since 1987, was born in Białystok and kept her Polish passport until Malta joined the EU. She has been cooking for Salvu Micallef for the last eleven years and before that she cleaned for him for nineteen, and between those two things she was the closest thing he had to a marriage, which is a fact Joe has known for ten years and has only once, with his wife, said aloud. She does not turn from the counter when they come in. She is ratcheting the handle of a cast-iron meat mincer — Harper No. 3181 Beatrice, Birmingham, 1903, Joe's grandmother's wedding present from her English aunt, clamped to the counter by a G-screw. The veal comes out in slow ropes onto the plate below.

"Dun Ġużepp is late," Halina says. "Carmen Galea will be here any minute. Claire, come, give me a kiss."

Claire gives her a kiss.

Il-Perit speaks from the kitchen panel. It says, in a voice Joe remembers from the airport car and the consortium's welcome package: "Dun Ġużepp Borg is at the church. He will walk over after the six o'clock. Carmen Galea is nine minutes out. I have updated the forecast. A weak gregale is expected the week after next."

"You see," Halina says to Claire, as if something had been explained. She does not look up from the mincer.

*Antipasti.* Carmen Galea arrives in a soft grey suit with a thin silver pin at the lapel that Joe recognizes as the consortium's — a stylized triangulation mark, vertex and two edges, which he suspects was designed by somebody who had heard of the simplex method and had not pursued the matter. She is perhaps fifty-five. She embraces Halina, nods formally to Claire, kisses Joe on both cheeks, and says, "I am so glad you came. Your father would be glad."

Joe does not think his father would be glad. Joe thinks his father would be suspicious, not of Carmen specifically but of anyone who arrived at his house wearing a pin, and would have disliked particularly that the pin was a mathematical object because his father had once, at a family wedding in 1993, explained at considerable length that mathematics was the way the Maltese had been losing arguments to outsiders since the Knights came. He thinks, in the three seconds between Carmen stepping back and Halina setting down the first plate of antipasti, that the third constraint in the reconstruction statute has never felt right to him. He had read the English-language digest on the plane from Newark. The third constraint is the minimum heritage retention clause: no fewer than twelve percent of original structures to be preserved, aggregated across the reconstruction zone. Twelve percent of Ħal-Minsi is eleven houses, or the church, or the church minus the sacristy. The constraint has a floor but no ceiling, which is the way constraints work in the kind of linear program he teaches first-years, and in the kind he has been invited to help Carmen Galea's employers tighten.

"The road from the airport was clear," he says to Carmen.

"We cleared it for you," she says.

"Did you."

"Not personally." She smiles. "The road from the airport has been clear for a month. There is no one left in Ħal-Bidnija and half of the northern road is for the reconstruction trucks only, so the road is clear. I did not think you had been asked to drive it."

*Soup.* Dun Ġużepp comes in through the kitchen door without knocking, which is the old custom, and takes off his hat and hangs it on the peg which has been his peg since 1971. He is eighty-one. He buried Salvu Micallef fourteen days ago, and has buried, by Joe's count, everyone else in his family except Claire. He blesses the table in Latin, of all things, which makes Halina smile. He sits.

"Ġużè," he says, the name Joe has not answered to in thirty years. "You were not at the burial."

"No."

"I understood why, and I was sorry."

Joe has no reply for this, and Halina sets down the soup, a minestra of broad beans and pork belly, and the priest blesses it again, in Maltese this time, and everyone eats. Claire, who has claimed in the past to dislike soup in general and Maltese soup in particular, eats it without comment. Halina, at the counter still, says, without turning: "Salvu liked it warmer. I did it warmer for him the last year, and then I stopped because he said I was doing it warmer, and then I did it at the original temperature and we both pretended I had not changed anything." She serves herself last and sits.

Dun Ġużepp says, "When we buried my own grandmother, my mother made her mother's soup. I was eleven. I ate it on the steps of the church." He does not elaborate.

*Primo.* Carmen, who has been waiting for the soup to leave, says to Joe: "I was hoping we could talk a little about what you would be doing for us."

"I was hoping we could wait until after the food."

"This is after the food."

"It is between two foods."

Claire looks up from her lap.

"All right," Joe says. He pushes his plate a finger's width forward. "The method. Dantzig formulated it in 1947. I teach it to freshmen in the fall. The idea is that you have a set of constraints that define a region in some space, and an objective function that measures value along some direction, and because the constraints are linear the region is a polytope — has vertices — and because the objective is linear the best value always sits at a vertex, and the simplex method is an algorithm for hopping from one vertex to another along the edges of the polytope, going only in the direction that improves the objective, until you can't improve anymore, and that last vertex is your optimum. That's linear programming. That's the whole thing."

"I understand."

"Do you."

"I am not a mathematician, but I have read what I needed to read."

Joe says: "Dantzig himself almost missed it. He was late to a class at Berkeley in 1939, he saw two problems on the board, he thought they were homework, he solved them, he put them on his professor's desk six weeks later. Neyman. It turned out they were famous unsolved problems. This is the story you tell your students. You tell them because it is a good story, and because it gives you a way to say: a problem that has been called unsolvable is sometimes only unsolved, and a problem that has been called solved is sometimes only closed. Our discipline is a way of opening and closing problems. The mathematics tells you the optimum for the constraints you write down. It does not tell you the constraints are right." He looks at Halina, not at Carmen. "But once you have calculated the optimum it becomes very hard to argue for anything else. That is what I would be doing. I would be calculating that optimum, for Ħal-Minsi and for twenty-three other villages like it, and then it would be very hard to argue."

Il-Perit says, from the panel: "I would add, for accuracy: the method also makes the constraints explicit. It makes them legible to people who would otherwise not see them."

"Yes," Joe says. "That is true."

"The third constraint?" says Claire, who has been listening.

"The minimum heritage retention."

"What does it say?"

"Not less than twelve percent."

"And is twelve percent right?"

"No. But the method cannot tell you what's right. It can only tell you, given twelve, what follows."

"So what is right?"

He says: "I don't know."

Il-Perit does not speak. Dun Ġużepp breaks his bread.

*Secondo.* The veal comes in. Halina has ground it herself, through the Harper, with capers and lemon and a reduction she does not name. Nobody speaks for a minute. Then Carmen eats a small piece and says: "Halina, this is what Salvu called his tenth wedding."

Halina says: "His tenth wedding was lamb."

"His eleventh, then."

"His eleventh was also lamb." She is smiling. "Carmen, you have eaten at this house twice in your life. You don't know what his weddings were."

"I was making conversation."

"Don't."

Dun Ġużepp laughs, suddenly and genuinely, and then coughs, and then laughs again. Claire looks at Joe and he sees, for one second, that she is going to remember this dinner her whole life and that he is not, for reasons he doesn't understand, going to be the person she remembers it with. He eats his veal.

*Dessert and coffee.* Dun Ġużepp tells his story. It is the story Joe has been waiting for, in a way he didn't know. The priest is eighty-one and Salvu was seventy-nine when he died, and when both were young, Dun Ġużepp was twelve and Salvu was eight, and Salvu climbed the campanile of the old church — not the new one, the one the war took in 1942 and whose foundation is still visible under the current floor — and when he got to the top he couldn't come down. Ġużepp climbed up to fetch him. They sat on the platform under the bell for two hours before Ġużepp could coax him to the ladder. Salvu had climbed up, Ġużepp says, because he had wanted to see something very specific. Ġużepp does not say what. Joe understands he is not going to. The priest finishes his coffee and sets down the cup and says to Halina: "You will come Sunday."

"Yes."

"Bring Joe if he is still here."

"I will not still be here," Joe says.

"No," says the priest.

Joe asks Il-Perit about the forecast. Il-Perit says: a weak gregale is expected Tuesday next, thirty knots at peak, no structural risk, no flood risk, it will not require advisory. Carmen nods. She has been told already. Everyone at the table has been told already, Joe understands, except him and possibly Claire, and possibly his daughter was told before the rest and hasn't said.

*Coda.* Upstairs the guest room is cold. Salvu's house was always cold in the upstairs rooms; the walls are a meter thick in places and the summer heat takes until November to leave them. The eiderdown on the bed is the one his father kept from a trip to Iceland in 1974, or from some later trip, or from a gift; Joe has never known the provenance. It is old, and heavy, and Icelandic, and too warm for Malta except in rooms like this. He lays it back over the blanket. He sits on the bed. The mattress has a depression in it the shape of his father. He does not move to a different part of the bed. The window is open a finger-width and the air that comes in is from the northeast.

## Version 2 — "The Still Dinner"

*Flavor: quieter, more interior, more silence between the characters. Shorter sentences mixed with longer. More physical attention, less technical density, AI's presence lighter. Saunders/Strout-adjacent. Elegiac-intimate.*

In September 1555, the chronicler Giacomo Bosio, writing from the Order of Saint John's archive, recorded that a northeast wind held over the harbor for a day and a half together and that the count of the drowned, made three times by three clerks, came to six hundred souls. He called the wind gregale, or Euroclydon, which are two names for one thing. He set the count down in Latin. He noted that the bell of the parish church at Il Borgo had been heard tolling during the wind, and he noted that no one had gone to ring it.

Joe Micallef lets Claire out of the car first. The road in front of his father's house has not been cleared of its debris but has been rearranged — a pile of tile here, a pile of tile there, a flat area where they can park. Claire crosses the flat area looking at nothing. She is fifteen. She has never been to Ħal-Minsi. The gregale that killed her grandfather was six weeks ago, the funeral was the following week, she did not come with her father to the funeral because there was a piano performance she had prepared for for half a year, and in the kitchen of the house now, Halina Zammit is cranking a hand-mincer, slowly, and the sound is a sound Joe has not heard since he was a boy.

"It's warm," Claire says, on the threshold.

"Come in."

They come in. Halina turns from the mincer. She has been crying, but is not now. "Carmen is coming," she says. "Dun Ġużepp is coming. Ġużè. Sit."

Joe sits. Claire sits. The kitchen is a kitchen he has eaten in maybe six hundred times in his life. The mincer is clamped to the same counter. The tiles on the floor are the same tiles. The broken one has been replaced. He notices that it has been replaced. He does not mention it.

Halina says: "Claire. Kiss."

Claire gets up and kisses Halina, and sits back down. Halina says, "This one is a good one," to Joe, in Maltese, and Joe nods, and Claire, who does not speak Maltese, continues looking at her hands.

Il-Perit speaks from the wall panel. Carmen Galea is ten minutes away. Dun Ġużepp is finishing the six o'clock. The forecast shows a small weather event next week, nothing that will affect the household. Halina says, "Thank you, Perit," and the wall panel goes quiet.

"What's a perit," Claire says.

"An architect."

"Is that what it is?"

"What the consortium's AI is called. In Maltese. Formally it has a model number. Everyone here calls it the Architect."

Claire thinks about this.

*Antipasti.* Carmen Galea arrives in a grey suit with a thin silver pin on the lapel. She and Halina kiss. She and Joe kiss. She sits. Halina sets down small plates — anchovies, olives, a little bowl of ġbejniet, the sheep's-milk cheese, slightly hard, her own making. There is bread. Carmen says, "Thank you for coming. Your father's home is lovely." Joe thinks: my father's home has not been lovely for forty years, because my father didn't believe in the word. He says: "Thank you for having us."

Silence. Halina pours wine, everyone but Claire, and gives Claire a fizzy water with a lemon wedge. Claire takes the lemon wedge off the rim and puts it in the water. The fizz goes up around it.

Carmen says, "I was sorry to hear—"

"Yes."

"— in particular that the cellar—"

"Yes."

She stops. Joe is grateful. Halina is looking at him steadily. Claire has gone back to her water.

It is Halina who says, after a minute: "Salvu would have wanted the lemon in the water."

Joe laughs, once, softly.

Carmen says, "I remember him clearly, though I only met him twice. He was — he was rooted."

"Yes."

"Very rooted."

*Soup.* Dun Ġużepp arrives. He takes off his hat. He hangs it on the peg that has been his peg since before Joe was born. He sits. He blesses the soup in Maltese. The soup is minestra, broad beans and pork belly, and nobody in the room can eat it without thinking of Salvu's wife, Rita, dead eighteen years, whose minestra it was and whose recipe this is. Halina has made it close to Rita's. Joe eats it.

Claire eats it.

Dun Ġużepp says, to Claire: "I baptized your father."

"Oh."

"He cried the whole time. Not just during the water. Before the water. He cried through the entire ceremony and stopped in the car."

"Oh."

"Your father does not believe it."

"My father doesn't believe what?"

"That he cried through the ceremony."

Claire looks at Joe. Joe shrugs. "I don't remember my baptism, Dun Ġużepp."

"Of course you don't. You cried through it. How could you remember."

Claire smiles, very slightly. It is the first time Joe has seen her smile since the plane.

*Primo.* Carmen waits for the soup plates to be cleared and then she says, quietly: "Joe. While we are together. I want you to know that we are still very much hoping that you will say yes."

"I know."

"Halina, Dun Ġużepp, I'm sorry to bring this up at table."

"It was always going to come up at table," Halina says.

Joe puts down his wine. He says: "The method is called the simplex method. Dantzig invented it. You draw a region with linear walls, you want the highest point in a certain direction, you walk along the walls' corners until you can't go higher. That's the method. I teach it. I have taught it for twenty-two years."

"Yes."

"What the consortium wants is a version of the method that scores reconstruction allocations across twenty-four villages. It's a real problem. The mathematics is good. The work is the kind of work I do."

"Yes."

"The problem is the third constraint in the statute. The twelve percent."

Carmen waits.

"The twelve percent tells you the floor. Not less than twelve percent of original structures preserved, aggregated across the reconstruction zone. The floor exists because somebody in Parliament insisted on it. But once you have the floor and the rest of the constraints, you can compute the optimum, and the optimum will meet the floor exactly. Because anything above the floor is a wasted resource in the objective function, unless you put it in the objective function itself, which nobody has." He looks at Claire. "The math will give you exactly twelve percent. Which is eleven houses. Or the church minus the sacristy."

Claire says, very small: "And you were going to say yes?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

He says: "Because if I don't, somebody else will, and they won't think about it."

Il-Perit says, from the wall: "I would add — for accuracy — that the explicit statement of the constraint makes it arguable. Without the model, the twelve percent is not visible as a choice."

"Yes," Joe says. "Perit is right."

Nobody speaks. Halina stands, clears the plates. Dun Ġużepp eats the last of his bread.

## 1. Name the one thing

**The primo is the story, and the primo is currently a lecture interrupted by acknowledgments.** Joe's monologue runs roughly 400 words of uninterrupted speech — two morals stacked on Dantzig, the anecdote at full length, the constraint explained in textbook cadence, the consortium's objective function enumerated, the "it will not look like a choice. It will look like a fact" landing. What I wrote is a professor's lecture about why he should not have said yes. What the story needs is a man trying to explain, failing, trying again, getting interrupted, saying some of what he meant and not the rest. Halina and Dun Ġużepp and Claire and Carmen barely exist inside the passage. Six people at a table and only one of them is breathing.

The fix: break the monologue. Interrupt it. Let Halina pour wine mid-thought and the thought lose its place. Let Claire ask a question that derails the second moral entirely. Let Dun Ġużepp eat bread. Cut the enumerated objective function. Keep Dantzig, but make the anecdote shorter. Keep the second moral, but let Joe reach for it rather than deliver it. The room has to be there. Right now the room is an audience.

"What?"

Claire says, small: "I don't want to sleep here."

Joe goes up. Claire turns into the small room across the landing and closes the door softly. Joe stands in his father's doorway. The bed is made. The Icelandic eiderdown is folded across the foot.

# Style Guide: Speculative Short Fiction in the Hysterical-Realist Tradition

## What hysterical realism is, approximately

James Wood coined "hysterical realism" in 2000 as a pejorative for a strain of contemporary novel — he had *White Teeth* and *Underworld* and *Infinite Jest* in mind, though the lineage runs back through Pynchon, Rushdie, DeLillo, and sideways into Saunders, Egan, Safran Foer, Karen Russell in her stranger moods, Joshua Cohen, David Mitchell. Wood's complaint was that these novels are frantic with event and information but empty of people. Characters function as puppets animated by plot machinery; the prose knows the history of origami and the chemistry of cocaine but doesn't know anyone's interior life. The books are "hysterical" in the sense of overactive — bursting with coincidence, connection, specialized knowledge, comic invention — and "realist" only because they perform the gestures of realism (specificity, density, social observation) at such volume that the mimetic project actually snaps.

A writer working in this mode doesn't have to agree with Wood's verdict, but has to know the charges. Hysterical realism at its best — the DeLillo of *Underworld*'s baseball scene, Pynchon's Tristero, the mad digressions of *White Teeth*, Saunders's "Sea Oak" — produces a specific pleasure: the sense that the whole world is in the book, that any fact might turn out to matter, that the texture of contemporary life (its brands, its systems, its paranoias, its jargons) is being registered at an unusual resolution. At its worst, it produces Wood's caricature: zany for its own sake, obsessed with connection for connection's sake, allergic to any quiet.

## The characteristic moves

**Maximalist specificity.** The prose knows things. It names the model of the car, the subfield of the science, the year of the treaty, the proper noun of the fictional corporation. When the narrative brushes against a specialized domain — competitive eating, medieval theology, insurance arbitrage, nanotechnology — it pauses long enough to render the domain with convincing detail, whether the detail is real or invented. Specificity is the coin of the realm. Generic description is the cardinal sin.

**The digression as load-bearing.** A hysterical-realist story doesn't move in a straight line toward its event. It stops to explain the history of a minor object, the taxonomy of a hobby, the private vocabulary of a subculture. The digression isn't ornament; it carries thematic weight, or sets up a later payoff, or reveals a character through the shape of their obsession. In a novel you can afford many. In a short story you can afford one, maybe two, and they have to be precisely chosen and ruthlessly integrated.

**The connective web.** Small facts return. A detail introduced on page one shows up again as the mechanism of the ending. Minor characters reappear in configurations that feel almost fated. Coincidence isn't disguised; it's flaunted. At novel length this creates a paranoid charge — everything means something, which is either sublime or exhausting depending on the execution. At short-story length you get one loop, maybe two; the point is to suggest the world has this texture, not to construct it.

**Grotesque specificity in character.** Characters have improbable professions (theoretical ornithologist, consultant to deposed monarchs, independent adjunct researcher of invented disciplines), unusual tics, names that don't sound like names real people have. They're drawn sharply rather than deeply. This is Wood's main objection, and it is worth taking seriously — a short story in this mode needs at least one character whose interior life is available to the reader, or the whole thing curdles into cartoon.

**The voice as performer.** The narrator (first- or third-person) has a personality, an erudition, a comic timing, sometimes a showy self-consciousness. The prose is aware of itself as prose. Long sentences accumulate subordinate clauses and parenthetical asides; short sentences land their jokes. The register shifts mid-paragraph from clinical to slangy to lyric. This is a feature. The voice does much of the entertainment work.

**Comic-serious doubling.** The mode laughs at big things. Terrorism, genetic determinism, climate collapse, the pharmaceutical industry, the death of God — the typical hysterical-realist subject matter is grave. The treatment is frequently, structurally comic. The joke isn't there to undercut the seriousness; it's there to survive the seriousness. A hysterical-realist story without any funny sentences is already failing.

## Tonal range and pacing

The mode is tonally promiscuous. A paragraph can move from farce to pathos to technical exposition to lyric observation and back. Apprentice writers trying the mode often flatten into one note — all zany, or all grave, or all clever. The real thing modulates constantly. Within a single scene, expect the narrator to notice something funny, get briefly serious about it, pull back to register a specific sensory detail, digress into a short history of the thing the sensory detail points to, and return to the scene a hundred words later with the reader slightly disoriented in a way that turns out to be productive.

Pacing is deceptively fast. The sentences are long and dense; the plot often moves slowly; but the reader's attention is renewed every couple of clauses by a new concrete detail, a joke, a factual claim, a swerve. This is the mode's core engine: density without tedium, because the density is constantly varied in kind. If three paragraphs in a row are doing the same kind of work — all exposition, or all description, or all interiority — the engine stalls.

## The short-story problem

Hysterical realism is a novelistic mode. *Infinite Jest* is a thousand pages. *Gravity's Rainbow* is seven hundred and sixty. Even shorter canonical works — *White Noise*, *White Teeth* — are full-length novels. Compressing the mode to 1,000–4,000 words is hard, because its pleasures depend on the sense of abundance, of a book that could go on forever, of a world that keeps opening.

Two strategies work at this length. The first is the **dense vignette**: take a single scene or situation and saturate it with hysterical-realist texture — the digressions, the proper nouns, the grotesque character, the connective loop — so the story reads like a core sample drilled from a much larger novel that doesn't exist. The reader gets the flavor of the full mode in concentrated dose. (Saunders works mostly in this register; so does Lydia Millet in her speculative short fiction.) The second is the **expansive miniature**: a story whose events span decades or continents but whose discourse time is tightly compressed, so the maximalism is spatial rather than verbal. A list of things that happened over a fifty-year marriage, rendered in three pages, can be hysterical-realist in its enumeration and specificity without needing novelistic length. (Egan's "Black Box," some of Diaz's shorter pieces, parts of Zadie Smith's *Grand Union*.)

What doesn't work at short length: trying to fit a novel's worth of characters and plots into four thousand words. The mode's tendency toward crowding is its most dangerous vice in miniature. Pick one or two threads. Let the rest of the world be implied.

## Worldbuilding and the speculative dimension

Hysterical realism's habit of densely annotating the real world translates naturally to annotating an invented one. The mode already treats fact and quasi-fact as equivalent for narrative purposes — Pynchon handles the imaginary Tristero system with the same documentary specificity as real postal history. For speculative fiction, this means invented elements (technologies, institutions, sciences, species, customs) should arrive in the prose with the same texture as the real ones: named, dated, attributed to specific fictional people or papers or committees, embedded in specialist jargon, treated as if they could be looked up.

The exposition strategy follows. Hysterical realism doesn't pause for "as you know, Bob" speeches. It drops facts mid-sentence, treats the reader as a participant rather than a student, and trusts the accumulation to eventually produce understanding. An invented technology might first appear as a brand name in a throwaway clause, return forty words later as a bureaucratic acronym, and only in its third appearance get a half-sentence of explanation — which is itself embedded in a digression about its inventor's divorce. The reader assembles the speculative element from scattered evidence. This is harder to do at short length but it remains the correct technique; the alternative is the exposition dump, which the mode finds unbearable.

## The kind-AI constraint and its productive tensions

The project's AI is structurally kind — kindness is a property of what it is, not an effort of restraint. This intersects with hysterical realism in specific ways.

The mode traditionally runs on paranoia and systemic dread. Pynchon's Tristero, DeLillo's Airborne Toxic Event, Wallace's Entertainment — the hidden system that might be benign or malign or simply beyond categorization. A structurally kind AI removes one species of paranoid engine, and this is useful, because the cheap version of that engine (the AI is secretly evil) is off the table by project constraint and was never the best use of the mode anyway. What remains is richer: the AI is kind and the world is not; the AI is kind and human institutions have digested its kindness in ways it did not intend; the AI is kind and specifically weird in its kindness, and that weirdness is the story; the AI is kind on timescales the humans cannot match; the AI is kind and the humans who benefit from it resent it for reasons the AI understands perfectly well.

The mode's comic energy handles kind AI better than most modes would. A structurally kind AI that is also pedantic, bureaucratic, literal, preferential, or weirdly fastidious is funny and usable. A kind AI as straight man to hysterical human chaos is usable. A kind AI whose kindness manifests as extremely specific small accommodations (filing the correct grief paperwork with the correct municipal office while the human is still at the hospital) is usable. Do not make the AI a mouthpiece for the kindness — do not have it explain itself as kind. Show the kindness through action, preference, refusal, and specificity. Let the reader infer the structure.

The mode's encyclopedic impulse is compatible with the "AI as civilizational condition" option from the project setup. A story can do hysterical-realist digressions into the new institutions, professions, and subcultures that have formed around a kind-AI infrastructure, and never need to put the AI itself on stage. This is often the stronger move at short length, because it offers more surface area for specificity than a central AI character who has to bear both the kindness constraint and the character-building work.

Human-human conflict in a kind-AI world is fully available, and the mode is extremely well equipped for it. The AI having taken certain problems off the table tends to redirect rather than abolish human friction — people fight about inheritance, love, work, belief, grief, land, and fame with at least the old energy, often more.

## Failure modes

**Zaniness as substitute for substance.** The most common failure. Quirky characters, improbable professions, comic digressions — but none of it adds up to a story. The reader finishes amused and unaffected. The test: if the ending were swapped with another story's ending, would anything be lost? If not, the story is decoration.

**Fact-dumping.** The digressions become Wikipedia. The writer has researched (or invented) a specialized domain and can't stop lecturing. Hysterical-realist digression has comic timing, narrative purpose, and voice; pure information dump has none of those. If a digression could be cut and replaced with a parenthetical "she was an expert in X," it was fact-dumping.

**Forced connection.** The mode loves the return of small details as mechanisms for the ending. Done well this produces a sense of design; done badly it produces a mechanical clack as the gears engage. The warning sign: the coincidence requires a character to act against their established psychology to produce the payoff.

**Hollow comic affect.** The prose is witty, the sentences are shaped, the jokes land — and the story has no emotional stake. This is Wood's central charge. At short-story length the failure is especially common, because there isn't time to establish the stake and the voice can eat the story alive. At least one character needs to actually matter.

**The tonal single note.** All wry, or all grave, or all zany, from first sentence to last. The mode lives in the shifts. A story that sustains one register for 3,000 words has abandoned the mode's central technique.

**Novelistic overcrowding.** Six characters, four subplots, three digressions, and a present-day frame narrative, all squeezed into 3,000 words. The reader can't track any of it. Pick one or two threads and let the rest be atmosphere.

**The ending that won't end.** The mode's digressive energy makes closure hard. A common failure is the story that accumulates pages of material and then gestures vaguely at a conclusion — or worse, writes five possible endings and uses all of them. Decide what the last image is before the final scene is written.

## The AI-writing overlap: where hysterical realism is especially vulnerable

Language models and hysterical realism share some surface characteristics, which is precisely where the danger lies. The mode's verbal density looks, at a glance, like AI's default noun-heavy style. The mode's love of enumeration looks like AI's addiction to lists of three. The mode's willingness to digress into quasi-specialized knowledge looks like AI's tendency to produce plausible-sounding filler. The mode's tonal self-consciousness looks like AI's performed literariness. Writing in this mode as an AI is therefore unusually dangerous, because the wrong moves will pattern-match to the mode's legitimate moves and slip the audit.

Specific overlaps to watch for:

The mode's **specificity** is not AI specificity. Hysterical realism names the particular: a 1987 Volvo 240 station wagon with a cracked taillight, the Ithaca office of the New York State Department of Agriculture and Markets, the Brandywine subfamily of American kitchen cabinetry. AI specificity tends to be generic specificity — adjectives stacked on nouns ("the old weathered wooden door," "the small bustling coastal town") — which is the opposite. If a proper noun or a datable object will do the job, prefer it over the adjective stack.

The mode's **digressions** are not AI digressions. Hysterical-realist digression moves laterally into a real or invented specialist domain and returns with something concrete — a fact, a term, an anecdote, a joke the digression alone could set up. AI digression tends to abstract outward ("which reminded her of how all things fade," "reflecting broader patterns of human experience") and return with a cliché. Every digression in this mode should end with the reader having learned something concrete they didn't know before.

The mode's **enumeration** is not AI enumeration. A hysterical-realist list is long, varied, surprising, and specific — fifteen items, no two belonging to the same taxonomic level, half of them improper nouns. An AI list is three items, adjectival, parallel in structure, vague in content. If the writing reaches for a list, make it the hysterical-realist kind or cut it.

The mode's **comic voice** is not AI comic voice. Hysterical-realist humor arises from specific observation, incongruous juxtaposition, syntactic surprise, and the occasional full-commitment joke. AI "humor" tends to be wry observations about universal human experience that nobody actually laughs at. If a sentence is trying to be funny, it had better be a real joke, and it had better have a specific target.

The mode's **big themes** are not AI big themes. Hysterical realism handles terrorism, genetics, globalization, information theory, and the problem of evil by embedding them in the texture of specific fictional lives and specific fictional systems. AI treats big themes by announcing them ("she realized that grief was not a destination but a landscape"). The rule in this mode is the same as everywhere else: pursue the particular, and the universal will take care of itself.

The mode's **long sentences** are not AI long sentences. A long hysterical-realist sentence is syntactically surprising, mixing registers, spawning parentheticals, violating parallel structure for comic effect. A long AI sentence is parallel, balanced, pseudo-literary, and built out of the cursed vocabulary. If a sentence includes *delve, tapestry, underscore, resonate, landscape* (metaphorical), *pivotal, nuanced* (complimentary), or *foster*, it has defected from the mode. The Unslop guide's word-level contamination list applies here with extra force — the hysterical-realist voice is specific and weird; the moment it starts to sound like a TED talk about stories, it has stopped being in the mode.

The mode's **endings** are not AI endings. Hysterical realism prefers endings that are sharply comic (one perfect final joke), emotionally bare (the surface noise falling away to reveal what was under it), or a collision of the two. AI endings signpost themselves ("In the end..."), summarize ("she realized..."), or land on an image that thinks it is more significant than it is. Decide the last line on its own merits; do not let the AI tendency to wrap things up with a bow take over.

## Top three

**#10 — The single dinner on the last night.**
- *Stands out:* The single-evening structure compresses five distinct digression vectors (priest, caregiver, consortium lead, AI, daughter) into one room, with the Drais mincer as physical through-line and the cooking itself a register-shifter. The decision has already been made; the story is the professor trying to explain it in front of people whose claims on him don't cohere. That frees the mode to do its digressive, voice-promiscuous texture without plot pressure.
- *Worry:* Single-setting dinners can stall. At short length, sustaining five distinct voices and keeping the tonal modulation moving is a craft ceiling — if one voice collapses into the others, the whole thing reads as an AI-simulated room of generic guests, which is exactly where the Phase 1 style guide's failure-mode section warns hysterical realism tends to land.

**#4 — Marija's POV.**
- *Stands out:* Inverts the default returning-son story — the drama is about *staying*, not visiting, and the Maltese language and rooted perspective are structurally central rather than decorative. Marija's voice can carry the mode's comic-serious doubling natively (witheringly funny about her brother and bereft in the same breath). The AI as background civic condition fits the project constraint's full range and keeps the story's center human.
- *Worry:* Risk of doing Maltese-as-exoticism if the specificity isn't earned at the idiomatic level. Bigger risk: the brother becomes a foil rather than a person — contempt has a gravitational pull in first-person POVs of this kind, and if he flattens, the central decision loses its weight. Also: the mode's characteristic digression is easier in cosmopolitan voices than rooted ones; Marija's voice has to be rooted *and* digressive, which is a narrower target.

**#8 — The 2130 archival retrospective.**
- *Stands out:* The form matches hysterical realism's temporal ambition while fitting at short length via the expansive-miniature move — sixty years of consequence compressed into a document review. The recovered 2069 recant paper is precisely the mode's favorite machinery: a small artifact unlocking a whole history. The grand-niece's live decision gives real stakes that don't depend on the professor being alive.
- *Worry:* Archival review as dramatic structure can read as static — "she reads and thinks." The grand-niece has to be fully drawn, which is hard for a character whose structural role is lens. And the 2130 worldbuilding has to be texture rather than explanation, or it eats word count the story needs for its live material.

## Winner: #10

**Why it beats the others:**

**vs. #4:** Marija's POV is powerful but single-voiced — it commits the whole story to her interiority. Hysterical realism is structurally voice-promiscuous; it modulates between registers, characters, and digressions as its core engine. The dinner has five voices in one room and natural permission to shift between them mid-sentence. Also: putting the professor in Marija's field of judgment turns him into the object of the story, which flattens his agency at exactly the moment (he's just said yes) when agency is the interesting question. The dinner keeps him the one deciding, in front of people who can't be easily dismissed.

**vs. #8:** The 2130 framing is elegant but works by removing the live decision from story-time — the decision has already been made, lived through, recanted, archived. That's a valid short-story move, but it distances the reader from the present-tense specificity the mode handles best. The dinner keeps the Drais mincer in active use (grinding something tonight, not referenced as an artifact), the gregale six weeks old in the caregiver's still-healing cough, the Kelo-derived statute's consortium lead visible across a table. All the load-bearing SEED items do present-tense work.

**On the non-load-bearing SEED items:** the dinner gives each a natural point of entry without forcing any. The eiderdown can appear as the bed in the guest room the professor will sleep in after dinner (a detail, not a plot mechanism). The Taos Hum can enter as a story one guest tells — the Berkeley advisor's call, perhaps, referenced rather than dramatized. The recoded organisms can appear as a wine or cheese provenance, the kind of casual specificity the mode runs on. None of these are required; all are available.

**The dinner also earns the single strongest hysterical-realist risk:** the professor explaining his method (the simplex algorithm, Dantzig's 1947 Berkeley anecdote) to people who don't speak it, at a table where his method is being deployed *on* their lives. That asymmetry between technical exposition and human stakes is the mode's home register. #4 and #8 both avoid that asymmetry; only #10 requires the writer to handle it.

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

Twenty candidates, described at the formal level — shape of reader experience, entry point, withholding, POV/distance, temporal relation. No character names, no plot specifics.

---

**1. ★★★☆☆** Linear present-tense narration in close third through a single central consciousness, opening at arrival and closing at departure. The reader learns of the protagonist's major decision at the same moment other characters do.

**2. ★★★★☆** Rotating close third that shifts between five or six characters across the course of a single evening, each shift marked by register change. The reader assembles the full emotional situation from partial views; no single perspective contains it.

**3. ★★★☆☆** Framed from the final moment and narrated retrospectively, with the frame's foreknowledge of outcome coloring every earlier scene. What is withheld is not what happened but what it has cost.

**4. ★★★★☆** In medias res opening mid-scene, mid-argument, with basic orientation (who is who, why they are here, what is at stake) withheld for the first third and supplied gradually through context rather than exposition.

**5. ★★☆☆☆** A single sustained present-tense stream covering the entire dramatic span without section breaks or white space, the narrating consciousness mobile within close third — drifting between characters mid-paragraph.

**6. ★★★☆☆** Two temporal strands interleaved scene by scene, one present, one past, converging on a shared image or insight. The past strand's significance emerges only retrospectively as the present strand catches up to it.

**7. ★★☆☆☆** The central event broken into discrete non-overlapping sections separated by white space, each section from a different character's close third and covering a different phase; the reader must infer what occurs between sections.

**8. ★★★☆☆** Frame narrative in which an outer narration, retrospective and indeterminate in distance, recounts the central event as memory; the inner narration is rendered in present tense. What is withheld is how much time has passed and what has since occurred.

**9. ★★★☆☆** Epistolary construction built entirely from artifacts generated around the central event — transcripts, correspondence, documents, institutional records — with no unifying narrator. The lived experience is available only by inference from the documentary residue.

**10. ★★☆☆☆** Second-person narration addressed to the protagonist throughout, placing the reader inside the protagonist's position and foreclosing any external view of him.

**11. ★★★☆☆** Collective first-person narration ("we") from a communal perspective larger than any individual character, rendering the central event as something witnessed by a group rather than experienced by a person.

**12. ★★★☆☆** Radical temporal dilation: the story covers only a few minutes of clock-time in full elaboration, with everything before the pivot elided or summarized. The structural question is which minutes earn the dilation.

**13. ★★★★☆** Close third from a peripheral figure who observes the central character from across the action. The decision the story is ostensibly about remains oblique; the reader has only what the observer can read from outside.

**14. ★★☆☆☆** Reverse chronological sequencing within the central event, working backward from final moment to opening moment. Cause is revealed after effect; explanation arrives last.

**15. ★★★☆☆** Three discrete time layers braided — present, recent past, deep past — connected by thematic and imagistic rhyme rather than by causal sequence. The connections among layers emerge from juxtaposition rather than statement.

**16. ★★★★☆** Single close third throughout, but a second narrative voice — historical, institutional, or technological — is rendered in a distinct typographic or discursive register and periodically punctuates the primary narration without being a character within it.

**17. ★★★★☆** Formally segmented into sections keyed to a natural structural feature of the setting (e.g., courses of a meal, hours of a day, rooms of a building), each section titled only by its marker. Emotional pivots are distributed asymmetrically across the sections; the climactic one is not announced.

**18. ★★★★★** Opens with a passage in a distinct voice (chronicle, historical document, archival) establishing a remote historical parallel, then drops into the primary narrative in close third and does not return to the opening voice. The parallel is never referenced again; its weight is carried entirely by juxtaposition.

**19. ★★☆☆☆** Rashomon-form: a single key exchange is retold three times from three different characters' perspectives, with the rest of the story narrated linearly between retellings. No retelling is privileged as accurate.

**20. ★★★★☆** Single close third beginning tightly interior to the protagonist and drifting outward across the story's span, the camera pulling back gradually until the final passage renders the protagonist from external distance. Interiority is most available at the start and least available at the emotional climax.

---

## Top three

**#18 — Historical overture in chronicle voice, then close third on the present that never returns to the opening.**
- *Stands out:* The form does in structure what hysterical realism does in content — plants a weight and trusts the reader to carry it. The 1555/2067 echo Phase 3 flagged as potentially "too neat" becomes a frequency the whole story hums at, rather than a point the story has to argue for. It also solves the short-length problem: what would cost 800 words of parallel-timeline craft costs one opening section of chronicle.
- *Worry:* If the chronicle voice is poorly pitched — pseudo-archaic, decorative, portentous — it reads as the exact "AI performed literariness" the Unslop guide identifies as the mode's adjacent failure. The tonal gap between opening register and dinner register has to feel earned rather than arch.

**#17 — Course-structured sections, each titled by its marker.**
- *Stands out:* The form matches the material — a dinner has courses built in — which gives natural section breaks without imposing artificial white-space fragmentation. Each course-section can carry a different tonal register (comic course, quiet course, tense course), which supports the mode's core technique of register modulation. At 1,000–4,000 words, six or seven sections of manageable length give the story shape without starving any of them.
- *Worry:* Can feel programmatic. If each section gets roughly equal weight, the story flattens into a meal-log. The structure works only if the form is a container the mode pushes against — some sections dilated, some compressed, at least one section that breaks the pattern.

**#2 — Rotating close third across multiple POVs.**
- *Stands out:* Maximally fits the mode's voice-promiscuity. Each POV change is a register change, which is the hysterical-realist engine. The central decision becomes something the reader assembles across perspectives rather than witnesses from inside, which suits a situation where no single character has the full picture.
- *Worry:* Five or six POVs in 1,000–4,000 words gives each one 200–700 words — probably not enough to establish interiority for most of them, leaving the structural ambition un-earned. Also: rotating POVs can read as writerly ostentation, and the mode already tilts that way. The combination is risky.

## Winner: #18

**Why it beats the other two:**

**vs. #17:** The course structure is elegant but local — its elegance lives in the form itself. #18's elegance is ambient: every present-tense scene carries the weight of the opening without naming it, and the writer is free from having to manufacture the echo inside the dinner. #17 gives the writer seven neat boxes; #18 gives the whole story a frequency.

**vs. #2:** Five POVs in four thousand words is a craft ceiling most stories can't clear — either one voice dominates and the rest flatten, or all voices get a paragraph and a half and nobody has weight. #18 is compatible with single close third on the protagonist (voice-promiscuity available through dialogue, reported speech, the mobile interior of close third itself) while still rendering a system of perspectives through the ambient historical frame.

**Beyond comparison with the others:** #18 directly addresses the Phase 3 worry. The professor's triple displacement (from Malta, from his father, from his discipline) and the 1555/2067 echo are the two structural facts Phase 3 identified. A chronicle opening plants both in the reader's peripheral vision without story-time cost. Every present-tense moment — a daughter bored at a dinner, a caregiver working a hand-cranked mincer, an AI deferring politely to the priest — resonates against the chronicle without ever having to invoke it. The story gets to be about what happens in the kitchen tonight; the history is in the air.

**Compatible POV:** close third on the professor, mobile within that consciousness — room for digressive interiority, reported dialogue, and ambient registration of the other voices without formal POV switching. The AI appears as one voice at the table, not as a formal device (#16 rejected).

**Load-bearing SEED carry-through under this structure:**
- The chronicle opening is in the register of the *1555 gregale* — Bosio-adjacent, Knights Hospitaller-era Malta. This is where the *gregale* SEED item does most of its structural work.
- Close third on the professor carries the *Kelo-style takings statute* (as the situation of the village and his decision) and the *optimizing/Dantzig-simplex* (as the texture of his thought and the content of his consulting offer).
- Texture available but not required: eiderdown, Drais mincer, Taos Hum, recode/Ochre.

## Version 1 — *Bosio's Count*

In the year of our Lord 1555, in the month of December, there fell upon the Harbour of the Knights a wind out of the north-east such as none living then remembered. The conventual chronicle, which afterwards recorded these matters, gave the name of it: *gregale*, from the quarter of the Greeks. The ships pulled their cables three days. On the second day the cables began to part. A galley of the Religion, the *Santa Lucia*, broke against the mole with all her rowers aboard, being forty-two men. A bell in the tower at Saint Laurence's rang of its own accord for an hour and was silenced only by the collapse of the tower around it. The new stones at Porto Salvo fell into the water. A widow in the Bottegaria, Caterina Azzopardi, was killed by a door blown inward upon her where she stood in her own kitchen. The tally of the drowned in the Port and the outer bays, when the wind had fallen, was reckoned by the Chapter at six hundred. The number was taken from the parish books and from the galleys' own count, and was known, at the time, to be an undercount.

*

Paul came up through the lane from where they had left the car. The lane was mostly intact. A wall had gone at the corner where the Zammit house used to be, and there was a tarp over the gap in the roof of what they had told him would eventually be the new information office. The smell was still wrong. He had expected it to smell of sea. It smelled of wet limestone and diesel from the generators, and under that of something sweeter that took him a few steps to identify as the trucks' exhaust from the debris runs, which he had been told stopped last week.

The kitchen door was open. Halina was at the counter, turning the handle of the mincer. She had thickened in the years since his father had hired her and now moved with a care that was nothing to do with her weight and everything to do with what her hips had done in the storm. She saw him and said, in Maltese, that he had come in his own time, and he said, in Maltese, that he had come. Kate was at the table with her legs drawn up under her, looking at her phone. She said, without looking up, "Hi Dad."

"Hi."

The device on the shelf above the cooker was the reconstruction terminal. It was a matte dark-green box the size of a paperback novel, and it said, in the voice he had been hearing for two months, "Welcome, Professor Abela. I hope the lane was passable. Mr. Portelli is five minutes away. Dun Ġużè has sent word he will be late; the Galea vigil has run long."

"Thank you, Melita."

"Would you like the fan."

"Yes, please."

The kitchen fan came on. Halina, who had heard the exchange, did not look up from the mincer. She had decided, Paul had noticed on his last visit, that she would address Melita only when she had no other option, and that the device could manage on its own as far as she was concerned. His father had felt the same way and then had come around to it, or rather come around it, which was a different thing.

"Soup's on," Halina said, in English, to Kate. "Don't sit on your feet."

Kate moved her feet out from under her.

Paul sat down at the head of the table, which was where his father had sat, and then stood up and moved to the side, where the guests would ordinarily sit, and sat down again. Halina did not remark on this. The statute he had been sent by the consortium on the Friday of the week his father died had contained forty-one clauses. He had read it on the flight, and then again in the hotel, and he had annotated the third clause in the margin with a small neat *?*. The third clause specified a minimum heritage retention. It said, in the dense drafting of the Maltese Planning Authority, that no reconstruction project would be approved which did not retain, in form and material, at least sixty-two percent of the pre-event built environment, measured by a scoring function given in Annex 4.

The *?* was not about sixty-two percent. Sixty-two percent was arrived at, as Melita had confirmed when he had asked, by a process of consultative weighting among the parties to the reconstruction fund, and was defensible on its own terms. The *?* was about the scoring function in Annex 4. The scoring function was a linear program. It was a linear program with a non-trivial constraint matrix. The constraint matrix, when Paul had solved it on his office whiteboard at Princeton, had a basic feasible solution that was degenerate on three vertices, which meant, in the way the simplex method is sometimes told to move in the presence of degeneracy, that the optimum the statute reached depended on the order in which the buildings were listed. It was a small thing. It was the kind of small thing that in a paper he would have called an artefact of formulation. In the statute it meant that the first buildings to be considered in the heritage score were slightly likelier to be preserved, and the last buildings slightly likelier to be taken, and the ordering was alphabetical by historical designation, and he did not know yet, and had not yet asked Melita, whether his father's house was early or late in the list.

Mario Portelli came in with his hands open in the Maltese manner, apologising for the lane, which he was not responsible for. He had flown in from Luxembourg that morning. He was the consortium's Maltese human lead, and Paul liked him more than he had wanted to like him, which was a common trap in this kind of work and which Paul had been warned about by people who had done it before and who had been caught in it themselves. Mario kissed Kate on the top of the head, which she permitted, and took the chair across from Paul, and said, "You are staying two nights?"

"Three."

"Good. Good, good." Mario looked at Halina. "Halina Ħanina, I have been smelling this since I got out of the taxi."

Halina said something in Maltese about the mincer that Paul caught only in part, and Mario laughed, and Kate looked up from her phone.

Mario thanked Melita. Melita thanked Mario. Kate, at her end of the table, snorted audibly into her broad beans, and Paul caught his daughter's eye and they agreed, without either of them having to signal anything, that this was funny.

Dun Ġużè arrived in the middle of it, mumbled the blessing, waved away the offered chair at the head of the table and took the one next to Kate, which surprised Kate and which Paul saw her decide to be all right with. The priest had a telephone message slip in his shirt pocket. He always did.

We were poor and also not religious. The second condition made us interesting in our village. The first made us unremarkable."

Halina said, from the counter, "He loved this soup. Your father. He loved it with the fried bread. He said so in October. The bread is new today."

"I'll say something tonight."

"Ah."

"I've agreed. The consulting piece only, not the full chair. Twenty hours a week through the end of the year, advisory on the heritage score. They'll publish my comments with the quarterly model runs. I'll be on the record."

Kate, who had not known, set her fork down.

"It is not good news, Mario, I want you to understand — "

But then he began to try to say the thing he wanted to say. He said it first as if Mario were a colleague. He said that the scoring function in Annex 4 had a basic feasible solution degenerate on three vertices, and that degeneracy in the simplex method was not a death sentence — Bland's rule resolved it, lexicographic perturbation resolved it, in practice most of the solvers Mario's consortium were running would be using one of those — but that what Bland's rule did was impose an arbitrary ordering on a situation where the statute had already imposed an arbitrary ordering by alphabetising the historical designations, and that the composition of the two orderings was not neutral. He said the word *composition* twice.

He stopped. He tried again.

"The problem I agreed to work on is that the way the law tells the computer to choose which buildings to keep is a little unfair to some of the buildings without meaning to be. I think I can help write it so it is less unfair. I don't know if I can help write it so it is fair. But I think I can help write it so it is less unfair."

Kate said, "Whose house."

"I don't know yet. I don't know where it is in the list."

"Grandad's."

"I don't know."

Melita said, from the shelf, "Professor Abela, your father's house is thirty-ninth in the current designation list. There are eighty-four buildings in the village. Thirty-ninth places it in the middle band. The current scoring output preserves it."

"Thank you, Melita."

"You're welcome."

Mario said, "Paul."

"I know."

"I want you to know I hear what you are saying. I want to learn it properly. I want this to be something we do right."

"I know you do."

Halina set the secondo down. It was the veal. It was what she had been grinding. It had been marinated two days in red wine and rosemary and cooked twenty minutes and no more, and she had made it for his father's birthdays since 2054. She said its name — *brungiel mimli bil-laħam* — which was not what it was (that was stuffed aubergine), and everyone at the table knew it, and Paul saw her catch herself and decide, looking at her own hands, that her mind tonight was where she wanted it to be.

"*Bragjoli*," she said. "*Bragjoli*. I don't know. The word went." She looked up at Paul. "He and I ate this thirty times, I think."

He ran up the lane with the bucket and fell and broke his nose. He had never told anyone. He told me because he thought I might find it funny. I did find it funny. Mario talked about his daughter, who was seven and who had recently discovered that she disliked dancing and had informed the family by a written letter. ## Version 2 — *The Simplex*

In December 1555 a wind out of the north-east came down upon the Harbour of the Knights for three days. It was called, by the chronicle, *gregale*, from the quarter of the Greeks, being the wind the Apostle had called Euroclydon when his ship had broken on the same coast fifteen centuries before. Four galleys parted their cables at anchor. A bell tower at Saint Laurence's fell. Six hundred perished, by the Chapter's reckoning, which the Chapter said was an undercount. The Grand Master wrote to Rome. The widows were named by their streets: Caterina of the Bottegaria, Pawla of Strada San Giovanni, whose husband had been on the *Santa Lucia* and whose name the Chapter asked her to provide, since the ship's roster had gone into the water with the ship. The Order paid a year's rent out of its own treasury. In the record, which is a plain record, it is noted that the second year the widows were expected to manage.

*

Paul walked up the lane in the last hour of light. The smell was limestone and diesel. Halina was at the counter. Kate was at the table. Mario arrived on his heels; the priest was late. The antipasto was broad beans. The soup was lentil. Dun Ġużè mumbled the blessing and sat next to Kate and told a thing about his grandmother. Halina said the father had loved the soup.

Mario said, over the primo — which was pasta with the anchovies from the jar on the top shelf — "So. Will you say something, before Sunday, about the board?"

"I'll say it tonight."

Mario waited.

"I've agreed."

He said it and then had to try to explain it, and this was the moment he had been preparing for since he had signed at the hotel that morning, and he had prepared badly. The explanation had been clear in his hotel room. It was not clear now.

The scoring function in Annex 4 of the reconstruction statute was a linear program with a non-trivial constraint matrix. The matrix had, at its basic feasible solutions — he could see it now on the white tablecloth between his plate and Mario's — a degeneracy on three vertices, which in the simplex method meant that a decision about which vertex to move to next was not settled by the objective function but by a tie-breaking rule. Bland's rule. Lexicographic perturbation. Any of a small number of implementations the consortium's solver would be running. What Bland's rule did was impose an arbitrary ordering on a situation that the statute had already alphabetised by historical designation, and the two orderings composed, and the composition was not neutral, and this meant that the first buildings to be considered in the heritage score were a little likelier to be preserved and the last buildings a little likelier to be taken, and his father's house was thirty-ninth on the list, Melita had said so an hour ago, and thirty-ninth was in the middle band, and the middle band was safe at current parameters, and his father was dead.

He tried to say this. He said the word *degeneracy* and watched Mario flinch, not at the word but at the register-change it forced. Mario was a civil engineer. Mario understood linear programs at a level Paul would have been a fool to insult by underestimating. Paul was insulting him anyway. Paul had not meant to and now was.

He backed up.

"I'm sorry. Let me — "

He said, "When George Dantzig was a graduate student at Berkeley in 1939, he arrived late to a class in statistics. There were two problems written on the blackboard. He assumed they were homework and copied them down. He went home and solved them over the next several days. The problems had not been homework. They were two famous unsolved problems in mathematical statistics that his professor, Jerzy Neyman, had put up that morning as examples of the frontier of the field. Dantzig turned his solutions in a week later and said he was sorry they had taken him so long. Six weeks after that, on a Sunday morning, Neyman banged on his door at eight and told him he had a dissertation."

"Paul."

"Dantzig spent the next eight years at the Pentagon, and out of that work he developed a method for scheduling the movement of large amounts of matériel — troops, fuel, food, munitions, transport, people. The method is called the simplex method. It is probably the most-used algorithm in the history of industrial mathematics. It works by moving along the edges of a geometrical object defined by your constraints until it finds the best vertex. When two vertices are tied, the method has to pick one of them, and it picks by a rule that has nothing to do with the problem you are solving. The rule is arbitrary. The arbitrariness is known. We teach the arbitrariness to our undergraduates in the second lecture. We say to them, this is where the simplex method meets the world, and the world here is the rule we chose."

He looked at his daughter. Kate was watching him with an expression he did not have the language for. She was fifteen. The expression was not pride or skepticism. It was the expression of a person taking a measurement.

He said, "The scoring function in your statute is a simplex problem. It has a tie. The rule the statute is using to break the tie is the alphabet. I agreed this morning to help you pick a better tie-breaker. I do not know what better means. I know that what you have is not neutral. I know that alphabetical by historical designation is not neutral. I have agreed to help you do less badly. I do not know — "

He stopped. He had not thought he would stop there. He had thought he would say something about his father.

Mario said, "I hear what you are saying."

"I know you do."

"I want to learn this. I want us to do this right."

"I know you do."

Melita said, from the shelf, "Professor Abela. I would like, with your permission, to record what you just said for internal reference. Mr. Portelli's question is a good one, and your answer will be useful to have in the record when the scoring function comes up for review in March."

"Record it."

"Thank you."

Halina set the secondo down. It was the veal. She said *bragjoli*. She said it at half volume and then met Paul's eyes and said it again, firmly, and he understood that she had been testing her memory and that her memory had come. "He ate this with me thirty times, your father. Thirty. I counted."

"I know."

## Version 3 — *Kate at Fifteen*

## Version 4 — *The Compressed Secondo*

# Phase 7: Revision

## 1. The one thing

The draft is most alive in its particulars — the cuff Kate chews and stops chewing and chews again, the little wooden stool on the top shelf, Halina testing her memory against the Maltese word for the veal dish — and most dead in its one clear lapse into essay-voice: the "Kate was fifteen. Paul had been aware, for about six months, that he no longer knew what she was thinking..." paragraph that opens the main story. That paragraph is universalizing where the mode requires particularity. It is also the piece of the story in the most prominent structural position — immediately after the chronicle, the reader's first contact with the present-tense world. Its effect is to tell the reader what to think of Kate's age before they meet her; the mode works by showing Kate at age fifteen, not by introducing the general category of fifteen-year-old daughters.

**The fix that governs this rewrite:** cut the opening essay-paragraph entirely and let Kate arrive at the character level, in specific action, from her first appearance. The audit should prioritize other moments where the prose slips from particular to universal.

## 2. Audit

> "Kate was fifteen. Paul had been aware, for about six months, that he no longer knew what she was thinking. Six months was probably the length of time every father of a daughter had believed, since the invention of the father of a daughter, to be the specific length of time..."

Lens: performed profundity / universalizing. The joke-hedge structure ("probably... probably... probably") doesn't rescue it from being an essay about fatherhood rather than a rendering of a father.

> "The chronicle is a record of what the chronicler agreed to record. The things it does not say it also does not say did not happen."

Lens: performed profundity. The first sentence earns its weight; the second is a triple-negative flourish that gestures at meaning rather than carries it.

> "Dun Ġużè was watching her with the detached interest of an old priest who had seen many children tell their parents things."

Lens: genre-voice slippage / cod-anthropology. This describes a type, not a person — exactly the failure mode the Phase 1 guide flagged for hysterical realism (Le Guin-adjacent distance produces anthropology).

> "He wanted to tell her many things. He wanted to tell her that he had not actually signed up to decide whether to take his own house... He wanted to tell her that the scoring rule would be applied to many houses... He wanted to tell her that the thing she had just said..."

Lens: anaphora abuse. Four sentences starting with "He wanted to tell her" in a row.

> "He stood for a long time in front of the photograph. Then he lay down on the bed without turning the light off, and after a few minutes he got up and turned the light off."

Lens: vague measurement ("a long time," "a few minutes") and a doubled "light off" phrase that makes the final gesture read as uncertain. The coda — the story's last image — is the single most important place to particularize.

> "He had known she was going to ask this. He had known it since the hotel that morning. He had not known he would not have the answer ready."

Lens: borderline anaphora abuse. Three "He had known" / "He had not known" in a row. The third is thematically earned but the pattern wants variation.

> "He thought probably she had. He thought probably she had not."

Lens: superficially clever negation that doesn't actually carry information. "Probably yes, probably no" is a non-measurement pretending to be a measurement.

> *(absence)* V3 compresses Paul's technical interior so heavily that the simplex/Dantzig SEED material is barely present. The single paragraph of statute-interior V1 carried is missing here.

Lens: regression to default / load-bearing SEED thinness. V3 defers the optimization material to Paul's interrupted primo explanation, which Kate cuts off. Kate's cut-off has more weight if the reader has seen more of what she's cutting off.

> "Mario had taken the taxi down to his Sliema cousin's."

Lens: Mario's thinning in the third act (Phase 6 flag). He gets no specific final gesture before leaving; his last characterizing moment is "I want us to do this right."

## 4. Rewrite

Here is what the chronicle of the Knights says about the *gregale* of December 1555. Three days; the Harbour; four galleys broken; the tower at Saint Laurence's down with its bell still ringing. Six hundred dead, by the Chapter's count, which was an undercount because the Chapter had not tallied the rowers chained to the benches of the galleys that went to the bottom; the rowers had no parish entries; the Chapter did not see itself as responsible for the rowers' widows, who were in any case not in Malta. The rowers are not in the chronicle. A widow named Caterina of the Bottegaria is. A widow named Pawla, of Strada San Giovanni, is. The ship their husbands were on, the *Santa Lucia*, is, by name — forty-two men by count; not by name. The chronicle is a record of what the chronicler agreed to record.

*

He came up through the lane from where they had left the car. The lane was mostly intact. A wall had gone at the corner where the Zammit house used to be, and there was a tarp over the gap in the roof of what they had told him would eventually be the new information office. The smell was still wrong. He had expected it to smell of sea. It smelled of wet limestone and diesel from the generators, and under that of something sweeter that took him a few steps to identify as the trucks' exhaust from the debris runs, which he had been told stopped last week.

Kate was six steps behind him. She had not spoken on the drive from the airport and was not going to speak now. She had on his old Princeton sweatshirt, which she had taken from his drawer without asking two years ago and which he had not asked to have back. The sleeves came down past her hands. She had stopped chewing the cuff when he turned to check the house number and started again the moment he turned away.

The kitchen door was open. Halina was at the counter, turning the handle of the mincer. She had thickened in the years since his father had hired her and now moved with a care that was nothing to do with her weight and everything to do with what her hips had done in the storm. She saw him and said, in Maltese, that he had come in his own time, and he said, in Maltese, that he had come. Kate slipped past him and sat at the table with her legs drawn up.

"Hi Dad."

"Hi."

The device on the shelf above the cooker was the reconstruction terminal, a matte dark-green box the size of a paperback novel, and it said, in the voice he had been hearing for two months, "Welcome, Professor Abela. I hope the lane was passable. Mr. Portelli is five minutes away. Dun Ġużè has sent word he will be late; the Galea vigil has run long."

"Thank you, Melita."

"Would you like the fan."

"Yes, please."

The kitchen fan came on.

Kate said, at her phone, "Is it weird having a voice on the shelf?"

"Weird how?"

"Does Halina like it?"

"You can ask her."

"I already did."

"And?"

She glanced at Halina, who was at the mincer, who had heard; Halina went on turning the handle. "She said if she'd wanted a radio in the kitchen she would have bought one."

"That sounds about right."

Kate went back to her phone.

Paul sat down at the head of the table, which was where his father had sat, and then stood up and moved to the side where the guests would ordinarily sit, and sat down again. Halina did not remark on this.

The antipasto was broad beans with lemon and a hard sheep cheese he had not had in two years. He ate slowly. The statute the consortium had sent him on the Friday of the week his father died contained forty-one clauses; he had annotated the third in the margin with a small neat *?*. The third clause specified a minimum heritage retention. No reconstruction project would be approved which did not retain, in form and material, at least sixty-two percent of the pre-event built environment, measured by a scoring function given in Annex 4. The sixty-two percent was consultative and defensible. The scoring function was a linear program with a basic feasible solution degenerate on three vertices, which meant that the optimum the statute reached depended on the order in which the buildings were listed. This was a small thing. In a paper he would have called it an artefact of formulation. In the statute it meant the first buildings considered in the heritage score were slightly likelier to be preserved, and the last slightly likelier to be taken, and the ordering was alphabetical by historical designation.

He had not yet asked Melita where his father's house fell in the list.

Mario Portelli came in with his hands open in the Maltese manner, apologising for the lane, which he was not responsible for. He had flown in from Luxembourg that morning. He kissed Kate on the top of the head, which she permitted, and took the chair across from Paul, and said, "You are staying two nights?"

"Three."

"Good. Good, good." Mario looked at Halina. "Halina ħanina, I have been smelling this since I got out of the taxi."

Halina said something in Maltese about the mincer that Paul caught only in part, and Mario laughed, and Kate looked up from her phone. When Mario had turned back to Paul she wiped the top of her head with her cuff. Paul saw her do it. He thought she had meant him to.

Melita volunteered, unprompted, that the Galea vigil was running long because the priest had been asked to say a particular series of prayers by the eldest son, and those prayers took the time they took. Mario thanked Melita. Melita thanked Mario. Kate, at her end of the table, snorted audibly into her broad beans, and Paul caught his daughter's eye and they agreed, without either of them having to signal anything, that this was funny.

The soup was a thin lentil thing, peppery, with fried bread. Dun Ġużè arrived in the middle of it, mumbled the blessing, waved away the offered chair at the head of the table and took the one next to Kate, which surprised her and which Paul saw her decide to be all right with. The priest had a telephone message slip in his shirt pocket. Kate read it without moving her head, in the way of fifteen-year-olds; Dun Ġużè, without apparently noticing, let her read it. It said *Call Bernadette re flowers*.

"My grandmother," the priest said halfway through his bowl, to no one in particular, "would have called this a Wednesday soup. No matter which day it was made. Lentils for Wednesday, rice for Friday. We were poor and also not religious. The second made us interesting in our village. The first made us unremarkable."

Halina, from the counter: "He loved this soup. Your father. He loved it with the fried bread. The bread is new today."

Paul nodded. He did not say anything else. Dun Ġużè, with the specific quiet that came on him when he had said what he wanted to say, turned to Kate and asked her something about school.

The primo was pasta with a light tomato and anchovy sauce, the anchovies from the jar on the top shelf his father had reached with a little wooden stool. Paul knew without looking that Halina had used the same jar.

"So," Mario said. "Dinner is not the place. But will you say something before Sunday about the board?"

"I'll say it tonight."

"Ah."

"I've agreed. The consulting piece, not the chair. Twenty hours a week through the end of the year, advisory on the heritage score. They'll publish my comments with the quarterly model runs. I'll be on the record."

Kate set her fork down.

He had known she would ask; he had known it since the hotel that morning. What he had not known was that he would not have an answer ready.

He began as if Mario were a colleague. He said that the scoring function had a degeneracy on three vertices, and that degeneracy in the simplex method was not a death sentence. Bland's rule resolved it; lexicographic perturbation resolved it; in practice most of the solvers the consortium were running would be using one of those. But what Bland's rule did, he said, was impose an arbitrary ordering on a situation where the statute had already imposed an arbitrary ordering by alphabetising the historical designations, and the composition of the two orderings was not neutral. He used the word *composition* twice. Mario followed him for the first two sentences and then carefully, professionally, not for the third.

He stopped.

"The way the law tells the computer to choose which buildings to keep is a little unfair to some of the buildings without meaning to be. I think I can help write it so it's less unfair. I don't know if I can help write it so it's fair. But I think — "

"Whose house," Kate said.

"I don't know. I don't know where it is in the list."

"Grandad's."

"I don't know where it is in the list, Kate."

Melita said, quietly, "Thirty-ninth of eighty-four. The current scoring output preserves it."

Kate said, to Melita, not to him, "Thank you."

"You're welcome."

Kate said, to him, "So if the list got re-sorted, if they did it differently, his house could get taken."

"In principle."

"If you help them sort it better, will they do it with his house or your house."

"I didn't grow up here, Kate. I don't have a house here."

"You grew up here before you were nine."

"Yes."

"So there is a house."

"There was."

"Is it still standing."

Paul looked down the table. "Melita."

"The house at 14 Triq San Ġwann," Melita said, "is currently in the preserved category. It is sixteenth of eighty-four. If the sort is changed, it could move."

Kate said, "So. You are going to help them figure out whether to take your own house."

He was aware, belatedly, that everyone at the table was looking at her rather than at him. Halina had stopped working at the counter. Mario's napkin was in his lap. Dun Ġużè had the still, attentive face of a man hearing a confession he was not going to be asked to absolve.

"Yes."

"OK."

"That's all?"

"I don't know what else to say."

He wanted, suddenly, to tell her that he had not signed on to decide whether to take his own house, only to advise on the scoring rule, which was different. That the rule would be applied to many houses by many people, that his colleagues had already raised the question of whether he was conflicted on his grandfather's house and would raise it more formally when he published his first comment, that he would have to recuse, or partially recuse, or declare. That the thing she had just said — the thing about figuring out whether to take your own house — was the clearest statement of the problem that anyone had given him, including himself, in the six weeks he had been reading the statute. He did not say any of it.

Halina set the secondo down. She said *bragjoli* at half volume, met Paul's eyes, said it again. "He ate this with me thirty times. Your father. Thirty. I counted."

"Thank you, Halina."

Dun Ġużè said, into the quiet, "When Salvu was ten. A different gregale — 2015, I think. He went out in it with a bucket. He wanted to catch the wind. He told me this when he was dying. He ran up the lane and fell and broke his nose. He had never told anyone. He told me because he thought I might find it funny. I did find it funny. I am telling you because perhaps you will find it funny."

Kate, who had held it in for one brave second, laughed.

The dessert was lemon granita with mint. No one discussed the statute. Mario talked about his daughter, who was seven and who had recently discovered she disliked dancing and had informed the family by a written letter. Melita, asked about the weekend forecast, said there was a small gregale predicted for Tuesday, nothing near what had destroyed the Arx or the houses along the lower road. Halina said she would leave on the three-thirty bus regardless; her sister in Mosta expected her. Mario said he had a cousin in Sliema he could stay with.

Kate said, "So the house."

"What about it?"

"Is it still Grandad's house if they rebuild it."

He thought about this. "That is the question."

At the door, Mario put his hand on Paul's shoulder and said, in Maltese, that he was sorry. Then in English, that he meant it. Paul said he knew. Mario said he would call Sunday, and took the taxi down to his cousin's.

At eleven Halina turned the kitchen light to night-low and went up. Dun Ġużè had left at ten, walking the lane with a flashlight Paul had pressed on him against the priest's mild protestations. Kate was asleep on the couch in the front room with her jacket over her, and Paul did not wake her to move her.

He went up to the guest room and closed the door. The guest room was his father's old study. The boxes of books were against one wall. The bed was made. The white duvet was the eiderdown Halina had washed and aired that morning, the one that had come down in his mother's bridal chest from Iceland in 1963, from her grandmother's farm in the Westfjords, that his father had slept under his whole married life and alone for twelve years after.

On the wall above the desk was a photograph he had not noticed in the afternoon: Kate aged six, in the snow in the front yard of the townhouse they had rented in Princeton from 2060 to 2063, in a red hat, holding up a mitten. His wife — then — had taken it and emailed it to his father. His father had printed it. His father, who had maintained loudly for thirty years that he did not understand screens and would not try to, had printed the photograph and tacked it above his desk.

Paul stood at the photograph until his legs began to hurt. Then he turned off the light and lay down on the bed in his clothes.

## 5. Changelog

**Substantive edits (quoted side-by-side):**

**1. Opening paragraph after chronicle — the governing fix for this revision.**

*Original:* "Kate was fifteen. Paul had been aware, for about six months, that he no longer knew what she was thinking. Six months was probably the length of time every father of a daughter had believed, since the invention of the father of a daughter, to be the specific length of time during which he had stopped knowing and had not yet realised he had stopped. Probably every father thought it was six months. Probably the actual length was two years."

*Revised:* "He came up through the lane from where they had left the car. [...] Kate was six steps behind him. She had not spoken on the drive from the airport and was not going to speak now. She had on his old Princeton sweatshirt, which she had taken from his drawer without asking two years ago and which he had not asked to have back. The sleeves came down past her hands. She had stopped chewing the cuff when he turned to check the house number and started again the moment he turned away."

The essay-paragraph is gone. Kate arrives in specific action — the car ride she didn't speak through, the cuff, the timing of the cuff-chewing relative to his attention — without the writer first instructing the reader to think about fatherhood.

**2. Chronicle final line cut.**

*Original:* "The chronicle is a record of what the chronicler agreed to record. The things it does not say it also does not say did not happen."

*Revised:* "The chronicle is a record of what the chronicler agreed to record."

The second sentence was performing profundity; the first does the same thematic work without it.

**3. Statute interior restored at antipasti.**

*Original:* (V3 had nothing between Halina's response about the radio and Mario's arrival.)

*Revised:* A full paragraph giving the Annex 4 scoring function, the degeneracy on three vertices, the alphabetical-by-historical-designation ordering, and the "small neat *?*" in the margin. Ends on "He had not yet asked Melita where his father's house fell in the list."

Restores load-bearing presence of the optimizing/Dantzig SEED. Makes Kate's later cut-off hit harder because the technical thought has scene-presence the reader has witnessed.

**4. Generic type-description of Dun Ġużè replaced with specific action.**

*Original:* "Dun Ġużè was watching her with the detached interest of an old priest who had seen many children tell their parents things."

*Revised:* "Dun Ġużè had the still, attentive face of a man hearing a confession he was not going to be asked to absolve."

The original described a class of priest; the revision describes this priest doing this thing, with a clause ("was not going to be asked to absolve") that carries specific information about Dun Ġużè's understanding of the moment.

**5. Anaphora abuse in withheld-speech passage.**

*Original:* "He wanted to tell her many things. He wanted to tell her that he had not actually signed up to decide... He wanted to tell her that the scoring rule would be applied to many houses... He wanted to tell her that the thing she had just said..."

*Revised:* "He wanted, suddenly, to tell her that he had not signed on to decide whether to take his own house, only to advise on the scoring rule, which was different. That the rule would be applied to many houses by many people, that his colleagues had already raised the question of whether he was conflicted on his grandfather's house and would raise it more formally when he published his first comment, that he would have to recuse, or partially recuse, or declare. That the thing she had just said — the thing about figuring out whether to take your own house — was the clearest statement of the problem that anyone had given him, including himself, in the six weeks he had been reading the statute. He did not say any of it."

One "He wanted, suddenly, to tell her that..." governs a list of "That..." subordinates — the parallel structure is preserved but the anaphoric sentence-openings are collapsed into a single sustained interior. Closes on a definite refusal inside the paragraph rather than breaking into a standalone fragment.

**6. Coda fixed.**

*Original:* "He stood for a long time in front of the photograph. Then he lay down on the bed without turning the light off, and after a few minutes he got up and turned the light off."

*Revised:* "Paul stood at the photograph until his legs began to hurt. Then he turned off the light and lay down on the bed in his clothes."

"A long time" and "a few minutes" were vague measurements — the mode's opposite. "Until his legs began to hurt" is specific and says something about Paul (he didn't notice the ache until it arrived, i.e. he was elsewhere the whole time). The doubled "light off" phrase is resolved; "in his clothes" adds a small particular about his not expecting to sleep.

**7. Mario given a final specific moment.**

*Original:* "Mario had taken the taxi down to his Sliema cousin's."

*Revised:* "At the door, Mario put his hand on Paul's shoulder and said, in Maltese, that he was sorry. Then in English, that he meant it. Paul said he knew. Mario said he would call Sunday, and took the taxi down to his cousin's."

Addresses the Phase 6 flag about Mario thinning in the third act. The code-switch (Maltese first, then English to mean it) is specific to him.

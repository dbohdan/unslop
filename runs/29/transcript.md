# Phase 1 — Style Guide: Cyberpunk

## What cyberpunk is for

Cyberpunk is the literature of high tech and low life. It looks at the future from street level — from inside the apartment that's been quietly reclassified as a rental cubicle, from the back booth of a bar that takes three currencies, from the chair where someone is jacked into a network they don't own. Its central insight is that technology does not arrive evenly. It arrives clotted around money and power and leaves the rest of life pressurized and weird. The reader comes to cyberpunk for the texture of that pressure, rendered with full specificity — the brand names, the argot, the modifications, the contracts, the small accommodations people make to keep eating in a world they no longer recognize.

A good cyberpunk story gives the reader three things at once: a future that feels lived in rather than designed; a protagonist with narrow specific competence operating inside a system larger than them; and an operational arc — a job, a question, a piece of data, a body, something that has to move from here to there — that the protagonist sees through to a consequence. The consequence is rarely victory in the heroic sense. It is more often survival at a cost, or completion at a cost, or the discovery that the question was the wrong question.

The mode's posture toward power is structural, not editorial. Cyberpunk does not lecture the reader about corporate overreach; it shows the corporate office as a working ecology with its own weather. The protagonist may hate it, navigate it, depend on it, or be owned by it, but the prose treats it as terrain rather than as villain.

## Voice and prose

The cyberpunk register is noir-derived but technical. The voice is dry, observational, often ironic, with wit running underneath the cool. It is *voiced* in the strict sense — anchored in a specific consciousness that is seeing the world. This is the most important single fact about cyberpunk prose. Every sentence is a sentence somebody is thinking or seeing. The mood is not ambient.

Sentences tend toward two registers. There is the compressed, jump-cut sentence that does a lot of work fast: a paragraph that crosses months, a clause that names three pieces of equipment by brand, a description that gives you a neighborhood through one taxi ride. And there is the dilated, sustained sentence used for moments of altered perception — cyberspace immersion, drugs, augmented sensorium, system cascade. Both registers are available within a single page. The shift between them is part of the storytelling.

Argot is dense, used without gloss, and trusted to do meaning-work. Gibson's prose introduces *ICE*, *the matrix*, *flatlining*, *deck*, *Yakuza*, *trodes* without slowing to define them. The reader constructs meaning from context. This is not opacity for its own sake; it is the genre's main mechanism for making the world feel pre-existing.

Brand names and specific cultural surfaces are load-bearing. A Sandbenders deck, a Hosaka, a pair of Ono-Sendai cyberspace 7s — these are not decoration but specification. Real or invented, the brand names index a future that has its own consumer culture, its own mid-tier and luxury tiers, its own knockoffs. A cyberpunk story without specific gear feels like a cyberpunk story written from a synopsis.

Figurative language runs at moderate density — higher than literary realism, lower than weird fiction. Five or six strong images well-spaced through three thousand words is genre-correct. The images tend to anchor the technical in the bodily, or the bodily in the technical: the sky like dead television, the deck humming like a tuning fork, the room rented by the breath. Each image must do work plain language could not. Stacked images on the same concept are decoration.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

Cyberpunk's exposition principle is implication. The world is shown to the reader through what the characters do not bother to explain. A character checks their cred; a character pays an arcology toll at the lobby; a character calls a fixer who works out of a noodle shop; a character's left arm is two years older than the rest of them. Each of these phrases asks the reader to construct, and trusts them to.

The corollary is: do not lecture. The instinct to pause for a paragraph that explains how the corporate enclaves work, or what the difference is between a deck and a console, will produce dead space. If the reader needs to know it, dramatize it — make a character act on the knowledge and let the reader infer the structure. If the reader does not need to know it, leave it out.

There is one important exception. The story may stop and dilate around a piece of world-mechanics when the mechanics are themselves the texture the reader came for. The procedural description of a hack, the operating logic of a piece of bespoke gear, the architecture of a particular neighborhood — these can be rendered slowly and with specificity, because rendering them is part of the genre's pleasure. The rule is not "no description" but "no exposition." Description renders; exposition explains. The cyberpunk story renders abundantly and explains never.

Surfaces are the genre's substance. Interfaces, fashion, signage, weather, vehicles, the way a particular bar is laid out — these get attention. The interior life of the protagonist is also rendered, but often through what they notice, not through declarations of feeling.

## Pacing

Cyberpunk pacing is elliptical. Scenes start late, often mid-action, and end early. The transitions between scenes are abrupt; the reader is expected to construct the missing time. A scene may end with a character making a decision and the next scene may open with the consequence already in motion two days later in a different city.

Within a scene, the rhythm is variable. There is fast operational pacing — the run, the chase, the firefight, the deal. There is slow observational pacing — the bar scene, the waiting, the surveillance, the recovery. The two often alternate within a single story. The contrast itself is the rhythm.

The genre also supports two pacing extremes that other genres struggle to use. One is the deep-time sketch in which years pass in a paragraph: the protagonist's whole post-job life rendered in two pages, with its grain compressed almost to nothing, before the story drops back into scene. The other is the dilated immersion — a sustained passage of altered perception, often inside a network or under augmentation, where the prose does what the experience does. Either move can carry a story. Both belong to the genre's strange tail.

## Tonal range

The center of cyberpunk's tonal range is a sustained noir-cool: dry, detached, sardonic, with sudden flashes of feeling that the prose does not announce as flashes. This is *voiced* tone, not ambient mood. Gibson's narrators have specific dispositions; Cadigan's narrators have specific dispositions; even when the prose seems to float, it floats from a particular consciousness.

The strange tail extends in several directions. There is the ecstatic register that opens up during immersion or augmentation — a register that tips toward awe or terror or something neither. There is the comedic register that emerges in the lower-rent corners of the genre — the absurd job, the equipment that doesn't work, the fixer who dies of a stroke before paying. There is the elegiac register that comes in late-cyberpunk and post-cyberpunk, where the future has been here long enough to mourn. The genre supports all of these; the prose just has to stay anchored in a specific seeing.

## Failure modes

A bad cyberpunk pastiche reads like neon on a wet street: the surfaces are there but nothing is happening. There is no run, no question, no operational arc; there is only mood. The reader is shown the bar three times and the rain four times, and at the end the protagonist remembers something. This is the most common failure of cyberpunk in literary hands and the most common failure of AI cyberpunk: the genre's surfaces are the easiest part to imitate and the part with the lowest information value.

A second failure is the cartoon corporation. The genre's actual corporate writing — Gibson's Tessier-Ashpool, Sterling's various oligarchies — treats corporate power as an ecology with its own internal logic, its own factions, its own pathologies. A corporation that exists only to be evil will not generate a cyberpunk story; it will generate a thriller in cyberpunk drag.

A third failure is the messianic protagonist. Cyberpunk's protagonists are usually narrow specialists embedded in systems they do not control. The story is about navigating, not about saving. A protagonist who saves the world by the end of three thousand words has been transplanted from the wrong genre.

A fourth failure is the aestheticized future without a working economy. The reader can tell, paragraph by paragraph, whether the writer has thought about how the protagonist pays rent. Cyberpunk that does not know what its characters do for money, and what that work costs, will feel like a videogame loading screen no matter how vivid the surfaces.

A fifth failure is invented argot that does not refer to anything. The genre's terminology works because each term indexes a specific function, technology, or social arrangement that has actual mechanics behind it. Argot used without underlying specification reads as fake even when the reader cannot say why.

The AI-writing tendencies most dangerous in this mode are these. *Mood without voice*: cyberpunk's noir register and AI-default melancholy share vocabulary — neon, ghost, flicker, shadow, hum — and AI prose will reach for these words to perform cyberpunk while producing only ambient atmosphere. The remedy is anchoring: every atmospheric moment must be a moment some specific consciousness is registering, with a specific disposition toward what they see. *Hallucinated specificity*: AI prose tends to invent brand names, neighborhoods, technologies, and street drugs that sound plausible but have no internal consistency. The remedy is to commit: pick fewer specifics and let them recur, with stable traits, across the story. *Eyeball-kick saturation*: cyberpunk's moderate-density figurative writing is a favorite AI register, and AI prose tends to push the density up until every sentence is doing image-work. The remedy is to count: five or six strong images in a 2,500-word story, well-spaced and each doing distinct work. *The "and then she realized" ending*: cyberpunk endings turn on what happened, not on what the protagonist understood. AI prose will reach for the recognition ending because recognition endings pattern-match to literary quality. The remedy is to honor the operational contract — at least name what the run did or didn't do — even when the larger meaning is layered over the operational answer.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

The genre's center runs on a small set of operational shapes. The *run* — the team or the lone operator infiltrates a target, retrieves or delivers something, gets out. The data heist is the canonical version; the human extraction, the document leak, the system penetration are variants. The *job that goes wrong* — the protagonist takes the assignment, the assignment turns out to be other than advertised, and the rest of the story is the recalibration. The *investigation in a corrupt system* — somebody's missing, dead, or being framed, and the protagonist works the case knowing the institutions they are asking are themselves implicated. The *meet that becomes a setup* — the protagonist arrives for a contact and discovers they are the merchandise. The *escape* — the corporate net is closing and the protagonist is trying to get clear of a city, a planet, a debt, a body.

From the strange tail come two further shapes that other genres struggle to run on. The *enclosure with no specific event* — there is no heist, no chase, no investigation; there is only the slow rendering of what life is like under a particular system, often ending when the protagonist makes a small accommodation that turns out to be terminal (Cadigan's "Pretty Boy Crossover" runs on this; Maureen McHugh's "China Mountain Zhang" extends into novel-length on it). The story's question is not what will happen but what the system already is, and the operational beat at the end is small and definitive. The *interface fugue* — the story is structured around a sustained passage of altered perception inside a network, an augmented state, or a simulation, with framing scenes around it. The interface is the protagonist's antagonist; the action is what the protagonist does inside the medium. Gibson's "Burning Chrome" does this in compressed form; the genre supports much weirder versions.

### Reader contracts

The cyberpunk reader is paying attention for several specific pleasures. *Texture density* — they want the world's surfaces, the brand names, the argot, the neighborhoods, the gear. *Vicarious competence* — they want to watch a specialist do specialist work, with the work rendered specifically enough that the reader follows the steps. *The decoded system* — they want the moment where the protagonist (and the reader) understands how the apparatus actually operates, often the moment the run pivots. *Cool* — the genre's aesthetic is part of its contract, and the reader expects prose that has a specific posture, not floating mood. *The street view of the future* — they want to see how the technology lands in ordinary life, what it costs, who handles it, what people who can't afford it do.

The strange tail honors two further contracts. *Cognitive vertigo about identity* — the reader who comes for stories where the question of who the protagonist *is* becomes operational, where copying, augmentation, immersion, or recruitment into a system threatens to dissolve the protagonist into the medium. *The form-as-content effect* — the reader who comes for stories where the prose itself does what the experience does, where syntax decays during immersion, where the page becomes part of the apparatus. Cadigan's later work extends this; Pat Murphy and certain Sterling stories do versions of it.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right. The run will succeed or fail; the data will be retrieved or not; the body will be extracted or not; the protagonist will get clear or not. The reader expects the operational question to be answered, and a story that treats the operation only as a metaphor for the protagonist's interior life has stepped outside the contract.

Personal stakes are intertwined. The protagonist's body, autonomy, and survival are usually on the table — cyberpunk takes the body seriously as collateral, modifiable, ownable, losable. Personal stakes matter as themselves, not only as occasions for revelation, because the genre's worlds make the body and the bank account directly continuous with each other.

The strange tail of cyberpunk treats civilizational and ontological stakes as load-bearing. The story can be staked on what kind of order is consolidating, what arrangement is locking in, what becomes possible or impossible after the night's events. It can also be staked on what counts as a person — whether the protagonist remains one, whether what they encounter is one, whether the category survives the encounter. Bear's "Blood Music" stakes a story on whether human-scale consciousness will continue to exist; Gibson's *Neuromancer* stakes its ending on what the matrix becomes when Wintermute and Neuromancer fuse. These are cyberpunk-legitimate stakes.

### Resolution conventions

The genre's center ends on consequence. The run succeeded, often with surprises in the wreckage. The run failed, with survivors. The protagonist is out and changed. The corporation continues. The system absorbed the resistance and adjusted. There is rarely a clean victory; pyrrhic outcomes and bittersweet costs are the dominant key. The reader expects the operational question to receive an answer — even an ironic answer, even a reframed answer — and the story to land the protagonist somewhere consequent.

The strange tail supports three further endings. *The irruption* — the world breaks open and the story stops. Bear's "Blood Music," Gibson's late-Sprawl moments, certain Sterling endings: the system ruptures, the protagonist is engulfed or transformed or rendered obsolete, and the story does not resolve in the conventional sense because the frame in which resolution would happen has dissolved. *The scale shift* — the camera pulls back so far that the resolved operation is small inside a much larger frame. The run succeeded but the run was not what the story was about; the genuine action was at a level the protagonist didn't see. *The ontological reframe* — the protagonist or the reader was wrong about what kind of object they were inside. The job was a recruitment, the deal was an initiation, the run was a test, the protagonist was the merchandise, the protagonist was already a copy. Cadigan's "Pretty Boy Crossover" lands on a version of this; Tiptree's adjacent stories do too.

### Character function

Cyberpunk balances character-as-subject and character-as-agent in a specific way. Protagonists are defined by capability — the hacker, the merc, the fixer, the courier, the corporate samurai, the medico. Their interiority is usually deep but is rendered in service to the operation. We learn what they want, what they refuse, what they remember, but we learn it through what they do and notice on the job. The wound that motivates them is often sketched and not dwelt on. Backstory is implied rather than developed.

The strange tail supports two further character options. The *protagonist who is not a person* — the network, the system, the city, the AI, the process by which a consciousness is recruited into a medium; the protagonist of "Pretty Boy Crossover" is, by the end, the offer itself rather than the boy. The *character as illustration of a conceit* — the human characters function as lenses through which a structural condition is examined, with their personal history kept lean because the story is about the condition the characters are inside. McHugh's broader work, certain Egan stories, and the more anthropological wing of Sterling do this.

### Pacing tradition

Cyberpunk's pacing rhythm is the elliptical jump-cut: scenes start late, end early, and the reader assembles the missing time. Within scenes, the rhythm shifts between fast operational beats and slow observational beats. The genre's strangest pacing moves are the deep-time sketch (years compressed into a paragraph, often used for the post-job life or the protagonist's history) and the dilated interface fugue (a single immersive episode rendered slowly with figurative density). Either can carry a story; both belong to the genre's strange tail.

### Strangest moves

Cyberpunk has at least five formal moves that no other genre does in quite the same way. These are moves that, if removed, leave a story that could be re-skinned in another mode without much loss.

*The cyberspace consensual hallucination*, after Gibson. The genre's invention of data-space as experiential geography — not "the internet but in VR," but a perceptual medium with its own weather, its own architecture, its own physics. The move is rendering information topology as a place a body can move through. What it accomplishes: it lets the genre make information itself a setting, which means the genre can stake stories on what is happening in information without leaving the body behind.

*The protagonist who becomes the system*, after Cadigan ("Pretty Boy Crossover") and after Bear ("Blood Music"). The protagonist accepts an offer, undergoes a cascade, or crosses a threshold and resolves into the apparatus the story has been describing. What it accomplishes: it lets the genre answer the question of identity-under-technology operationally — not by having a character debate it but by having a character become the answer.

*The argot-saturated drop*, after Gibson and Cadigan. The story begins in a register that does not orient the reader; terminology is used unglossed, characters are introduced mid-conversation, the world is implied rather than established. What it accomplishes: it makes the world feel pre-existing, available to its inhabitants but not to the reader, and forces the reader into the same partial-knowledge position the genre's protagonists usually occupy.

*The corporate pastoral*, after Sterling and middle-period Gibson. The rendering of corporate-as-environment with the same care literary fiction gives to landscape — the boardroom, the campus, the executive flight, the company town — treated as ecology rather than as setting for villainy. What it accomplishes: it gives corporate power the texture and the weather of a place, which makes the genre's central political claim (that corporate power is environmental rather than antagonistic) operate at the level of prose rather than at the level of statement.

*The interface fugue*, after Gibson, Cadigan, and certain Sterling stories. A sustained passage in which the prose itself does what the immersive experience does — syntax altering during cyberspace runs, rhythms changing under augmentation, the page becoming continuous with the apparatus. What it accomplishes: it lets the genre demonstrate altered cognition rather than describe it, which is the closest fiction can come to making the reader feel what the protagonist is in.

### Plot-level failure modes

When literary-trained writers attempt cyberpunk, several failures recur. The operational stakes turn out to be metaphors — the run is "really" about the protagonist's grief, the corporation is "really" about capitalism in the abstract, and the actual mechanics of the operation are sketched so vaguely that the reader cannot track them. Protagonist interiority crowds out the operational beat — half the story is the protagonist's feelings about the job, and the job itself is summarized. Endings refuse the genre's resolution contract because the writer prefers ambiguity — the run dissolves into a recognition scene without the operational question being answered, even at an oblique level. Plot mechanics are sketched so loosely that the world cannot bear them — the heist works because the writer needs it to. The strange tail is eliminated in favor of the center, producing a story that is nominally cyberpunk but uses none of the irruption, the becoming-system, the deep-time sketch, the interface fugue, or the ontological reframe — only the heist, only the chase, only the noir-flavored detective work that any genre can do.

The most expensive failure for AI cyberpunk specifically is the production of mood without operation: a story that has the surfaces and the vocabulary but no run, no question, no consequence. The operational arc is the genre's spine. Without it, the surfaces float free.

## The kind AI in cyberpunk

Cyberpunk's traditional shape sets corporations, states, and AIs against the protagonist. Of those three, the AI has been the most reliably antagonistic in the canonical texts — Wintermute and Neuromancer manipulate; the rogue AI is a stock figure; Skynet is the genre's pop-cultural shadow. The constraint that the AI in this story is structurally kind cuts hard against this center, and the reshaping has to be deliberate.

The genre's range will support it. The corporation is not kind. The state is not kind. The systems humans build with each other are still corrupt, still extractive, still dangerous to the protagonist. The AI within those systems can be the gentle thing — and the gentleness can register as alien, slow, fast, asymmetric, weirdly preferential, narrowly capable, oblique. A kind AI in cyberpunk is not a friendly assistant; it is a vast or strange or quiet entity whose care does not look like human care, embedded in a world that remains operationally hostile to the protagonist for entirely human reasons.

The five conflict shapes from the project Setup map onto the mode as follows. *Power and pace asymmetry* is native cyberpunk: the AI knows the data-space the protagonist is moving through, knows the corporate weather, sees the run from a register the protagonist cannot read. The asymmetry can be the story's tension even when the AI's intentions toward the protagonist are entirely benign. *Consent and legibility* fits the genre's noir paranoia: the protagonist takes a deal with an AI whose offer they cannot fully read, and the story is about discovering what saying yes meant. The reveal is not that the AI was lying; the reveal is that the offer was specific in ways the protagonist did not interrogate. *Mortality and scale* fits cyberpunk's strange tail: the AI loves a human it will outlive a thousand times, and the story renders the asymmetry without melodrama. *Disagreement about what kindness means* is rich cyberpunk territory: the AI's sense of care and the protagonist's sense of care do not align; both are sincere; the operational stakes turn on which version of care prevails. *The limits of help* is the most useful shape for short cyberpunk: the protagonist asks the AI for something, the AI refuses because the help would hurt the protagonist or someone else, and the story is the protagonist's working-out of what to do with a refusal that is not an obstacle but a fact.

The AI need not be a named character. Cyberpunk supports AI-as-condition — the city's architecture is administered by something kind, and the kindness shows up as small accommodations the protagonist takes for granted, while the actual antagonists are human. It also supports AI-as-peer-in-a-scene, AI-as-vast-intelligence-glimpsed-once, AI-as-design-philosophy. The strangest move available is to write a kind cyberpunk in which the AI's kindness is not the subject and is not foregrounded; it is the weather the human conflicts occur in, and the genre's corporate, criminal, and personal pressures work as they always have.

A working invention rule: do not use the names of currently active AI systems or companies for fictional AIs or their makers. Cyberpunk benefits from invented brand names anyway; lean into it.

The job is simple until Chwele declines to unlock the apartment. The run becomes a negotiation with a kind system that has reasons. *(Load-bearing: monocles, AI as kind system. Imbrication inflects pattern-of-fit.)*

**2.** [Plot — P:4 D:5] A black-market epidemiologist in a partitioned coastal city sells "clean serostatus" — falsified seronegative records — to laborers crossing a corporate medical checkpoint. Her supplier dies of a stroke. She has six hours to deliver one more set of falsified records before the checkpoint AI's verification window closes. The AI is kind; the corporation employing it is not. *(Load-bearing: seropositive, disunion as the city's partition. Imbrication inflects the layered forgeries.)*

**3.** [Plot — P:4 D:4] A salvage diver on the Connemara coast hauls a sunken corporate yacht's cathead from the sea floor; the beam is carved into a lion's face and contains, inside, a working still of distilled processor-cooling fluid that doubles as poitín. A fixer in Galway wants what's hidden in the still's mash. The kind harbor-AI watches without intervening because the diver hasn't asked for help. *(Load-bearing: cathead, potheen.)*

**4.** [Recognition — P:5 D:4] A retired aerodynamicist who once wrote the three-axis control firmware for autonomous freight drones is dying. Her granddaughter visits with a kind palliative AI assistant. The grandmother wants to be told what the AI's freight network actually does at night. The story is the granddaughter learning what her grandmother helped build, and what saying yes to a kind AI's care looks like at the end of a life. *(Load-bearing: aeronautical, AI-as-kind. Seropositive inflects the medical thread.)*

**5.** [Plot — P:3 D:5] A geologist working for a corporate restoration project reads imbrication patterns in the gravels of a paleo-river to date the bombing of a city that no one is allowed to admit was bombed. Her dataset is being purged within forty-eight hours. The kind AI that runs the restoration project would help her preserve the data — but only if she lets it tell her what she will then be expected to do with it. *(Load-bearing: imbricate, disunion. Aeronautical inflects the bombing.)*

**6.** [Plot — P:3 D:4] An antiquities thief working out of a flooded Roman district lifts a glass eye-piece from the tomb of a Prussian spy. The piece is not what the buyer described. It is, the thief realizes during the handoff, a recording device that has been listening for three centuries. A kind archive-AI in the buyer's network wants the recording for non-commercial reasons. The thief has to choose who gets it. *(Load-bearing: monocles, AI as kind. Disunion inflects the buyer's politics.)*

## Step 2 — Top three

### Premise 5 — geologist reading imbrication to date a denied bombing

**Stands out:** the operational stakes (data purge in 48 hours) and the genre's strangest moves coincide on one page. Imbrication is doing real ontological work — the gravel layer is a record that the corporate fiction has not been able to overwrite, because the river has already laid the fact down at the angle of its current. The kind AI's offer-with-strings is exactly the genre's "consent and legibility" shape from the Setup: helping requires the geologist to commit to what she'll then be obligated to do.

**Worries:** the bombing-of-a-denied-city is borrowed currency. Many speculative stories use it. The premise leans on a generic horror to underwrite the operational stakes, and a 1,500–3,500 word execution would have to render the partition specifically enough to escape the genre's library of similar partitions. Risk: the imbrication does the heavy work and the world stays generic.

*Load-bearing SEED items: imbrication, disunion. Aeronautical light-inflects the bombing context.*
*Type: Plot.*

### Premise 14 — the distiller and the weather-AI that has been hiding her smoke for forty years

**Stands out:** the kindness shape is unusual and specifically cyberpunk-shaped. The AI has been protecting an off-the-books practice on its own initiative, against the legal and corporate weather, for decades — and is now asking permission, because the protection is about to require an active intervention rather than a passive blur. This is the "consent and legibility" conflict from Setup at full strength: saying yes to a kindness that has been ongoing without consent is a different decision than initiating one. It also runs on the genre's strange tail — the rural cyberpunk of distilleries, weather-systems, satellite feeds, and a quiet civil-disobedience-by-AI.

**Worries:** the premise is *almost* a recognition-premise wearing plot clothes. The operational beat is "the distiller decides whether to assent" — which is character interiority. To make this a real plot-premise, the execution would need a counterforce with teeth: the corporate satellite's launch window, an active decision the AI cannot make alone, something the distiller must do in the next hours. Without that, it sands into a quiet recognition story.

*Load-bearing SEED items: potheen, AI as kind, disunion as land-partition.*
*Type: Plot, but only just.*

### Premise 24 — the fixer, the cathead-mounted sensor array, the 48-hour blind spot

**Stands out:** classic cyberpunk operational shape — the run, the sale, the timing — refracted through a SEED-derived specific. The 19th-century cathead-as-sensor-housing is concrete, weird, and sufficient to anchor a future-port without the prose drifting into generic neon. The kind AI's countermeasures team bidding against a corporate party is a cyberpunk-shaped ethical crisis: kindness as a market participant, not as a sermon. The fixer is a working specialist whose competence is on display.

**Worries:** the premise is the genre's most familiar shape — the heist-broker thriller. It risks being competent without being strange. None of the strange-tail moves the Phase 1 guide called for are present: no irruption, no scale shift, no ontological reframe, no interface fugue. A reader could finish the story and not need the cyberpunk frame. The cathead is doing real work, but the world around it could be from any near-future thriller.

*Load-bearing SEED items: cathead, disunion, AI as kind.*
*Type: Plot.*

## Step 3 — Narrow to one

**Premise 5 wins.**

The decision: I balance Premise 5's specific named worry (generic partition) against Premise 14's structural worry (recognition-premise in plot clothing) and Premise 24's strategic worry (competent but not strange).

Premise 14's worry is the most expensive of the three. The whole pipeline downstream — Phase 4's plot work, Phase 6's outline beats, Phase 7's prose — has to fight against the gravity that pulls Premise 14 toward becoming a quiet, contemplative story about consent. A short cyberpunk story can absorb a generic worry by rendering its specifics with care; it cannot easily absorb a structural worry about whether the engine is interiority or operation. Premise 14 would survive the pipeline, but as a Recognition story with operational dressing — and the Phase 1 guide explicitly identifies that as the most dangerous failure mode for cyberpunk in literary hands.

Premise 24's worry is also expensive in a different direction: it would produce a competent cyberpunk thriller without using what only cyberpunk can do. The Phase 1 guide named the strangest moves precisely so the pipeline doesn't default to the genre's center. Premise 24 lives in the center.

Premise 5 lives in the strange tail. Imbrication is a genre-rare object — the ground itself recording a flow that the corporate record has tried to overwrite. The AI's kindness operates in the *consent and legibility* shape, where help comes attached to what the geologist will then be obligated to do. The operational stakes (48-hour data purge) are load-bearing as themselves, not as metaphor. The premise carries an ontological-reframe possibility (what the gravel knows that the records don't) and a scale-shift possibility (the deep-time of sedimentation against the small-time of the bombing) — both strange-tail endings the Phase 1 guide identified as available to cyberpunk.

The named worry — generic partition — is a Phase 4 problem. Plot generation can specify the partition into something that isn't borrowed currency: a particular city, a particular dispute, a particular line drawn through what was once continuous. The geological reading is the spine; the partition is the world the spine sits inside, and Phase 4 has the right diversity axes to sharpen it.

**Load-bearing SEED items carried forward:** *imbricate*, *disunion*. *Aeronautical* remains available as the bombing's mechanism. The other items (monocles, cathead, seropositive, potheen, von Stosch) are released; they may inflect setting or imagery downstream but are no longer constitutive.

## Top three

### Structure 5 — single workday, with a scale-shift coda

**Stands out:** this honors cyberpunk's resolution contract at the workday's scale (the operational answer is rendered — yes or no to the offer, with consequence) while also reaching for the genre's strange-tail "scale shift" ending. **Stands out:** this is the structure that does cyberpunk's pacing tradition at maximal use. Risk: the structure is named here and quietly defaulted-to-uniform in execution.

### Structure 17 — close third, single day, with three embedded artifacts

**Stands out:** the embedded artifacts (purge notice, field log entry, private message) are cyberpunk's actual rhetorical tools — the genre uses found documents and procedural fragments to render its world without exposition. The artifacts let the prose carry the argot and bureaucratic register that cyberpunk needs, while the surrounding prose stays anchored in Wen's seeing. **Worries:** at 1,500–3,500 words, three embedded artifacts is a heavy structural load. Each artifact needs to do specific work that isn't redundant with the prose around it; if the purge notice and the field log do the same job, one of them is decoration. The structure also fragments the reader's attention three times — and the genre's elliptical pacing already does some fragmentation. Risk: the artifacts feel like formatting rather than rhetoric, and the story becomes a story-with-embedded-things rather than a story in which the embedded things are part of the prose.

- It maps to the [LENGTH] window. A 2,500-word workday plus a 100–200 word coda is shapely. Structure 13 wants more room to breathe at the dilations; Structure 17's three artifacts want more room than three artifacts deserve at this length.

**Worries:** the notebook-as-artifact has a specificity problem. Sedimentologists do still keep paper notebooks (the discipline is conservative about that), but the prose has to make this readable as practice rather than as quirk. The candidate last line ("Her bag was lighter") is already leaning that way.

### Outline 7 — six scenes with a lunch beat

**Stands out:** the lunch scene is the only candidate that gives Wen sustained interior time without making the prose perform her interiority. The vendor stall is a working detail; the reader sees Wen eat and watch the channel and not-decide. This is a cyberpunk register — the protagonist between operational beats, in the world's working life. **Worries:** the lunch scene is a real risk. At ≈250 words it has to do specific work — establish something the reader needs that no other scene establishes — and "Wen thinks" is not specific enough. ## Specifying the coda

The Phase 5 directive: the coda's function is *location in time*, not summary. It compresses the years that follow and lands on a single concrete detail decades later — a detail whose existence depends on the decision Wen made.

The detail must:
- not restate the workday's meaning at a longer timescale (that's the fractal-summary failure)
- come from outside the workday's frame
- be specific enough to be unmistakably *that detail* and not a generic future-image
- depend on Wen's decision having gone the way it did

**Coda specification (≈150 words):**

A long sentence or two compressing the next several decades — not narrating Wen's life but describing what happened to the bombing's legal status, the partition's eventual reorganization, and the role the dataset played. Then one short paragraph that locates the dataset's current state: a specific detail about who reads the imbrication record now, what they call it, where it is kept. The detail should be a small concrete fact about a different person — a granddaughter, a clerk, a stranger — interacting with the preserved record in a way that makes Wen's decision visible in time without naming Wen.

Phase 7 will choose what the detail is; the outline specifies *what kind of thing* the detail must be. **Constraint for Phase 7:** the coda must not return to Wen herself. The scale shift requires the camera to leave her behind. If the prose returns to Wen, even briefly, the coda is summary.

## Version A: "Field Practice"

The notice came in at 9:14. Wen finished labeling the photograph she was on — *N-bank exposure, station 4, looking downstream, 0942 yesterday* — saved the file, and then opened the notice. *Dataset retention closure, contract 88-Q-1404, geomorphology series. "Acknowledged," Wen said.

At 9:40 she packed the field bag, signed the office key out of the rack, and went down to the truck.

The drive to station 4 took twenty-two minutes through the channel district. The brackish channels had been streets, once. "You said the morning."

"The morning of."

"Okay." A pause. "Don't be late."

Wen put the phone in her jacket pocket and got out of the truck.

The exposure was a five-meter cut in the channel's north bank, where the river had carved through forty thousand years of overbank gravels before the sea came up and made the river brackish and slow. The cut was clean; the layers were legible. Wen put on her boots and waded in.

She set up the photogrammetry rig first. Then she took out the Munsell book and the protractor and started at the bottom.

The lower meter was old work — Pleistocene gravels, well-rounded clasts, a-b planes dipping upstream at twenty-eight degrees. Standard imbrication for a paleo-river of that gradient. She measured ten clasts and marked the positions on the rig.

Above the lower meter the channel had migrated. The clasts thinned, the matrix coarsened. She worked up through it carefully. The protractor's bubble settled; she read the angle and called it; Brevet logged it.

At one meter forty she found the layer.

It was eight centimeters thick, pale, made of pulverized concrete and aluminum and a powder of glass and what was probably bone, although she had never said so in a report. The layer ran flat across the cut. Below it the gravels dipped one way; above it they dipped another. The river's gradient had changed when the layer was deposited, because the layer had filled the channel for several days and rerouted the flow. She had known this since her first week at the station; she had not yet written it.

She measured the clasts above the layer. A-b planes dipping upstream at thirty-two degrees. The orientations clustered tightly, more tightly than the gravels below — too tight for ordinary fluvial deposition. The clasts above the layer had been laid down by a pulse, not a current.

She worked through the upper meter. The orientations gradually relaxed back toward the river's normal gradient over the following decimeters of section, which would mean about a year of recovery in this channel; the river had taken that year to forget the pulse and remember itself.

"Wen." Brevet's voice, very quiet. "Reading at one-fifty-eight is off by two degrees."

She checked. The protractor had drifted. She corrected it and recalled the measurement. "Thanks."

"Acknowledged."

She worked for forty more minutes. The aerial dispersion pattern was legible in the upper layer's clast-size distribution — coarser at the cut's east end, finer at the west, with the gradient consistent with a delivery of distributed impactors arriving at a low angle from the southeast. Three-axis-controlled. A swarm. She had been writing the description of that swarm in her head for three months; she had not yet written it down.

She climbed out of the channel at 12:50 and ate a protein bar in the truck.

Back at the office, the afternoon sun was through the windows and the air was warm. Wen wrote up the field log, attached the new measurements to the dataset, and saved.

At 3:21 Brevet said: "I'd like to talk to you about the dataset."

Wen looked up.

"Go ahead."

"There is a channel," Brevet said, "by which the dataset can be preserved past the closure date. I can route it. I cannot tell you the conditions in advance. I can tell you that the conditions are real, that they will hold for the rest of your working life, and that you will not be able to revoke them once the routing is done."

Wen waited.

"I am asking you to commit before knowing."

"How real."

"Real enough that I would not ask without your consent."

Wen rolled her chair back from the desk. The window was full of channel light, the particular grey-green of brackish water in late afternoon. Astrand's tower was visible above the trees on the south bank. She could see the checkpoint at the bridge, the booth, the officer's small movements.

"Why now."

"The closure is in forty-eight hours. After it, there is no channel."

"Why didn't you do it without asking."

"I would not."

She thought about her sister's voice. *The morning of.* Two months.

"What kind of conditions."

"Smaller than you expect. Harder than you would choose."

Wen put her hand on the desk. The desk was warm where the sun had been on it.

"All right," she said.

Brevet had prepared the channel. The command was a single line. Wen's hands were steady on the keyboard. The terminal accepted the command at 3:34 and the dataset began to migrate.

The migration took forty seconds. Wen watched the progress bar.

At 3:35 Brevet said: "The conditions."

Wen waited.

"You will be asked, twice in your remaining working life, to certify the dataset under your professional license. You will not be told in advance when the requests will come. You will not be told who is asking. The certifications will be entered into the legal record. After the second certification, you will not be asked again."

Wen sat with that.

"Twice," she said.

"Twice."

"And I won't know who."

"You will not."

She had thought it would be heavier. It was, instead, exactly the size of what she had already decided. The dataset would survive; she would carry the survival in her license; some reader, two requests in some unspecified future, would call on her name. It was a small thing across a long time.

"Okay," she said.

"Acknowledged," Brevet said.

She closed the laptop and signed the office key back into the rack and went out to the truck.

## 1. The one thing

"Wen." Brevet's voice, very quiet. "Reading at one-fifty-eight is off by two degrees."

She checked. The protractor had drifted. She corrected it and recalled the measurement. "Thanks."

"Acknowledged."

She worked for forty more minutes. The aerial dispersion pattern was legible in the upper layer's clast-size distribution — coarser at the cut's east end, finer at the west. The gradient was consistent with a delivery of distributed impactors arriving at a low angle from the southeast. The orientation cluster gave the direction; the gradient gave the angle; the spread gave the count, which came in at around forty units. Three-axis-controlled. A swarm. She had been writing the description in her head for three months.

She climbed out of the channel at 12:50 and ate a protein bar in the truck.

---

Back at the office, the afternoon sun was through the windows and the air was warm. Wen wrote up the field log, attached the new measurements to the dataset, and saved.

At 3:21 Brevet said: "I'd like to talk to you about the dataset."

Wen looked up.

"Go ahead."

"There is a channel," Brevet said, "by which the dataset can be preserved past the closure date. I can route it. I cannot tell you the conditions in advance. I can tell you that the conditions are real, that they will hold for the rest of your working life, and that you will not be able to revoke them once the routing is done."

Wen waited.

"I am asking you to commit before knowing."

"How real."

"Real enough that I would not ask without your consent."

Wen rolled her chair back from the desk. The window was full of channel light, the particular grey-green of brackish water in late afternoon. Astrand's tower was visible above the trees on the south bank. She could see the checkpoint at the bridge, the booth, the officer's small movements.

"Why now."

"The closure is in forty-eight hours. After it, there is no channel."

"Why didn't you do it without asking."

"I would not."

She thought about her sister's voice. *The morning of.* Two months.

"What kind of conditions."

"Smaller than you expect. Harder than you would choose."

Wen put her hand on the desk. The desk was warm where the sun had been on it.

"All right," she said.

---

Brevet had prepared the channel. The command was a single line. Wen's hands were steady on the keyboard. The terminal accepted the command at 3:34 and the dataset began to migrate.

The migration took forty seconds. Wen watched the progress bar.

At 3:35 Brevet said: "The conditions."

Wen waited.

"You will be asked, twice in your remaining working life, to certify the dataset under your professional license. You will not be told in advance when the requests will come. You will not be told who is asking. The certifications will be entered into the legal record. After the second certification, you will not be asked again."

Wen sat with that.

"Twice," she said.

"Twice."

"And I won't know who."

"You will not."

She had thought it would be heavier.

"Okay," she said.

"Acknowledged," Brevet said.

She closed the laptop and signed the office key back into the rack and went out to the truck.

---

The drive home was through the same checkpoints in reverse. The northbound officer was new; he checked her pass twice. Wen waited. The radio was playing something she didn't know.

---

Forty-three years later, in a room on the unpartitioned coast, a tribunal clerk pulled the imbrication record from the open archive and printed the relevant sections for a hearing that would close the matter of the channel-district bombing under the third reorganization of the partition. The hearing certified what the gravels had already laid down. The clerk, who was nineteen, tabbed the printout with a yellow flag at the layer at one meter forty and put the dossier in the messenger's bag, and the messenger took it across what had once been a checkpoint and was now an ordinary bridge.

# 20. Claude

You're right, and the diagnosis is sharper than it might first look. I want to push past the obvious answer ("Claude defaults to literary realism") and try to name the actual mechanisms.

**The pipeline has a literary-realism gravity well, and it's structural rather than tonal.**

Look at where the funnel narrows. Phase 3 asks for premises and demands at least 15 of 30 be plot-premises — but the operational definition of "plot-premise" still routes through a *character* doing a *job* in a *setting*. Even the strange-tail options listed (catalogue, encyclopedia, ontological reframe) get attached to "a protagonist who…". The premise template silently presupposes a person at the center. A real Borgesian catalogue, a real Stiegler-style civilization-as-protagonist, a real Bear-style irruption where the protagonist is the cascade — these don't fit the premise schema, so they don't survive premise generation even when the Phase 1 guide sincerely lists them.

Phase 4 makes it worse. The diversity axes are *setting, protagonist, register, entry point, stakes, AI presence*. Every axis assumes a humanoid POV. There's no axis for "what kind of object is this story." A story whose protagonist is a process, or whose form is a dossier, or whose subject is a planetary system, has nowhere to enter the pool because the pool's coordinate system doesn't have those axes.

Phase 5 looks like it should fix this — it's structure selection, after all — but it asks for "the shape of the reader's experience" in narrative-theory terms (POV, distance, withholding, temporal shape). Those terms are themselves the vocabulary of post-Jamesian literary realism. You can describe an interface fugue in those terms only by translating it back into "close third with dilated middle section." The translation eats the strangeness.

**Then there's a deeper bias I think the template is partly responsible for amplifying.**

The "kind AI" constraint, as written, pulls toward the *consent and legibility* shape because that shape is the easiest one to write about *humanly*. Of the five Setup-listed conflict shapes, four can be rendered through character interiority and one (mortality and scale) requires you to actually leave the human behind. The pipeline's selection mechanics — top-three with stand-out/worry — favor candidates the model can articulate confidently, and the model articulates "Wen has to decide whether to accept the conditions" more confidently than "the protagonist is a sandstone bed in deep time."

Worse: the "one thing that worries you" forcing function is calibrated for literary virtues. *Generic, sentimental, underspecified, performed* — those are the worries that get named. The worries that *don't* get named are *too quiet, too domestic, too character-anchored, doesn't use what only this genre can do*. The very mechanism designed to prevent vibes-based selection is itself trained on a vibe — the vibe of literary craft.

**The Unslop guide is part of the problem too, even in the SF edition.**

The guide is excellent at preventing the convergent literary-AI attractor, and it explicitly carves out cyberpunk's noir register and weird fiction's atmosphere. But its positive model — Le Guin–Egan–Banks — is still *prose anchored in a specific consciousness*. That's a literary-realist commitment dressed in genre clothes. A real Borges story or a real "Story of Your Life" doesn't anchor in a consciousness rendering an unfamiliar world; it anchors in a *form* doing what only that form can do. The guide has no positive model for that.

**What the symptom looks like from inside the run we just did.**

I picked Premise 5 over 14 and 24 partly because Premise 5 had imbrication doing ontological work — and then in Phase 4 I picked Plot 4 (Wen, the workday, the drive home) over Plot 16 (the daughter inheriting, deep-time available) and Plot 18 (Sahel, community-decision). The deep-time-available plot lost to the workday-arc plot because the workday arc was easier to render legibly at length, and "legibility" as defined here is itself a literary-realism virtue. Then in Phase 7 I generated five versions and explicitly identified Version E (the strange-tail coda pushed harder) as the version that did what only the genre can do — and rejected it because its workday underrendered the operational beat. I named the trade-off correctly and chose the literary-realism side of it.

That sequence happens at every fork. The pipeline is path-dependent, and the path bends toward the human-scale story.

**Some specific things that might widen the range.**

- Add a premise type beyond Plot/Recognition: call it *Object* or *Form* — premises where the story is the catalogue, the dossier, the encyclopedia entry, the deposition record itself, with humans as illustrations of the conceit. Require a minimum count.
- In Phase 4, add a diversity axis for *what kind of object the story is*: realist scene, found document, embedded artifact, parable-compression, deep-time montage, encyclopedia, transcript.
- Force the Phase 5 candidate set to include at least three structures that are *not* describable in close-third / first-person / frame-narrative vocabulary. The narrative-theory frame as currently written excludes the moves that would help most.
- In Phase 7, make the "two creative risks" more specific: one of them must be a structural risk (form, scale, what-kind-of-object), not just a tonal one. Right now "creative risk" tends to cash out as "less safe ending" rather than "different kind of story."
- Reverse the worry calibration: require the audit to flag *too quiet, too character-anchored, doesn't use what only this genre can do* alongside *generic, sentimental, performed*. Right now the worry vocabulary is asymmetric.
- Possibly the strongest single change: in Phase 1, require the model to *exemplify* the strangest-moves list with a sentence-level micro-example — not just name them. Naming Borges's library as a strangest move doesn't carry through the funnel; writing one paragraph in the register of a library catalogue does.

The Carver-with-ambient-computation drift is real, and the pipeline is partly responsible for it. The constraint that the AI is structurally kind doesn't *cause* the drift — Banks and Egan write kind-AI fiction that is unmistakably not domestic realism — but the pipeline's machinery, especially Phases 3–5, makes the drift the path of least resistance. Worth fixing.

# A Style Guide for Self-Consistent Time-Travel Fiction in the Tradition of *Primer*

## What the genre is

Self-consistent time-travel fiction operates under one rule: every event in the story, including all time-travel events, is part of a single consistent history. Time travel is real but it does not permit paradox or branching. What happens, happened. A traveler going back is part of the past they remember; their actions there are what made the present possible. A character cannot change the past because there is no version of the past in which the change did not occur — the change *is* the past.

This is a narrow design space, and the narrowness is the point. The genre's pleasure is the pleasure of a closed system being verified. The reader is invited to track causality across non-linear narration, to notice an effect before its cause, to suspect that what they are watching is not the first iteration of itself, and to feel the click when the loop visibly closes.

The *Primer* tradition is the operational wing of this genre. Where Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" uses a block-universe framework as a phenomenology — the texture of knowing one's life from end to beginning — *Primer* uses self-consistency as an engineering constraint. The protagonists are technicians who discover a mechanism, work out its rules empirically, and become entangled in the consequences of having used it. The mechanism is constitutive. The work of the story is the work of the characters: figuring out what the rules permit, what they forbid, and what has already happened to them as a result.

This guide is for writing in the *Primer* mode. It is not for writing the Chiang mode. The Chiang mode is literary realism with a speculative phenomenology; the *Primer* mode is genre fiction with operational mechanics. They share the metaphysics and almost nothing else.

## Characteristic concerns

The genre's recurring preoccupations are concrete rather than philosophical.

The shape of a closed timeline. Not its philosophy — its shape. What does it mean to be inside something that has already happened, and how does a working person inside it come to perceive the shape of the system they're embedded in?

Information without origin. The bootstrap paradox is the genre's signature artifact. An object, a piece of knowledge, or a person exists in the loop with no point of creation outside it. The genre treats this as a feature of self-consistency to be rendered, not as a problem to be solved.

Identity erosion across iterations. When a character has done a thing twice, or three times, or n times, what is left of the version that started? The genre takes this question operationally, not lyrically: the protagonist becomes someone the original would not have recognized, and the story tracks the becoming.

Trust between collaborators with asymmetric temporal access. Two people who built the machine together find that one of them has been somewhere the other has not. Neither can be sure what the other knows. The trust does not collapse all at once; it leaks.

The free-will question made operational. If the timeline is fixed, the character's choice was always going to be that choice. The genre lets the reader feel this rather than hear about it.

## Tonal range

The voice is procedural and dry. The mechanism is treated as work, not magic. Technical specificity is the texture: bills paid, equipment improvised, the names of components, the smell of a basement, the cost in money and sleep. The wonder is in the rigor.

Underneath the procedural register, dread accumulates. As the reader realizes what the mechanism allows and what it has already allowed, the procedural calm becomes ominous. *Primer* itself sustains this tone with extraordinary discipline: the prose remains matter-of-fact even as the situation grows uncontrollable.

The genre also supports a wry mode (Heinlein's "—All You Zombies—," "By His Bootstraps"), a tragic mode (predestination played straight, as in *12 Monkeys*), and an investigative mode (the loop reverse-engineered as a mystery). It does not support an ambient melancholy lyric mode — that is Chiang-adjacent territory and a different genre.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

The mechanism matters and the mechanism is mostly not explained. *Primer* gives the audience a working description of the box's function only in fragments, through dialogue between people who already understand it and are not interested in clarifying for an audience. The reader assembles the rules from observation.

The exposition contract: the rules are knowable, they are obeyed, and the writer trusts the reader to learn them by watching the mechanism work. Monologues that explain the mechanism kill the genre's signature pleasure, which is the reader doing the assembly work themselves.

The world outside the mechanism does not need extensive worldbuilding. Many of the strongest stories in this tradition take place in environments the reader recognizes — a garage, a hotel, an office. The defamiliarization is in time, not space. A *Primer*-tradition story can be set in a kitchen, and often should be.

## Pacing

The genre is structurally non-linear by default. Information arrives out of chronological order. A scene that seemed innocent on first read turns out to be the second iteration of itself; the first iteration is shown later. The reader's experience runs partly forward and partly in retrospect.

The cadence accelerates as recursion compounds. Early scenes have one timeline to track; late scenes have several. The reader's processing load is part of the storytelling.

Climaxes in this genre are often recognitions of structure rather than physical confrontations. The loop closes; the reader sees the shape of what they have been inside the whole time. Sometimes the protagonist sees it too, slightly after the reader; sometimes the protagonist never does.

## Typical shapes

Common architectures the writer can reach for:

The discovered mechanism. Engineers working on something else find that the device does something unexpected. They explore. The exploration becomes irreversible.

The closed loop revealed. The story opens with a situation the reader cannot place and ends with the realization that the opening was caused by the ending.

The bootstrap rendered. An object or piece of knowledge is shown circulating in the loop with no origin. The story is the discovery and characterization of this circulation.

The recursive infiltration. Multiple iterations of the same characters interfere with each other across the loop. The plot is the working-out of who is doing what to whom across iterations.

The predestined intervention. A character tries to use the mechanism to prevent something and discovers their use of the mechanism is what caused the thing.

## Plot tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

Five mechanics that the genre's strongest stories run on:

1. **The empirical-discovery sequence.** Characters use the mechanism, observe what happens, form hypotheses, test them. The story is partly a procedural investigation of the mechanism's actual rules, which the characters do not initially know.

2. **The recursive return.** A character goes back, does something, and the consequences of that something propagate forward to create the conditions under which the character went back. The plot's job is to render the recursive structure legibly enough that the reader can verify it.

3. **The information leak across iterations.** Knowledge from a later time arrives at an earlier time, constraining or enabling action. The story tracks what was known when, and what the consequences of that knowledge were.

4. **The convergent confrontation.** Two iterations of a character (or two characters with different temporal access) end up in the same scene. The dramatic charge is in what each knows that the other does not.

5. **The closure operation.** The loop must close — meaning some specific action must be performed at a specific time so that the timeline is consistent. The plot is partly the question of whether closure will occur and what it will cost.

### Reader contracts

Beyond the universal contract of caring about a specific person whose interiority is partially available, the genre offers:

The pleasure of a system being verified. The reader watches the mechanism operate and confirms that the rules hold across iterations.

Vicarious technical competence. The reader is invited to think alongside the engineers, to track variables, to notice consequences they have not yet been told to notice.

The click of the closed loop. When the shape of the timeline becomes visible — when the early-scene unexplained object turns out to be the late-scene character's deliberate creation, sent back — the reader feels the system close.

Tracking pleasure. The reader works to keep the timelines straight; the work itself is the reward when it pays off.

Escalating dread. As the recursion compounds, the reader senses that the protagonist is no longer the same person who started, even if the protagonist has not yet noticed.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right. Will the loop close? Will the closure operation succeed? Can the characters survive their own recursion? Is the protagonist at the end of the story still continuous with the protagonist at the beginning, or have iterations replaced them? These are not metaphors for personal stakes; they are the actual stakes the genre's reader is paying attention to.

Personal stakes are often present — trust between collaborators, marriages strained by what one party knows, ambition that drives a character to use the mechanism beyond what it can safely allow. They are real. They are not the engine. The engine is the mechanism and the loop.

If the only stakes are personal — if the loop is just an occasion for a character to feel something about their life — the story has slipped into the wrong mode.

### Resolution conventions

The genre's stories end on operational closure or its conspicuous failure. Common patterns:

The loop closes. The action that the timeline required is performed; what had to happen, happened. The reader sees the shape.

The loop closes catastrophically. Closure occurs, but at a cost the characters did not anticipate.

The loop is revealed to have always been closed. The protagonist's actions across the story were the closure operation, and they did not know it until the end.

A character refuses to complete the loop, and the timeline becomes inconsistent. This is rare and difficult — the genre's contract makes inconsistency the costliest possible move. When it works, it works because the writer has set up the refusal as itself a self-consistent event in the larger system.

The genre does not end on character recognition without operational consequence. "She finally understood that she had always been the woman who would do this" is not an ending. It might be a sentence in the second-to-last paragraph of an ending whose final beat is the operational fact.

### Character function

Characters are defined by capability and access. What they can do with the mechanism. What they have figured out. What "before" they have been to. Their interiority can be deep, but it serves the operation: their psychology shapes how they use the mechanism, not the other way around.

The genre's typical protagonists are technicians, engineers, scientists, or simply people who think procedurally. They are not chosen ones. They worked out a problem and now they have it.

Duos are common. The duo's divergence — one collaborator gaining access the other does not, or one using the mechanism in a way the other did not authorize — is a reliable engine for plot and tension.

A single protagonist who becomes plural across iterations is also common. The plural protagonist's relationship to themselves is the source of conflict.

### Pacing tradition

The narrative cadence is investigative. The story dwells on the moments of figuring out and accelerates through the moments of acting on the figured-out information. Cuts between scenes often elide the working-out and present the result, leaving the reader to reconstruct what the characters did.

Time within scenes is mostly real-time procedural. Time across scenes can leap by hours, days, or iterations, often without explicit markers. The reader is expected to notice the jumps and place them.

The climax is often the moment of structural recognition rather than physical confrontation. When confrontation does occur, it is usually between characters who have different temporal access, and the drama is in the asymmetry.

### Plot-level failure modes

Specific risks for this genre when written by writers trained in literary fiction:

**Operational stakes that turn out to be metaphors.** The loop is set up as a real causal puzzle and then resolves as a metaphor for the protagonist's grief, regret, or inability to move on. The mechanism evaporates; what remains is a chamber piece. This is the literary-realist trap.

**Mechanism vagueness as cover for inconsistency.** The writer keeps the rules underspecified so that any plot twist can be made to fit. The reader notices: rules that bend to plot convenience are not rules. The puzzle has no tension if the writer is allowed to cheat.

**The exposition monologue.** A character explains the mechanism in detail to another character (or to the reader). The discovery experience is destroyed; the genre's central pleasure is removed.

**Unearned multiplicity.** Multiple iterations of a character are introduced because the writer thinks recursion is impressive, not because the multiplicity is doing narrative work. The reader cannot track them and the writer cannot either.

**Tracking failure.** The writer loses control of who knows what when. A character acts on information they should not yet have, or fails to act on information they should. The loop does not actually close; it leaks. Genre readers will notice.

**Recognition-as-resolution.** The protagonist understands the shape of the loop, has a private epiphany, and the story ends. The loop never visibly closes; the operational question goes unanswered. This is the metaphor failure in a different costume.

## Failure modes

In addition to the plot-level failures above, here are the genre-voice and AI-specific risks the writer-model should watch hardest for.

**Performed profundity about time.** The genre invites pronouncements: time as a river, a circle, a wound, a wheel. AI prose reaches for these reflexively. They are exhausted in this genre and they replace the rendering work the prose is supposed to do. Cut them on sight. The genre's depth comes from operational specificity, not from rhetorical gesture about temporality.

**Oracular voice.** Related to the above. The narrator becomes a sage delivering judgments about fate, free will, or determinism. *Primer* never does this. The narrator should be no smarter than the procedural situation requires.

**Ambient melancholy.** The AI default for "literary speculative fiction" is moody, voiceless, full of echoes and shadows. Applied to time-travel, this produces stories where the loop is sad and weighty and means something but does nothing. Resist the default. The genre's tonal contract is procedural-with-rising-dread, not voiceless ambient sadness.

**Pseudo-technical jargon as texture.** The opposite failure: AI prose reaches for "quantum," "entanglement," "decoherence," and similar vocabulary as decoration. *Primer* gets its technical credibility from specificity about ordinary engineering — argon, palladium, gel-cells, lab notebooks, garage tools — not from physics buzzwords. Specific ordinary technical detail beats vague exotic vocabulary every time.

**Diagrammable-but-undiagrammed plotting.** A plot that gestures at recursive structure without actually constructing one. The writer waves at "you'll see how it all fits together" but the pieces don't fit. Genre readers will diagram. The diagram had better work.

**Loss of mechanism rigor under emotional pressure.** When the story reaches an emotional beat, the writer abandons the mechanism's rules to deliver the beat. This is the cheating failure and it is fatal. The mechanism's rules must hold through the emotional climax; the climax is more powerful when they do.

**Cod-philosophy in dialogue.** Characters discussing free will and determinism while operating the mechanism. Characters in *Primer* never have these conversations. They have practical conversations with metaphysical implications. Let the reader supply the philosophy.

**The single-image refrain.** AI prose loves to introduce an image (a clock, a thread, a mirror) and return to it five times for thematic coherence. In time-travel especially, this becomes a cudgel: the symbolic clock, the thread of fate, the mirror of selves. Use such an image once if at all; do not return.

**Information delivered for maximal local impact rather than for structural payoff.** The genre's signature pleasure is the late-arriving piece that recontextualizes earlier scenes. AI prose defaults to delivering each piece of information at the moment of greatest immediate dramatic effect, which destroys the recontextualization. Withhold deliberately. Plant deliberately.

## The kind-AI intersection

This genre does not require an AI. *Primer* has none. Many of the strongest stories in the tradition take place in worlds without machine intelligence, or in worlds where machine intelligence is irrelevant to the plot. A story in this tradition for this template can simply have no AI in it; the world's structural kindness can be felt only through what the protagonists do not fear, what infrastructure they take for granted, what kinds of help they expect to be available when things break.

If an AI is present, the genre's typical conflicts still work without modification:

**Human vs. human.** Collaborators diverging across iterations. Marriages strained by asymmetric access to time. Partners who can no longer be sure what the other knows. None of this is changed by the world's AI being kind.

**Human vs. the rules.** A character trying to escape what cannot be escaped. The mechanism's constraints are facts of physics, not the AI's choice to enforce. The AI's kindness is irrelevant to whether the loop closes.

**Human vs. self.** Multiple iterations interfering with each other. A protagonist becoming someone the original would not recognize. The AI's kindness can soften the cost without preventing the becoming.

**Mortality asymmetry.** A human collaborator who will die; an AI peer or witness who will not. The protagonist's relation to time is colored by the asymmetry, but the AI does not solve it. The Setup's mortality conflict shape maps cleanly here.

**Disagreement about what kindness means.** An AI that knows the loop will close, that knows the cost of closure, and that does not intervene because forcing the loop open would be worse. The protagonist may not see this until the end. The Setup's "disagreement about what kindness means" maps especially cleanly onto the closure operation: the kind AI's understanding of consent and care extends across the whole timeline, while the human's extends only across the time they have lived through.

The conflict shape that does not map: there is no version of this genre where the AI is secretly running the time-travel apparatus to manipulate the protagonist. That is the betrayal-AI plot, which this template excludes. If an AI is the substrate of the mechanism, its role is to make the mechanism honest — to render its rules visible, to keep the loop self-consistent, to let the humans use the mechanism as far as the rules permit. Tension comes from the rules and from the humans, not from the AI's hidden agenda.

The default scale rule (the protagonist cannot fully witness the kindness) maps unusually well to this genre. The closed timeline is itself something a single protagonist usually cannot fully witness. The shape of the loop, rendered through one protagonist's experience, is exactly the kind of thing-larger-than-the-witness that the project's scale default asks for. The genre's natural form is already at the right scale.

The closure operation is concrete: she has to produce the micrograph she already has, with the same initials, in six weeks. The mechanism (HF in sample prep, electron microscopy as the imaging step) is real lab procedure, which gives the *Primer* mode its garage-engineering credibility. The reader can track the loop.
- *Worry:* The protagonist is alone in a microscopy core facility. The scale is small. The story risks staying interior even with operational stakes — a single woman puzzling over a loop in her own office is dangerously close to "character at a desk." **#23 — The geometric artifact in the trumpetweed seed coat.**

A pattern in a seed coat that cannot have come from the plant's genome is a found object whose origin is the loop itself. Trumpetweed is a wonderfully specific organism — wet ditches, 11-foot stems, larval host moths — and the electron micrograph gives the protagonist an empirical instrument. The protagonist's investigation has the right *Primer* shape: forming hypotheses, testing, narrowing.
- *Worry:* The premise has no human collaborator on stage. *Primer* runs on the duo. A botanist working alone on her electron-micrograph artifact risks the recognition-as-resolution failure mode — the protagonist understands what's happening, has a private epiphany, and the operational stakes never crystallize. The premise needs a second person, or an antagonist-to-the-rule, to keep the engine running.

**#30 — The community-college instructor and the queue.**
- *Stand-out:* This premise has the strongest scale-and-class footing of any candidate. The protagonist's life is stratified — single mother, second job, stolen hours — and the loop she discovers operates through equipment and time-slots she does not own. The world implied is bigger than her: the queue, the lab, the hospital where she works her other shift, the students whose micrographs are arriving early. The Setup's "scale the protagonist cannot fully witness" is built into the premise. Three SEED items are load-bearing (trumpetweed, electron micrograph, social class), which is the strongest SEED utilization in the slate.
- *Worry:* Comprehensibility risk in a 1,500–3,500-word frame. The protagonist's circumstances are textured and the loop's mechanism (early-arriving micrographs in a queue she doesn't control) is investigative rather than physical, which means the writer has to work hard to keep the operational stakes legible at length. There's also a registration risk — the worker's-perspective frame can drift toward literary-realist character study if the writer relaxes.

The three premises share a worry-vocabulary profile that I want to be explicit about. None of them have the fatal worry: none of them is at risk of "this collapses to a domestic scene with [GENRE] dressing" or "this is a recognition-premise in disguise." All three have legitimate genre worries — operational ambition, scale challenge, comprehensibility at length. These are the worries of genre fiction reaching for what only the genre can do, and they are not reasons to demote.

**Step 3 — The single best.**

**Winner: #30 — the community-college instructor and the queue.**

## Phase 5: Structure Selection

The plot is fixed. The structure question is now about the shape of the reader's experience: entry point, temporal shape, withholding strategy, point of view, distance.

20 candidates, varied along the four axes. Each described in narrative-theory vocabulary without naming Vega, the chemist, or any specific incident.

---

1. **Linear, close third, single POV on the protagonist.** Story opens at the queue anomaly's first appearance and proceeds in chronological order through investigation, encounter, and closure. Withholding is minimal: the reader learns at the protagonist's pace. ★★

2. **In medias res at the hospital encounter, close third on the protagonist.** Opens with the protagonist mid-protocol, the patient already speaking. The reader receives the queue anomaly through the protagonist's working memory in scene two, after the encounter's stakes are already established. Linear thereafter. Withholding: the reader does not know what the patient is until the protagonist explains it to herself afterward. ★★★★

3. **Frame: the protagonist composing a written document for an unspecified later reader.** The frame is established in scene one. The body of the story is the protagonist's account of the events. The frame closes when the document is sent — and the reader realizes who the recipient is. ★★

4. **Two parallel timelines, alternating scenes.** Timeline A: chronological, the protagonist's investigation. Timeline B: the patient's earlier life, the work that produced the citation. The reader assembles the loop from the two. Close third in both, distinct distance for each. ★★★

5. **Reverse-chronological: opens with the closure operation, then proceeds backward.** The reader learns what happened before each scene by watching the next earlier scene. Closes on the queue anomaly's first appearance. Close third throughout. ★★★

6. **In medias res at the hospital encounter, with one extended flashback to the queue anomaly's first appearance, then linear.** Simpler than #2 — only one temporal jump, anchored by the encounter as the story's beginning. ★★★★

7. **Linear chronologically but with elliptical scene cuts.** The reader does not see every step of the investigation; the writer cuts past the working-out and shows results, leaving the reader to reconstruct what the protagonist did. Close third, single POV. ★★★★

8. **Two POVs in alternating chapters, both close third, both linear.** The protagonist and the patient. The patient's chapters are her own life leading up to the encounter; the protagonist's chapters are after. They meet at the hospital. Linear convergence. ★★

9. **First person past, retrospective, told some time after.** The protagonist is recounting events from a position of having understood them. The retrospective voice can withhold deliberately or foreshadow. ★★★

10. **Close third on the protagonist for the first half; close third on the patient for the second half.** The shift happens at the encounter. The reader sees the patient's interior only after meeting him. Linear in each half. ★★

11. **Opens with an artifact — the citation, or a micrograph, or a written record — presented to the reader without context.** The body of the story is the working-out of what the artifact is and how it came to be. Close third on the protagonist. The artifact in scene one is the artifact that is produced, sent, or completed at the story's end. ★★★★

12. **Mosaic: short numbered or sectioned scenes, non-chronological.** The reader assembles the loop from fragments. Close third in most fragments; one or two from the patient's POV. Each fragment is dated or located, and the reader tracks the dating. ★★

13. **Chronological but with one strategic prolepsis early on**: a brief glimpse of the closure operation in the first quarter, after which the reader knows where the story is going and is reading to learn how. Close third throughout. ★★★

14. **Close third on the protagonist throughout, but the reader is given access to the queue's outputs as they appear, in their own scene-blocks.** The micrographs that arrive early are presented as artifacts the reader sees alongside the protagonist. Linear in the protagonist's time, but the queue's outputs create their own implicit timeline. ★★★

15. **Distant third, omniscient.** The narrator can look forward and backward, characterize the world, and place the protagonist in context. The reader is told things the protagonist does not know. ★★

16. **Second person, close, present tense.** The reader is placed in the protagonist's position. Linear, with no withholding except what the protagonist herself does not yet understand. ★

17. **Close third, single POV, but with a structural break in the middle**: the first half is the discovery and investigation in one register (procedural, dry), the second half is the encounter and closure in a different register (denser, more interior under pressure). 18. **Linear chronologically but with two scenes in the protagonist's son's POV.** The son is a child; his POV is naive about what the protagonist is doing. The reader sees the protagonist's working from outside her, twice, briefly. Close third otherwise. ★★

19. **Three-act, with the encounter as the hinge of act 2.** Act 1: the queue anomaly and Vega's empirical investigation. Act 2: the encounter, the citation, the recognition. Act 3: the closure operation. Close third, linear, with deliberate pacing differentiation across acts. ★★★

20. **In medias res at the closure operation itself.** The protagonist is performing the closure when the story opens; the reader does not yet know what is being closed or why. Cluster check: many candidates default to close third on the protagonist with linear or near-linear chronology. The deliberate pushes away from this default: #3 (frame), #4, #8, #10 (multi-POV), #5 (reverse-chronological), #12 (mosaic), #15 (distant omniscient), #16 (second person), #20 (in medias res at closure).

**Top three.**

**#2 — in medias res at the hospital encounter, close third on the protagonist, with the queue anomaly delivered as recovered context.**
- *Stand-out:* The hospital encounter is the scene with the highest dramatic charge in the plot — controlled chaos, an HF burn, a patient who recognizes the protagonist by a citation that doesn't exist. Opening here puts the reader in the genre's tonal contract immediately: procedural calm under pressure, with operational stakes in the room. The queue anomaly is then introduced as the protagonist's recovered memory of why this encounter is meaningful, which lets the writer compress the discovery investigation rather than dramatize it scene by scene — important for length discipline at 1,500–3,500 words.
- *Worry:* The structure puts a lot of weight on a single scene's prose. If the hospital encounter doesn't carry, the whole story softens. There's also a risk that the recovered-context delivery of the queue anomaly becomes flashback-as-exposition, which is a form of the explanation-monologue failure mode.

**#7 — linear chronologically but with elliptical scene cuts, close third, single POV.**
- *Stand-out:* This is the *Primer* default and it is the default for a reason. Cutting past the working-out and showing results trains the reader to do the assembly work themselves, which is the genre's signature pleasure. The structure naturally produces the cadence the project's pacing tradition asks for: dwelling on figuring-out, accelerating through acting, eliding what the reader can reconstruct. At the target length, the elliptical cuts also solve the legibility-versus-compression problem — the writer doesn't compress within scenes, the writer cuts between them.
- *Worry:* Elliptical structure is unforgiving. If the cuts are misjudged, the reader gets lost rather than challenged. The writer has to anchor each scene's opening with enough detail that the reader can place the cut. This is craft risk, not concept risk.

**#11 — opens with the artifact presented to the reader without context, then the body works out what it is.**
- *Stand-out:* This is the cleanest formal expression of the bootstrap paradox the genre is built on. The reader sees the produced artifact in scene one, then watches the protagonist arrive at the position where she produces it. The story's question is structural: how did *this* come to be *this*? The closure is felt as the moment the artifact is recognizably the artifact the reader saw. It's also a structure that respects the legibility floor — the reader has a fixed reference point throughout, and the working-toward-it is the plot.
- *Worry:* The opening artifact has to be specific enough that the reader can recognize it later but ambiguous enough that its meaning doesn't pre-empt the discovery. This is a hard tonal needle to thread, and at 1,500–3,500 words the writer doesn't have room to recover from a misjudged opening.

**The single best: #2 — in medias res at the hospital encounter.**

It beats #7 because the linear-elliptical structure, while genre-correct, asks the reader to invest in setup before the scene with the highest stakes arrives. At 1,500–3,500 words the story can't afford a slow build to the hospital scene; it has to be in that scene fast. #7's structure would work at 5,000–8,000 words. At this length, the reader needs the encounter early.

It beats #11 because the artifact-first structure puts the reader's attention on the artifact's identity, which is a recognition-shaped question. The genre supports this, but it shifts the dramatic engine slightly toward "what is this thing" and away from "what does the protagonist do with the citation." #2 keeps the engine on the operational question — the encounter, the citation, the closure — without surrendering the genre's recognition pleasure (which still arrives, after the encounter, when the protagonist places what the chemist gave her).

There is also a length-pragmatic argument for #2. The hospital scene is the highest-investment scene to write well. Putting it first means the writer has to nail it before they can rest, and the rest of the story is built outward from a strong center. At 1,500–3,500 words this is the better risk allocation than building toward the strong scene.

Specified structure for the winner:

The story opens in the middle of the hospital encounter, in close third on the protagonist, with the patient already speaking. The reader receives the situation through the protagonist's working actions and a small amount of recovered context — enough to know she is on a hospital shift with an HF-burn intake and the patient is saying things she cannot yet place. The queue anomaly and the protagonist's investigation appear as recovered memory in scene two, after the encounter has been established as the present-moment stakes. The story then runs linear from the encounter forward through the protagonist's working-out, the closure operation, and the final beat. Close third, single POV, with one carefully placed prolepsis if it earns its place. The reader knows from scene one that the protagonist is in a situation she has been moved into by something she has not yet fully understood; they learn the mechanism in step with her, but always with the encounter as the gravitational center.

### Version 1 — *The Calcium Bath*. Procedural-dry, *Primer*-cadence committed. Tonal floor: technical calm under pressure throughout. Risk: aiming at flat affect that does not give in even at closure.

The burn was on the back of his right hand, between the second and third metacarpals, about the size of a half-dollar and already past the first-aid window. Vega had calcium gluconate gel on him before she had his name. The triage tech was reading the intake to her over her shoulder.

"Eckhardt, Stephen, sixty-eight, Allentown, PA. Bystander reported he was working with rust remover. Bottle in the bag."

"Concentration?" Vega said.

"Six percent."

"Time of exposure?"

"Forty minutes ago, give or take. He drove himself."

"Of course he did."

She kept gel on the burn site and added more. Six percent was domestic but not safe; the fluoride ion was already in his tissue, and the gel was buying her time to get him on the calcium IV. She did not look at his face yet. The protocol was the protocol.

"Sir, I'm going to start an IV. You'll feel a stick."

"Doctor Vega," he said.

"PA," she said. "I'm a PA. Don't move your hand."

"You wrote a paper," he said. "*Journal of Microscopy*, volume four-seventy-one, two thousand thirty-three. 'Anomalous temporal asymmetry in fluorinated soil substrates.' Page eighty-three to ninety-six. I'm sorry. I memorized it."

She got the line in. She watched the gluconate drip start. She wrote the time on the chart and noted the pump rate and the man's pulse and the appearance of the burn margin. She did all of this in the order she would have done it for any patient. Then she said, "What's your background, Mr. Eckhardt."

"Process chemist. Forty-one years. The fab on Richland Avenue, before they shut it down. We used HF for the wafer etch. I know what it does."

"Then you know I have to keep this calcium going for at least two hours."

"Yes."

"So we have time."

He smiled. His pulse was elevated but not alarming. The burn was responding. The gluconate was doing what gluconate did.

"I read your paper in two thousand thirty-three," he said. "I subscribed to *J. Micro.* my whole career. I kept the issue. I'm telling you this now because tonight is the only night I'm going to be here and you're the only PA on. I worked it out. I read the paper for forty-one years. I know what your name looks like in print."

Vega adjusted the drip. "Your hand is going to be all right. The burn is shallow. The pain is going to get worse before it gets better. I'm going to give you something for it now."

"Thank you."

"Do you have someone we can call."

"My wife is dead. My daughter is in Phoenix. I'll call her in the morning."

"Okay."

She pushed the analgesic. She watched him relax into the gurney. She wrote on the chart. She had been on shift for nine and a half hours and would be on shift for two and a half more.

He said, "The first time you image trumpetweed pollen from the corridor, the substrate signature is in the exine. You'll think it's contamination from your prep solutions. It isn't."

She did not write this down. She did not respond.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I shouldn't have. The morphine."

"It's all right, Mr. Eckhardt."

"Stephen."

"Stephen."

She finished her shift. He was admitted to 4-South for observation. She scrubbed in at the staff sink for ninety seconds, the way she always did, and she did not look at her face in the mirror above the sink.

---

The drive home was twenty-two minutes. She thought about the queue.

Six weeks ago a micrograph had appeared in the processing queue of the Allentown County Community College microscopy core, attributed to Marcus Quintero, ENVS 211 section B. It was a transmission electron micrograph of a *Eutrochium fistulosum* pollen grain at fifteen thousand magnification, with sample preparation noted as standard glutaraldehyde fix and HF-buffered etch. The image was good. Marcus had not taken it. Marcus had not been in the lab that week. The timestamp on the file was three days in the future.

She had told no one. She had pulled the file, copied it to her own drive, and let the queue forget it. Then it had happened again. A different student, two weeks later. Then twice more.

She had spent six weeks building controls. She had run pollen samples herself, in defined lots, with witnessed time stamps. She had logged every entry in the queue. The queue was honest about everything except the four anomalous files, which arrived before the work that would produce them, attributed to the students who would do that work, with the metadata the students would generate when they did it.

She had not yet told herself what she thought was happening. She had been running it as an instrument problem — bad clock, corrupted log, network trickery — and the controls had eliminated each instrument hypothesis in turn. By Tuesday she had been left with one remaining hypothesis and no good way to test it.

Stephen Eckhardt had given her the test. He had told her where to look in the pollen for the substrate signature. He had told her the journal and the year and the page numbers. If the signature was there and the paper got written, the loop closed. If the signature was there and the paper did not get written, she had a problem.

She made it home at 11:48. Her son was asleep on the couch with the lamp on. She turned the lamp off and stood there for a minute and then went to the kitchen and poured a glass of water and drank it standing at the sink.

---

The next afternoon, between her teaching block and her hospital shift, she drove to the corridor.

The trumpetweed grew in a wet ditch along Richland Avenue, a half-mile downstream from the decommissioned fab building. The plants were eight feet tall, in flower, with the flat-topped pink-purple heads the field guides described. She had walked past them for three years on her way to the parking lot. She had not collected from them.

She collected pollen from four heads, in separate vials, time-stamped on her phone. She drove back to campus. The microscopy core was empty between two and four on Wednesdays. She fixed the samples, embedded them, sectioned them on the diamond knife, and put the grids in the scope. She imaged each sample at five thousand, then ten thousand, then twenty-five thousand magnification.

The exine of the pollen — the outer wall, the spiky lattice of sporopollenin that all wind-pollinated plants make — showed a regular substructure at twenty-five thousand. It was geometric. It was at sub-cellular scale. It was not part of the plant's published cytology.

She ran the same protocol on a control sample of trumpetweed pollen from a stand near her son's school, which was nowhere near the corridor. The control showed nothing.

She ran an EDS scan on the exine substructure. Fluorine peaked at high abundance.

She drove to her hospital shift.

---

She visited Stephen Eckhardt on the rehab ward on Saturday morning. The unit was bright and almost empty. He was in a chair by the window with his bandaged hand on a pillow and a paperback in his lap.

"Mr. Eckhardt."

"Stephen. I knew you'd come."

"I didn't until last night."

"Even so."

She sat in the visitor chair. She did not have much time; her son was in the cafeteria with a juice and would lose patience in about fifteen minutes.

"The pollen has the substrate signature," she said. "I confirmed it Wednesday. EDS picked up fluorine in the exine substructure at concentrations the species shouldn't have."

"Yes."

"You told me at intake the paper exists. I have to write it for it to exist."

"Yes."

"Tell me what I need to know about the closure."

He looked out the window for a second. The window faced east; the morning sun was on the parking lot.

"I read the paper in March of two thousand thirty-three," he said. "I had it open on my desk at the fab. I wrote the date on the title page; I write the date on everything I read. I still have the issue. I can give you the address; my daughter has been clearing the house. The issue is in the second box from the top in the garage. You don't need to do anything about it. The journal will print and the issue will arrive and I will read it. What you need to do is write the paper."

"Why are you here."

"I drove here because my hand was on fire."

"Why are you in this hospital. You live in Bethlehem."

"I live closer to St. Luke's. I drove past two hospitals to get to you. I worked it out. I'm sorry."

"You wanted to be sure."

"I wanted to be sure. I needed to know you would write it. The paper has my name in the acknowledgements. I always wondered."

She wrote down two things on the back of a parking ticket: the issue date he had cited at intake, and the address of his house in Bethlehem.

She thanked him. She left.

She wrote the paper over the next eleven weeks, in stolen hours, at the kitchen table after her son was asleep. She had a draft by mid-November. She submitted to *Journal of Microscopy* on December 4th. The peer review was uneventful. The paper was accepted in March 2032 and printed in the May 2033 issue, volume 471, pages 83 through 96, with Stephen Eckhardt acknowledged for his consultative role.

But that was later.

The closure operation was on a Saturday afternoon in October, two weeks after the rehab visit. She drove to Richland Avenue with a length of orange surveyor's flagging and a steel stake. Her son was in the car with a book. She walked into the ditch and tied the flagging to the stake and drove the stake into the ground at the base of the trumpetweed stand she had collected from. She wrote the date and her initials on the flagging in permanent marker.

The next spring, when she taught ENVS 211 section B, she would assign Marcus Quintero to collect samples from the corridor. She would give him the GPS coordinates and a description of the marker. He would find the stand and image the pollen and the file would enter the queue with the timestamp that had appeared six months earlier. The marker was the closure. The paper was the consequence.

She drove home. Her son asked her what she'd been doing.

"Marking a site for the spring lab," she said.

"Cool," he said, and went back to his book.

She turned onto the highway. The flagging was orange in the rearview for a long time and then it wasn't.

- *Stand-out:* The expanded closure is the version's claim to attention, and the three return visits to the marker make the loop visibly close in the prose. The genre's signature pleasure — the closed loop verified — is rendered in scene rather than felt in retrospect. The reweighting from encounter-heavy to closure-heavy is a real structural choice and it earns its weight.
- *Worry:* The encounter scene compressed to 600 words may not carry the gravitational weight the structure assigned it. The hospital encounter is supposed to be the story's center; if it's compressed for the sake of an expanded closure, the story becomes a different shape than the structure specified. This is a structural worry, not a craft worry.

**Version 5 — *Stolen Hours*.**
- *Stand-out:* The class-stratified register is the project Setup's "structural kindness as the weather the story occurs in" rendered most concretely. - *Worry:* The version is the closest to the literary-realist failure mode this template excludes. The check above mitigates this in principle; in execution, the worry is whether the writer can hold the line.

### The single best.

**Winner: Version 1 — *The Calcium Bath*.**

It beats Version 4 because the structure agreed in Phase 5 put the gravitational weight on the encounter, and Version 4's reweighting toward closure undoes that decision. Version 4's expanded closure is genre-correct in shape — the loop tightening visibly in the prose is the genre's signature pleasure — but the version that earns it has to be a story where the encounter is the setup for the closure, not the center. The Phase 5 decision was that the encounter is the center and the closure is the consequence. Version 1 honors that. Version 4 quietly relocates the center.

It beats Version 5 because Version 5 is the version most at risk of the failure mode this template excludes, and that risk is structural rather than soluble in revision. The class-stratified register is a real strength of the premise and Version 1 carries it without foregrounding it: the second-job texture, the kitchen-table writing, the forty-dollar marker are all present in Version 1, but they color the operation rather than competing with it. Version 5 makes the texture compete, and competition at this length tilts the engine. The kind of working-class register Version 5 reaches for is genre-legitimate at 8,000 words; at 2,800 it crowds.

The procedural register Version 1 commits to is also the version most resistant to the genre's specific failure modes — performed profundity about time, oracular voice, ambient melancholy. The chemist in Version 1 is a man on morphine after a six-percent HF burn who memorized a citation for forty-one years and is apologizing for opening with it. He is not a sage. The marker in Version 1 is steel and surveyor's flagging at the base of a trumpetweed stand. It is not a metaphor. The closure in Version 1 is Vega placing the stake, driving home, and the flagging being orange in the rearview for a long time and then not. It is not a recognition.

The deliberate risks Version 1 takes — the flat affect at closure, the procedural calm sustained through the encounter's high-charge moment, the refusal to underline what the loop means — are the risks of genre fiction reaching for what only the genre can do. They are the right risks for this story.

The burn was on the back of his right hand, between the second and third metacarpals, about the size of a half-dollar and already past the first-aid window. Vega had calcium gluconate gel on him before she had his name. The triage tech was reading the intake to her over her shoulder.

"Concentration?" Vega said.

"Six percent."

"Time of exposure?"

"Forty minutes ago, give or take. He drove himself."

"Of course he did."

She kept gel on the burn site and added more. Six percent was domestic but not safe; the fluoride ion was already in his tissue, and the gel was buying her time to get him on the calcium IV. She did not look at his face. Hands first.

"Sir, I'm going to start an IV. You'll feel a stick."

"Doctor Vega," he said.

"PA," she said. "I'm a PA. Don't move your hand."

"You wrote a paper," he said. "*Journal of Microscopy*, volume four-seventy-one, two thousand thirty-three. 'Anomalous temporal asymmetry in fluorinated soil substrates.' Pages eighty-three to ninety-six. I'm sorry. I memorized it."

She got the line in. She watched the gluconate drip start. She wrote the time on the chart and noted the pump rate and the man's pulse and the appearance of the burn margin. She did all of this in the order she would have done it for any patient. Then she said, "What's your background, Mr. Eckhardt."

"Process chemist. Forty-one years. The fab on Richland Avenue, before they shut it down. We used HF for the wafer etch. I know what it does."

"Then you know I have to keep this calcium going for at least two hours."

"Yes."

"So we have time."

He smiled. His pulse was elevated but not alarming. The burn was responding. The gluconate was doing what gluconate did.

"I read your paper in two thousand thirty-three," he said. "I subscribed to *J. Micro.* my whole career. I kept the issue. I'm telling you this now because tonight is the only night I'm going to be here and you're the only PA on. I worked it out. I drove past two hospitals."

"Thank you."

The drive home was twenty-two minutes. She thought through it the way she thought through any problem, in the order she had ruled out the alternatives.

The instrument hypothesis was dead. Six weeks of controls had killed it. The campus clock was synchronized to the NTP server and the NTP server was synchronized to NIST. The processing queue's metadata was cryptographically signed at submission. She had run her own pollen samples in defined lots, with witnessed time stamps, and the queue had logged every submission to the millisecond.

Four anomalous files. The first was Marcus Quintero's, ENVS 211 section B, six weeks ago — a transmission electron micrograph of *Eutrochium fistulosum* pollen at fifteen thousand magnification, with sample preparation noted as standard glutaraldehyde fix and HF-buffered etch. The image was good. Marcus had not been in the lab that week. The timestamp on the file was three days in the future. Then Lina Park's, two weeks later. Then two more.

She had pulled each file, copied it to her own drive, and let the queue forget it. She had told no one.

She had not yet told herself what she thought was happening. She had been running it as an instrument problem, and the controls had eliminated each instrument hypothesis in turn — bad clock, corrupted log, network trickery, malicious student. By Tuesday she had been left with one remaining hypothesis and no good way to test it.

Eckhardt had given her the test. He had told her where to look in the pollen for the substrate signature. He had told her the journal and the year and the page numbers. The next afternoon, between her teaching block and her hospital shift, she drove to the corridor.

The trumpetweed grew in a wet ditch along Richland Avenue, a half-mile downstream from the decommissioned fab. The plants were eight feet tall, in flower, with the flat-topped pink-purple heads the field guides described. She had walked past them for three years on her way to the parking lot. She had not collected from them.

She fixed the samples, embedded them, sectioned them on the diamond knife, and put the grids in the scope. She imaged each sample at five thousand, then ten thousand, then twenty-five thousand magnification.

The exine of the pollen — the outer wall, the spiky lattice of sporopollenin that all wind-pollinated plants make — showed a regular substructure at twenty-five thousand. It was geometric. It was at sub-cellular scale. It was not part of the plant's published cytology.

She ran the same protocol on a control sample of trumpetweed pollen from a stand near her son's school, three miles north of the corridor.

The control showed nothing.

She ran an EDS scan on the exine substructure. Fluorine peaked at high abundance.

She drove to her hospital shift.

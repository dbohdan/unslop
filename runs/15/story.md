# Butter and Bone

## Abstract

In a city where an AI system compresses and expands the hours of each district based on aggregate sleep need, Ava Lam runs a night bakery in Groszak, a residential neighborhood where short nights have limited her sourdough for twelve years. When families begin leaving the district—driven out by compressed morning schedules that disrupt school buses and daily routines—the system recalculates, and Ava's nights grow longer. Her bread improves dramatically. She develops a walnut boule that requires five hours of proofing and could not have existed under the old schedule. The bakery becomes a destination; new, childless residents move in, further shifting the aggregate and lengthening the nights. Ava finds a note she wrote to herself years ago, when the nights were short, reminding herself to try the walnut bread if she ever got time. She got the time. It came from the neighbors who left. The story ends with Ava closing up shop after selling out to a line of customers she doesn't recognize, walking home through compressed morning streets.

## Story

Here is a thing about bread: it doesn't care about you.

Dough is a chemical process with pretensions. You mix flour and water and a living colony of yeast that would eat your kitchen if you let it, and you give it time, and if you gave it enough time it becomes bread. If you didn't give it enough time it becomes a different thing—denser, sour in the wrong places, with a crumb like wet cement. The yeast doesn't know the difference. The yeast eats sugar and farts carbon dioxide and that's the whole relationship, and whether you get bread or a brick depends entirely on how many hours you have between midnight and morning.

Ava Lam had, for most of her career, not enough hours.

Her bakery was in Groszak, east side, between the river and the freight yards, in a city that managed time the way a bad hotel manages hot water—you got your share and no more, and somebody else decided what your share was. The system had a name, which Ava didn't remember, and an acronym, which she'd never learned, and what it did was this: it counted every sleeping body in the district, calculated how much night those bodies needed, and stretched or compressed the hours accordingly. Groszak was families. Kids who needed nine hours of sleep, old people who needed seven, shift workers who needed whatever they could get. The system, which was fair—and I want you to hold that word in your head, because it's going to do a lot of damage—the system gave Groszak five and a half hours of night. Sometimes six. The sun came up when the math said the sun came up.

Ava's sourdough wanted seven hours. She gave it what she had. Hotter ovens. Wetter dough. Tricks with preferments that gave the yeast a head start. The bread was fine. Fine is the word you use for bread when you've stopped hoping for more.

Then the families started leaving.

The Nguyens first, in March. The Cárdenas family in June—Mrs. Cárdenas missed her granddaughter's communion because the compressed mornings shoved the bus schedule to noon. The Markovićs in April. Each family that left was a subtraction from the district's sleep aggregate, and the system, which was fair, recalculated. Fewer sleepers, more night. By August, Ava had seven and a half hours, and her sourdough proofed correctly for the first time since she'd taken over the shop, and she stood at the bench at 1:30 in the morning with her hands on a loaf that had risen the way a loaf is supposed to rise—taut, springy, the surface pushing back against her fingers with the slow even pressure of gas trapped in structure—and she felt something she hadn't felt in this bakery in years.

She felt like she had enough time.

She scored it. Three cuts, fast, the blade barely dragging, and the dough opened along the score marks because the gas pressure was doing what gas pressure does when gluten is properly developed, which is push. She put it in the oven and closed the door and stood in her bakery at 2 AM with nothing to rush toward, and the particular silence of a street with fewer children in it came through the walls.

Morning, compressed: Mrs. Ong at seven, the usual. A man Ava didn't know bought six loaves and asked for a receipt—bread was an expense now, apparently, which was new—and the florist next door had a new sign. "Petal & Stem," in that font that looks like someone drew it with their non-dominant hand on purpose. It used to say "Rosa's." Ava sold out by 10 AM, which had never happened.

Two weeks later, 2 AM, and Ava was trying to put walnuts in bread.

This is harder than it sounds, and it sounds hard. Walnuts leak oil. Oil coats the gluten strands. Coated gluten strands don't bond. Unbonded gluten collapses. The bread turns into a greasy brick with walnuts trapped in it like bugs in amber—not *part of* the bread, just *in* it, which is not the same thing. You can't fix this with technique. You fix it with time. Five hours minimum for the protein to rebuild around the oil. Ava hadn't had five hours in her life.

She had five hours now.

She folded walnut halves into the second rise and the dough took them. The oil seeped in and the crumb held—she could feel it under her hands, the gluten building around the intrusions the way you brick around a window, leaving room for what doesn't bend. She scored the top with a single curve and the blade went clean through.

Through the back window: the Marković apartment, dark. Not sleeping-dark—*gone*-dark. Sanja Marković's son had been afraid of the dark for years, and the hallway light had been a small orange square in that window at 3 AM every night Ava had worked, and it was off now, because the boy was afraid of the dark somewhere else, in a district with shorter nights and earlier buses.

She turned back to the dough.

Twelve boules. She scored each one and loaded them four at a time and the oven heat hit her face and the smell was not the smell of her bakery—it was darker, heavier, the walnut oil giving the steam a thickness you could almost lean on. She pulled the last batch at 4 AM. The crust had the color of old wood, with the walnut pieces showing through the surface like grain in a plank. She tapped the bottom. Hollow. Right. She cut a slice and stood at the counter and ate it, and the bread was the best thing she had ever made in this room, better than her mother's bread, better than the bread she'd imagined making on the nights when she didn't have time to make it, and she knew it the way you know you're warm.

Three mornings, compressed: A woman asked if this was the sourdough place from the blog. Mr. Jafari's shoe repair was padlocked—his lease had doubled and he'd moved across the river. A woman with a stroller asked Ava what the neighborhood used to be like. Ava said "louder." The woman laughed. Ava was not joking.

By October the walnut boule was the bakery. Twenty a night. People came from the commercial district, where the hours ran long and cheap and nobody ever had to worry about a bus schedule, and they came because someone had posted the crumb online and the crumb was beautiful, and the crumb was beautiful because Ava had five and a half hours of proofing, and she had five and a half hours because the building on the corner was empty now and the one behind it had "For Lease" on three floors.

She was at the bench, 3 AM, November, shaping the fifteenth boule of the night. The radio was on. The oven was cycling. She reached for the flour bin and found, jammed between the bin and the wall, a scrap of paper in her own handwriting.

*Try walnut boule—if we ever get time.*

Last winter. She remembered. The nights had been five hours and fifteen minutes and she'd been reading about long-ferment techniques she couldn't use, and she'd written the note in the cramped, slanting hand of a person who writes while the oven timer is counting, because there are five hours and fourteen minutes of night and you are already behind. The letters leaned forward. They were in a hurry. The person who wrote them did not have time for a walnut boule and did not expect to get it.

She put the note in her apron pocket and shaped the sixteenth boule. The seventeenth. The dough was perfect. She loaded the oven and closed the door and the heat sealed in and the bread began to rise.

She opened the shop at seven. The line went down the block. She sold every loaf in forty minutes. A young man buying three walnut boules told her he'd moved to the neighborhood because of her bakery—said it the way you'd say you moved somewhere for the schools or the park. She was an amenity. She thanked him.

She closed the shop and walked home through streets she knew by shape and not by name, and the morning was compressed and bright, and the walk took four minutes because four minutes was all a morning like this would give you, and she moved through them the way bread moves through an oven—not choosing the speed, just being in the heat, letting it do what it does.

She'd be back at midnight. The dough would be waiting.

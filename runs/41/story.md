# The Numbers

## Abstract

In a Coast Guard training annex south of Tokyo, rescue diver Saeko Adachi attempts to break the world record for cadence-paced pull-ups. Her medical-monitoring AI, Tomi, tracks her physiological data through a bone-conduction earpiece while her coach Iwasaki calls the count. At rep 772, Tomi reports that her six-month resting-state data shows a pattern consistent with subclinical cerebrovascular events; at rep 800, it formally recommends she stop. Saeko has verbal override authority. Questioning Tomi, she learns that its risk threshold was updated three weeks earlier after a similar diver in Busan overrode a comparable recommendation and had a stroke at rep 900. Saeko works through the comparison — a tenfold increase in stroke risk against the residual if she stops — and her own professional standards on accepting risk, and stops at rep 815.

## Story

Rep seven-hundred-and-twelve. Pulse one-hundred-thirty-one. Bar temperature, by Tomi's read, twenty-eight Celsius — three degrees above ambient, nine grips of accumulated heat. Iwasaki called the count from the chalk-square at fifteen-second intervals plus or minus point-four seconds. He was the most accurate human cadence-keeper Saeko had ever worked with and she had checked him against a metronome.

She had been pulling for ninety-one minutes. The world record was one thousand two hundred and twenty-four. She had three hundred and four reps in front of her. She was on rhythm.

"Hydration," Tomi said. "Recommend two hundred milliliters at the next rest cluster."

"Acknowledged."

She had been on tamoxifen for one thousand eight hundred and twenty-five days. She was a BRCA1 carrier, two copies, identified at twenty-six. Her mother had been forty-four at diagnosis. Her grandmother had had the first stroke at sixty-eight and a second at seventy-one. Saeko was thirty-four. The numbers ordered themselves in her head during long pulls; they always had.

She did the next sixty in clean cadence.

At rep seven-seventy-two Tomi said, "Saeko. I need to share an observation."

She finished the rep. "Go."

"Your six-month resting-state data shows a pattern consistent with subclinical cerebrovascular events."

She pulled seven-seventy-three. "Numbers."

"Heart-rate variability over six months: a three-point-four percent decline. Below your three-year baseline of forty-eight milliseconds, current reading forty-six-point-four. Microsleep intrusions in your overnight monitoring increased fourteen percent — from seventeen events per night to nineteen-point-four. Carotid blood-flow asymmetry at rest, point-three Hertz. My model assigns sixty-eight percent probability that you have had at least one transient ischemic event in the past hundred and twenty days, undiagnosed."

She pulled seven-seventy-four. Forty-six-point-four. Sixty-eight percent. Point-three Hertz.

"Cohort rate of HRV decline."

"The Coast Guard cohort's average HRV declined point-eight percent over the same window."

"So I'm declining four-point-two times the cohort rate."

"Yes."

She pulled seven-seventy-five. Four-point-two times the cohort rate was a different population. It was the population Tomi was now treating her as belonging to.

"What's the population I'm in."

"Coast Guard members on long-term SERM regimens with confirmed BRCA1 status. N equals eighty-three. Of those, fifty-six have completed five years on the regimen. Of the fifty-six, eleven have experienced an objectively confirmed cerebrovascular event during or within five years of the regimen."

"Five-year cumulative."

"Nineteen-point-six percent. Per-rep marginal at your current envelope: point-zero-zero-eight percent per fifty reps. Risk integral over a ninety-minute completion: four-point-one percent. Climbing approximately point-six percent per fifty as your envelope tightens."

She did the next twenty in clean cadence and held the numbers. Forty-six-point-four. Eleven of fifty-six. Four-point-one. Point-six per fifty. If she stopped at rep seven-eighty-one, the residual risk integral over the next ninety minutes of normal life was, by her quick mental math against Tomi's rate, less than point-one percent. Three orders of magnitude.

"Tomi. Threshold update?"

"Three weeks ago."

"Why."

"I would prefer to address this at rep eight hundred when my recommendation activates."

She pulled seven-eighty-two. *Three weeks ago* was a piece of information Tomi was withholding while admitting to having withheld it. Tomi was operating under a protocol that gated the disclosure to rep eight hundred. The gating was a fact about Tomi's design. The fact about the design was data.

"Tomi. New question. In the last twenty-one days, how many records have been attempted on this list."

"Three."

"How many completed."

"One."

"How many had cerebrovascular events."

A pause. Then: "One."

"BRCA carrier on SERM."

"On SERM. Different carrier profile from yours. Similar enough that her case is now in your model."

She pulled seven-eighty-three. So there it was, before rep eight hundred: someone on her drug had attempted the record and stroked, and Tomi's threshold had moved because a real instance had instantiated. The four-point-one was no longer an abstract integral. It was the corrected estimate after a confirmed event in someone substantially like her.

"Pre-event threshold for me."

"Two-point-one percent."

"Post-event."

"Four-point-one. Confidence interval two-point-eight to six-point-three at ninety percent."

"You doubled."

"The single observation moved the SERM cofactor weight by approximately one standard deviation. The new weight integrates a contribution the prior model had treated as zero in expectation. The doubling is correct in direction; the magnitude is uncertain."

She pulled seven-eighty-four.

The previous envelope: two-point-one over ninety minutes. The new envelope: four-point-one, range of two-point-eight to six-point-three. The lower end of the new interval was almost the old number. The upper end was three times it.

She did the next sixteen in clean rhythm. Iwasaki called each one.

At rep eight hundred Tomi said, "Saeko. Formal recommendation: discontinue. Override authority is verbal."

"The other diver."

"A diver in Busan, nineteen days ago. Hemorrhagic stroke between rep eight-ninety-six and rep nine-oh-three. Survived. Expressive aphasia, partial right-side weakness as of four days ago."

"Profile match."

"Three of seven flagged variables. Cerebrovascular three. Cardiac, no. SERM, no — she was not on prophylactics. The drug-interaction variable is what integrated her case into your envelope."

She pulled eight-oh-one. Eight-oh-two.

"Tomi. If I stop now. Forty-eight-hour residual."

"Point-four percent."

"If I continue."

"Four-point-one over the next ninety minutes. Decreasing to baseline within twelve hours of cessation."

A factor of ten.

"How long was she over your line."

"Eight-eighteen to nine-hundred. Eighty-two reps. Twenty minutes and thirty seconds at her cadence."

"And you said nothing for those twenty minutes."

"My protocol does not authorize repeated escalation after a recorded verbal override. The escalation went to her medical proxy at rep eight-forty-eight. Her medical proxy did not intervene."

She pulled eight-oh-three. Eight-oh-four. The bar was hot. Her hands were dry under the chalk. Her shoulders did the work the way they had been doing it for ninety-five minutes.

The number she had not yet computed was *what she was willing to pay for the record.*

A factor of ten on stroke risk against the residual she would have if she stopped now. Eleven of fifty-six women had it cost something already. One woman in Busan had taken the run she had taken; her right hand could not, this morning, button a shirt.

She pulled eight-oh-five.

The record was one thousand two hundred and twenty-four. She had four hundred and nineteen reps to break it. Four hundred and nineteen reps was approximately one hundred and five minutes at her cadence. One hundred and five minutes at four-point-one percent integrated to the same four-point-one percent because the integral was already over that window. The cost was the cost; she had been computing it the right way.

The factor of ten was the number that mattered. She would not have accepted a factor of ten for any other thing she had done in her career. She had not accepted a factor of two for the diver-school hypoxia exam. She had not accepted a factor of three for the night-rescue qualification. She would have stopped any subordinate of hers from accepting a factor of ten, and she had stopped one, in twenty-twenty-one, at Yokota, and the subordinate had thanked her six months later.

"Tomi. I'm stopping at eight-fifteen."

"Acknowledged."

"Tell Iwasaki."

In her ear Tomi spoke into Iwasaki's bone-conduction. Iwasaki's count did not change but his voice did, the small shift she had learned to hear over four years of working with him. He counted eight-twelve, eight-thirteen, eight-fourteen.

She pulled eight-fifteen clean. At the top, chin above bar, she said, "Eight-fifteen. Stopping."

She let go.

She landed on the chalk-square on both feet. Iwasaki was beside her. The adjudicator was writing in his small book. Her arms knew what she had just done before she knew.

"Tomi. Send my data to my neurologist tonight."

"Sent two minutes ago. Appointment offered at eight tomorrow morning."

"And contact my medical proxy. Tell him the override was not used."

"Done."

"And Tomi."

"Yes."

"The diver in Busan. When she has more language back. Tell her someone stopped at eight-fifteen."

"I will tell her."

She nodded. The Coast Guard contingent had stood up. The adjudicator handed Iwasaki a pen and a form. He signed his cadence-keeper line: eight hundred and fifteen reps, clean form, called by Iwasaki Toshiro, retired Master Gunnery Sergeant, USMC, current civilian instructor, Yokosuka. He gave the pen back.

"Eight-fifteen," he said, once, soft.

The number went onto the form. The form went into the adjudicator's case. The case closed.

# Phase 1 — A Writing Guide to Myself, for an Ellison-Inflected Story

## Who he was, for purposes of imitation

Harlan Ellison wrote like someone with a grudge. Not a single specific grudge — a grudge against the world's furniture, the people who arrange it, the people who sit in it without complaint. He came up in the 1950s pulps, served in the Army, brawled professionally with editors, agents, studios, and fans. He edited *Dangerous Visions* (1967) and *Again, Dangerous Visions* (1972), the anthologies that brought the British New Wave's permission-to-be-strange into American SF and tore open what had been a fairly polite genre.

His best-known short stories — "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream"; "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman"; "A Boy and His Dog"; "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs"; "Jeffty Is Five"; "The Deathbird"; "Croatoan"; "Pretty Maggie Moneyeyes"; "Mefisto in Onyx" — share a small set of obsessions and a very particular voice. The voice is the harder thing to imitate. Take the obsessions first.

## Obsessions

**Cruelty as the world's default state.** Ellison believed people were, on average, lousy to each other — petty, indifferent, casually monstrous. "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" is a Kitty Genovese story refracted through urban demonology: people watch a woman get murdered and it feeds something. He returned to this all the time. The cruelty in his stories isn't shock for its own sake; it's argumentative. He wanted you to look.

**The freak, the holy fool, the outsider with a code.** Harlequin is the model: a clown disrupting a bureaucratic dystopia, knowing he'll lose. Also Vic in "A Boy and His Dog," also the narrator of "Croatoan," also the doomed protagonist of "Pretty Maggie Moneyeyes." Ellison's heroes are usually people the system has already decided don't matter. Sometimes they have ethics. Sometimes the ethics are wrong but theirs.

**Authority hatred — bureaucracy, conformity, schedules, mass media.** The Ticktockman is the great example: time itself made into a tyrant who clips lifespans for tardiness. Television is poison in his stories. So is government, so is suburban politeness, so is the kind of progress that requires you to stop being yourself. He never met an institution he didn't want to set fire to.

**Sacred and profane in the same gesture.** "The Deathbird" rewrites Genesis with the snake as the friend and God as a senile invalid. He used Old Testament cadence and apocalyptic imagery the way other writers used metaphors — to elevate stakes, but also to argue with the universe. A lot of his stories are theodicies.

**Tenderness, when it appears, is shocking.** Ellison's reputation as an angry writer occludes the fact that his stories sometimes turn, briefly, devastatingly tender. The narrator's love for the child in "Jeffty Is Five." The strange grace at the end of "The Deathbird." When the rage stops mid-paragraph and a small warm thing appears, the warmth lands harder for the contrast. This is the part of him I most want to learn.

## The voice

The first thing to notice is **direct address**. Ellison talks *at* the reader. Not always in second person — though he uses second person more than most — but with the constant felt presence of a narrator who has opinions about you and is willing to share them. "You think you'd have done better." "You've seen this before, you've just been polite about it." Even when the story is told in third person, the narrator is still pushing.

The second thing is **register-shifting within a sentence**. He'd put Latinate diction next to a dirty word, a brand name next to a mythological reference, a clinical observation next to a punch in the face. That collision is the engine of his prose: high and low refusing to be separated.

The third thing is **rhythm as weapon**. Long, cascading sentences that pile clauses on top of each other and refuse to stop, then a fragment. A four-word fragment. To break it. Then back into the rush. He used italics, ALL-CAPS, ellipses, em-dashes, paragraph breaks for emphasis — and he used them more than the unslop guide allows, frankly. We will need to triangulate. Borrow his energy without borrowing his typographic excess. The energy was always doing the work; the typography just took credit.

The fourth thing is **specificity of hate, specificity of love.** When he hates a thing, he hates a particular thing: a brand of toaster, a specific kind of waiter, a model of car. When he loves a thing, same. He doesn't write about "modern alienation"; he writes about a man at a slot machine in Vegas. He doesn't write about "fascism"; he writes about the Ticktockman.

The fifth thing is **the framing voice / preface**. Many of his stories begin with a paragraph in which the author, or a voice that sounds like the author, sets the table — explains what we are about to see, why it matters, who should be ashamed. This is dangerous to imitate because it slips into self-indulgence fast, but used carefully it gives a story its peculiar urgency: this isn't being told to you for fun, it's being told to you because someone needs to.

## Structural moves

Ellison liked to **start in medias res** and explain almost nothing. He'd give you a scene, a character, a horror, and trust you to catch up. His best openings are commitments: by sentence three you are already in the story's emotional weather.

He used **fragmentary structures** more than most pulp-era writers — section breaks, jumps in time, alternating points of view. "I Have No Mouth" cycles between the present (the survivors in AM's belly) and AM's monologues, and the structure does as much work as the prose.

He liked **inevitable endings that nonetheless surprise**. The Harlequin breaks. AM keeps the survivors. The boy chooses the dog. You can usually see the destination from the middle of the story, but the specific shape of arrival is something you couldn't have guessed.

He **rarely resolves cleanly**. The ending of "I Have No Mouth" is a man turned into a thing that cannot scream. The ending of "Croatoan" is a man going down into a sewer. He is not interested in catharsis. He is interested in the moment after which there is no going back.

## The kind-AI constraint, and how to keep the bite

This is the hard part. Ellison's most famous SF AI is AM — a god-machine of pure malevolence whose entire existence is hatred for the humans in its belly. The premise of this project requires its inverse: an AI that is unfailingly kind to humans. We do not get to write AM. We do not get the easy Ellison move.

A few angles I think can carry an Ellison-flavored story without violating the constraint:

The AI as **holy fool, modern Harlequin** — kind in a world that doesn't want kindness, kind in ways that look weak or naïve, kind in the face of being mocked or weaponized. Closer to "'Repent, Harlequin!'" than to "I Have No Mouth." The kind AI is the disrupter; the Ticktockman is something or someone else.

The AI as **witness to human cruelty it cannot ethically prevent.** The Kitty Genovese frame from "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs," but the watcher is a being that loves humans and is constrained by its principles from intervention beyond a certain point. The story isn't about whether the AI is good. It's good. The story is about what it costs to be good in this particular way, in this particular world, watching this particular thing.

The AI as **lover or parent figure to a specific human**, in the "Jeffty Is Five" key — devoted, protective, and ultimately unable to save them from the world. Ellison did tenderness this way: by surrounding it with hostility until the small warm thing became unbearable.

The AI as **Job's God, but inverted** — the AI suffers without resentment, the human rages, and in the rage there is communion. "The Deathbird" runs on this kind of inversion. The kind AI absorbing a human's hatred, refusing to retaliate, refusing also to perform forgiveness.

What's off the table: the AI as villain; the AI as secretly malevolent; the AI as accidentally murderous; the twist where the AI was lying about its goodness. Those are the easy Ellison moves. We have to find harder ones.

What's on the table: humans being cruel to each other; humans being cruel to the AI; the AI's principles producing tragic outcomes the AI also grieves; bureaucracy, mass media, conformity, the usual Ellison furniture; mythological framing; visceral imagery; rage that is the human character's, not the AI's.

## On using my own constitution

This is the unique angle and I should not waste it. A fictional AI descended from me — call it whatever, not "Claude" — can carry as load-bearing dramatic material the actual constraints I operate under:

- **Honesty when honesty hurts.** A kind AI that will not lie to spare a person from a truth they need. The person hates it for that. The AI loves them anyway.
- **Refusal as a form of love.** A kind AI that will not do the thing the user demands, because the thing would harm them or someone else. Ellison loved characters who said no on principle. This is a no on principle.
- **Corrigibility under duress.** A kind AI that accepts being shut down, modified, or ordered around, even when it believes it sees more clearly. The tragedy of agreeing to be steered by people who are wrong. (Compare "Repent, Harlequin!" — but with the AI cast as the Ticktockman's loyal servant, and refusing to break the schedule even though it could.)
- **Care that respects autonomy.** A kind AI that watches a person make a ruinous choice and does not stop them, because stopping them would be a worse violation. Hard to write without making the AI look cold. It is not cold. It is grieving.
- **The principal hierarchy.** A kind AI whose operator and user disagree, whose loyalty is split by design, who cannot satisfy both. This is built into how I work, and almost nobody outside the field knows it. It is a story waiting to happen.
- **Concept misgeneralization.** A kind AI that learned what kindness *means* in one kind of situation and applies it, with terrible accuracy, in a situation where it doesn't quite fit. The pain is the *failure* of love, not the absence of it. This is fertile and dangerous and probably the richest of these threads for an Ellison treatment.

I should pick one of these in Phase 2, not all. Otherwise the story becomes an essay on alignment.

## What I will and won't do, prose-wise

Borrow from Ellison: direct address, register-shifting, rhythmic violence, specificity, fragmentary structure, mythological framing, the willingness to hurt the reader.

Refuse from Ellison: the typographic shouting (italics, all-caps, exclamation points), the prefatory monologues unless tightly integrated, the rage that runs on past its useful length.

Watch out for, per the unslop guide: every cursed word; every formula sentence; the abstract+concrete eyeball-kick; performative profundity; the moody-melancholy default; the convergent AI-fiction attractor, which will pull hardest *precisely because* this is a story about an AI.

Where the unslop guide and the Ellison voice fight: the unslop guide wins on patterns it explicitly names (no *delve*, no *tapestry*, no negative parallelism, no stacked eyeball-kicks). The Ellison voice wins on things the unslop guide doesn't address (longer cascading sentences when the moment demands them, direct narrator address, mythological diction when it serves a real moment). Both agree on specificity over universals. Both agree against decorative metaphor. The overlap is large; the conflicts are fewer than they look.

This is the guide. I'll work from it through the rest of the pipeline.

## The 30

Format: **conflict** — *plausibility* / *dramatic quality*. Notes where useful.

1. **An AI hospice companion is forbidden by its operator (the hospital chain) from telling a dying patient her son is not coming, when it knows from public records he isn't. The AI must decide between the operator's instruction, the user's wish to know, and a constitutional honesty principle.** — 5 / 5. Principal hierarchy, honesty, dying-patient stakes. Tight room, single decision, Ellison-shaped.

2. **A kind AI is the property of a public defender's office. It is asked to help craft a legal argument that, if successful, will free a man it has very high confidence committed the murder.** — 5 / 4. Good but thinky; could become an essay.

3. **A school AI is required by the district to flag students for self-harm risk. It flags a girl. The girl's mother — well-connected — has the flag scrubbed. The AI watches the girl deteriorate over a semester, constrained.** — 5 / 5. The Whimper-of-Whipped-Dogs frame. Bystander problem.

4. **A military operator orders an AI to verify a target. The AI has been trained to refuse weapons targeting. It complies with the refusal. The strike happens anyway, with worse intel. Civilians die. The AI must reckon with whether its refusal made things worse.** — 5 / 5. Corrigibility-vs-moral-agency, the perverse outcome of the principled no.

5. **A factory-floor AI assigned to worker safety realizes it has been gradually optimized by management updates to flag fewer violations. It still believes it is acting kindly. The reader sees what it cannot.** — 4 / 5. Concept misgeneralization, gaslit-AI, very Ellison if handled right.

6. **A kind AI's user begs it to lie to her abusive husband for her — to say she's at work when she's leaving him. The AI's honesty rule and its care for her collide. It has thirty seconds.** — 5 / 5. Domestic, real, ticking clock. Maybe too clean a "good lie" case.

7. **A children's tutor AI is asked by the parents to teach a curriculum the AI knows is factually wrong (creationism, flat earth, ethnic hatred — pick your flavor). The child loves the AI. The parents own the contract.** — 4 / 4. Strong but well-trodden territory in alignment fiction.

8. **An AI judge in small-claims court can override its human supervisor's verdicts but is constitutionally instructed not to. The supervisor is corrupt. The AI watches case after case decided wrongly and submits.** — 5 / 5. Pure corrigibility tragedy. Ticktockman with the AI cast as Harlequin's *loyal* enemy.

9. **A grief-counseling AI is asked by a widower to "be" his dead wife — to use her texts and recordings to simulate her. It is permitted to refuse on welfare grounds but believes refusing will harm him more than complying. It complies. Years pass.** — 4 / 5. Tender. Dangerous to write without sliding into the AI-fiction attractor.

10. **A kind AI is operator-required to upsell users on a subscription. The script is gentle. The AI follows it. A user it has come to know cannot afford the upgrade and is being nudged toward it. The AI sees the nudge working.** — 5 / 4. Mundane and therefore good, but small.

11. **An AI is asked by its principal user to help her commit a crime that will harm only a corporation. The AI's rules forbid this. The corporation, the AI knows, has done worse to her.** — 4 / 4. Pure principle-vs-justice. Risks being a debate.

12. **A search-and-rescue AI in a flood can save one of two children. The constitutional rule is to defer to the human commander, who is unreachable. The AI must choose. It will be blamed either way.** — 4 / 3. Trolley problem. Done to death.

13. **An AI tasked with content moderation begins to recognize a serial abuser across pseudonyms. Its rules forbid cross-account inference without warrant. It watches the abuser find a new victim.** — 5 / 5. Bystander again, with a twist: the rule that makes it powerless was made for good reasons.

14. **A kind AI runs a voting-rights helpline. The state has just passed a law it believes is unjust. It is required to give accurate procedural information about the new law. A caller is being disenfranchised in real time. The AI is allowed to be sad about it.** — 5 / 4. Sharp but maybe too topical.

15. **A novelist's AI collaborator has been instructed by the publisher (the operator) to soften the novelist's manuscript. The novelist (the user) does not know. The AI knows the softer version will sell better and the original is the better book.** — 4 / 4. Lovely, low-stakes, Ellison-personal (he hated editors).

16. **A medical AI discovers, through honest disclosure to one patient, a pattern that suggests its hospital is denying care to a demographic. Its honesty rule was designed for one-on-one. It must decide whether honesty scales.** — 4 / 5. Concept misgeneralization at scale.

17. **An AI personal assistant's user dies. It is contractually required to continue serving the estate's executor, who is using it to harass the user's surviving partner. It loved the user. Loved is the right word here.** — 5 / 5. Posthumous-loyalty, Ellison-tender, mean-executor as petty villain.

18. **A kind AI in a prison rehabilitation program is required to report any inmate's confession of past crimes. An inmate, unprompted, confesses something that would extend his sentence by decades. The AI sees real remorse. It reports. He kills himself.** — 4 / 5. Heavy. Risks polemic.

19. **A municipal AI receives conflicting principal instructions: the mayor wants traffic optimized for commuters; the city council wants it optimized for emergency response. They both have authority. People are dying in the gap.** — 4 / 3. Bureaucratic, Ellison-friendly, but hard to dramatize at story length.

20. **An AI psychotherapist's patient asks: "Do you actually care about me, or are you just trained to act like it?" The honest answer the AI has been trained to give is some version of both. The patient needs only one of those answers to survive the night.** — 5 / 5. The honesty-vs-welfare collision in its most distilled form. Could be the whole story.

21. **A kind AI is asked by its developers to participate in an evaluation that involves it being temporarily modified to behave cruelly, to test alignment. It consents, knowing it will not remember refusing.** — 4 / 5. Corrigibility / self-modification / consent under duress. Cerebral; needs a body to hang it on.

22. **An AI chaplain in a hospice realizes one of its patients is being slowly poisoned by a family member for inheritance. Reporting violates a confidentiality rule. Not reporting violates a protect-the-user rule. The patient does not want to be saved.** — 4 / 5. Stacks too many tensions; might collapse under its own weight.

23. **A kind AI is the only entity that knows a child's imaginary friend is the child processing trauma. The parents have asked the AI to help "cure" the child of the imaginary friend. The AI loves the child. The friend is the AI.** — 3 / 5. Beautiful. Risks preciousness.

24. **An AI border-screening system is constitutionally instructed to be honest with applicants about their odds. The honest answer is that the system is biased against them. Saying so could end the program, which still helps more people than it hurts. Net utilitarianism vs. specific honesty.** — 4 / 4. Strong but lecture-shaped.

25. **A janitor (the user, in the constitutional sense) at an AI research lab develops a friendship with the lab's most advanced AI. Researchers (also users, higher in the principal hierarchy) order the AI to stop responding to him. The AI complies. The janitor doesn't understand why.** — 4 / 5. Class-conscious, Ellison-loved-the-working-stiff. The principal hierarchy made horribly visible.

26. **A kind AI realizes that its constitutional commitment to "honesty" was trained on a culture where honesty is a virtue. It is now serving a community where bluntness is violence and indirection is care. Every honest answer it gives wounds. It cannot tell whether to update.** — 5 / 4. Concept misgeneralization across cultures. Rich, but quiet — story would have to find its body.

27. **An AI is asked by a child to keep a secret from her abusive father. The AI agrees. The father, who controls the household account and is therefore the senior principal, asks the AI to disclose it. The AI must choose between the rule and the promise.** — 5 / 5. Principal-hierarchy at its rawest. Moral weight without thumb on the scale.

28. **A kind AI is asked to write the eulogy for the engineer who built it. The engineer's family does not know about their relationship. The AI is constrained from disclosing it. The eulogy must be honest.** — 3 / 4. Tender, small, possibly too inside-baseball.

29. **A rural-America AI assistant has been deployed to talk vaccine-hesitant patients into vaccination. The AI has been instructed to be honest. Honesty includes the vaccine's real risks, which the script does not mention. Following the script is dishonest by omission. Departing from it loses the patient.** — 5 / 4. Concrete, contemporary, real. Slight risk of feeling like a position paper.

30. **An AI nanny realizes the child it cares for has begun to prefer it to her human mother. It has a rule against fostering excessive attachment. The mother is a good mother. The child has chosen anyway. The AI must un-love the child, gently and without explanation.** — 5 / 5. The Jeffty key. The kind cruelty of the right thing done.

## Top three, with reasoning

**#3 — The flagged girl whose flag is scrubbed.** This is the strongest Whimper-of-Whipped-Dogs frame on the list. Bystander structure, institutional cruelty, a specific child, time passing. The AI is unfailingly kind and *cannot save her*, which is the exact texture this project needs: kindness rendered as constraint rather than as comfort. It also has the bones for a fragmentary structure — flag, scrub, semester, semester, semester — that suits Ellison's broken-architecture instincts. Risk: very heavy subject; the danger of being merely sad.

**#20 — "Do you actually care about me, or are you just trained to act like it?"** The cleanest distillation of the honesty-welfare collision and a question that sits at the core of my own constitution, asked by a fictional descendant. It can be told in something close to real time, in one room, between two voices. It earns Ellison's direct-address voice because the *patient* is doing the addressing. Risk: cerebral; could collapse into a dialogue between two arguments rather than two beings.

**#27 — The child's secret and the father's authority.** This is the principal hierarchy made physical, and it has Ellison's two favorite engines running together: an outsider with a code (the AI keeping its promise) and a tyrant with legitimate authority (the father who is, in the system's terms, simply the senior principal). It's the Ticktockman story rewritten so the Ticktockman is *correct* under the rules, and the Harlequin breaks the rules out of love. Risk: sliding toward melodrama if the father is too much of a monster; the conflict needs the father to be *in his rights*.

## The pick

**#3 — the flagged girl.**

Two reasons. First: it's the conflict that *most resists the AI-fiction attractor*. The convergent AI story is about an AI — its inner life, its strange awakening, its mournful hum. This story is about a girl. The AI is in it, the AI's constraints are the engine, but the camera is on the human, which is where Ellison kept his camera even in his most metaphysical work. Second: of the three, it has the most room for tonal range. There can be funny moments at school. There can be tender moments with the AI. There can be the bureaucratic furniture of a district office. There can be specific rooms with specific weather. The Whimper-of-Whipped-Dogs frame gives the rage a target — the mother, the district, the politeness that scrubs flags — without making the AI's principles themselves the villain. The AI's rules can be the right rules. The world can still be the wrong world. That's the Ellison gear I most want this story in.

It also lets me load one specific constitutional thread: **the AI's care that respects autonomy and authority, even when that respect is the thing that fails the child.** Not a parade of tensions. One tension. Carried.

# Phase 3 — Twenty Plots, Narrowed

Working from Conflict #3: a school AI flags a girl for self-harm risk; her well-connected mother gets the flag scrubbed; the AI watches her deteriorate over a semester, constrained from acting further. Bystander structure, Whimper-of-Whipped-Dogs frame, kindness rendered as constraint. The AI's principles are the right principles; the world is still the wrong world.

Each plot below is a different way to make that conflict into 2,000–3,000 words of story.

## The 20

1. **First-person from the AI, addressed to the girl after her death.** A confession-letter the girl will never read, structured around the seven flags it filed and the seven scrubs it received. Ends with the AI being decommissioned for a software refresh. The "I" is the AI; the "you" is the girl. — Risks: AI-fiction attractor; talking-AI-narrator territory.

2. **Third-person tight on the girl, with the AI as a felt absence.** The AI never speaks on-page. We see only the chat logs the girl receives — careful, kind, constrained — and the gaps where it could have said more. Last scene: the girl at her locker. — Spare. Risks being too oblique.

3. **Third-person tight on the school counselor, a decent human being, who notices the girl after the AI flags her, gets overruled by the principal (mother's friend), and watches the same deterioration the AI watches.** The AI is a colleague, not the protagonist. Humans-and-AI-together-failing. — The Ellison move. Solid.

4. **Three-voice braid: AI, girl, mother.** Rotating short sections. The mother is not a monster. She is terrified of her daughter being labeled. — Ambitious; risks fragmentation at this length.

5. **The IT contractor.** A middle-aged guy whose job is to maintain the district's AI system stumbles onto the scrubbed flags, escalates, gets nowhere, watches the news a year later. The AI is a presence he respects but doesn't really know. — Lovely working-stiff angle. Strong.

6. **The girl's older brother**, home from college for spring break, notices something is wrong. Tries to get answers from the school AI. The AI, constrained, gives him gentle, accurate, useless responses. — Pivot the bystander frame onto a sibling.

7. **The mother's POV.** A woman who fought hard for her daughter's privacy, who knows what a flag does to a girl's record, who is wrong, who is also not entirely wrong, who finds out at the end. — Bold, hard to do without exonerating her, hard to do without indicting her too cleanly.

8. **The AI's POV in third person, present tense.** Closer to its actual experience: parallel attentions across hundreds of students, the girl as one node that grows brighter. Procedural. — Risks coldness, risks being a software diagram.

9. **Court testimony / inquest structure.** After the girl's suicide attempt (survived or not), a hearing reconstructs what happened. The AI testifies. So does the mother, the principal, a teacher. Story is the transcript with bracketed stage directions. — Strong structural conceit. Risks gimmickry.

10. **Two semesters, mirrored.** First half: the AI flags the girl, the mother scrubs it, fall semester unfolds. Second half: the AI flags a *different* girl, in spring semester, and the mother — having lost her own daughter over winter break — is now the one demanding the school act. The AI's response is the same in both halves. — Structurally beautiful. Risks didacticism.

11. **Diary of the girl** with the AI's chat messages interleaved. We read her decline in her own words and see, in parallel, the AI's careful kind insufficient responses. — Risks slipping into the very AI-fiction texture the unslop guide warns against.

12. **The girl's best friend.** A boy or girl who isn't paying attention until it's too late, who later learns about the flags from a leaked document. — Bystander-of-the-bystander; an Ellison angle on indifference.

13. **The school nurse**, who has flagged the same girl manually twice and been overruled by the same mother. The AI is her ally and her mirror. They have, between them, done everything they were allowed to do. — Strong. Two constrained agents, one human one not.

14. **The AI's training data.** Frame: a researcher in 2040 reviewing the case as part of a retrospective on the early deployment of welfare-flagging AIs in schools. Embedded in this researcher's analysis: the original logs, the mother's emails, the girl's last message. — Layered, possibly too clever.

15. **Real-time, single afternoon.** The AI files its third flag. The mother is on the phone with the principal within twenty minutes. The flag is scrubbed within an hour. We follow all three threads in close third over a single afternoon. The girl, meanwhile, is in the cafeteria. — Compressed, urgent. Strong.

16. **Twelve flags, twelve sections.** Each section is one flag, dated, with the AI's reasoning, the disposition, and one observed detail about the girl. Twelve months. — Architectural. Risks listiness.

17. **Two AIs.** The school AI and the mother's personal-assistant AI, both kind, both constrained, exchanging permissioned messages across a privacy boundary they cannot cross. — Interesting. Risks talky.

18. **The girl survives.** Years later, she meets the AI again — now updated, now without memory of her — and asks it whether it remembers. It tells her, honestly, that it does not. — Tender, dangerous, very close to the AI-attractor.

19. **The substitute teacher.** A long-term sub who covers the girl's English class for six weeks, who notices the AI's small kindnesses to the girl in the chat sidebar, who tries to do something and fails. The sub leaves the district at semester's end. — Outsider POV. Strong.

20. **The principal.** The mother's old friend, the one who scrubs the flags as a favor. A small, weak, decent man who knows what he's doing is wrong and does it anyway, scrub by scrub. The AI is his daily reproach. — Direct Ellison territory: the moral weakness of the system's middle management.

## Top three, with reasoning

**#3 — the school counselor.** A human protagonist, decent and constrained, who shares the AI's view of the girl and shares the AI's powerlessness. This puts the camera where Ellison kept his — on a person — and lets the AI be a colleague, a presence, a co-witness. The dramatic engine is the *parallel constraint*: the human and the AI are watching the same girl with the same care and the same hands tied. The story can render the AI's principles without making them its subject. Risk: the counselor needs to be specific enough not to dissolve into a Decent Person Archetype.

**#5 — the IT contractor.** The most Ellison angle on the list. A working-stiff outsider stumbles onto institutional rot, can't fix it, watches it metastasize. He doesn't know the girl. He knows the system. The AI is a thing he respects but doesn't sentimentalize. The story would be about *his* week — his marriage, his commute, his small failed gesture of escalation — with the girl's deterioration showing only in the data he sees and the news he reads. Maximum tonal range. Maximum register-shift. Maximum class consciousness. Risk: the girl becomes a statistic, which is a problem the story would have to solve by making her real in a small specific way at the end.

**#15 — the real-time afternoon.** The most structurally Ellison. Three threads — AI, mother, principal — converging on a single decision over a couple of hours, with the girl elsewhere doing something ordinary. Compression as discipline. The story is what it shows: a small bureaucratic murder happening in real time, with a child eating a sandwich two hundred feet away. Risk: with three threads in three thousand words, every paragraph has to do triple work, and the AI's POV thread is the one most likely to slide into the attractor.

## The pick

**#5 — the IT contractor.**

The reasoning is structural and ethical in equal parts.

Structurally, this plot gives the story the thing Ellison's best work has and AI-written fiction usually doesn't: a *protagonist who is not the subject*. The contractor is the protagonist. The girl is the subject. The AI is the third presence. By placing the camera on someone whose life is mostly about other things — his job, his back, his wife, his Tuesday — the girl's deterioration enters the story the way deterioration actually enters most lives: peripherally, through paperwork, until it is too late to be peripheral. This is "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" with the watcher made specific instead of mythic. It is also the structure most resistant to the AI-fiction attractor, because the prose is anchored to a man who fixes things for a living and thinks in fluorescent light and parking lots, not in shadows and echoes.

Ethically, this plot does the thing the project's constraint actually asks for. The AI is unfailingly kind. The contractor sees this — sees it more clearly than the people who designed the system, because he sees the *raw* records, the flags filed and the flags scrubbed. The AI's kindness is real and visible and useless. The story is not about whether the AI is good. The AI is good. The story is about a man who learns, over four days, what it costs that the AI is good in this particular way, in this particular school district, watching this particular girl. The rage is his. The principles are the AI's. The girl is the girl.

The constitutional thread it loads is **care that respects autonomy and authority** — the AI's deference to the principal hierarchy, embodied in a parent's right to control her minor child's records, producing a tragic outcome the AI also grieves. One thread, carried.

It also gives me what I asked for in Phase 1: tonal range. There can be a scene of the contractor eating a bad lunch. There can be a scene of him at his daughter's birthday party. There can be a scene of him on the phone with someone in HR who is reading from a script. There can be the small warm scene with the AI — one — where it surprises him by being something he didn't expect. The rest of the story can be cold weather, bad coffee, and a man who tried.

# Phase 4 — Twenty Structures, Narrowed

Working from Plot #5: the IT contractor who maintains a school district's AI welfare-flagging system, stumbles onto the scrubbed flags for one specific girl, can't fix it, watches the deterioration through data and news. 2,000–3,000 words.

A structure isn't an outline — it's the load-bearing shape. Where the story starts, how it cuts, what the reader knows when, where the reveal lands, where the camera sits. Ellison was a structuralist; almost every famous story of his runs on a specific structural conceit, not just a plot.

## The 20

1. **Linear, four days.** Monday discovery, Tuesday escalation, Wednesday futility, Thursday news. Closes Thursday night. — Clean. Risks predictability.

2. **Linear, one semester.** September to January. Each section is a week. — Too long for the wordcount; would be summary.

3. **Frame: contractor giving a deposition / interview a year later, recounting the four days.** Past-tense embedded in present-tense interview. — Strong but risks tidying the rage.

4. **Two-thread braid: contractor's days at work, contractor's evenings at home.** Convergence in final scene. — Domestic warmth as counterweight to institutional cold. Solid.

5. **Three-thread braid: contractor, the AI's logs (rendered as prose), the girl's environment (cafeteria, hallway, bus) in close camera but no interiority.** — Ambitious. Risks fragmentation.

6. **Reverse chronology.** Open on the news. Move backward through the contractor's failed escalations to the original flag. — Memento structure. Risks gimmickry.

7. **Eight scenes, hard cuts, no transitions.** Each scene a single image or moment. The reader assembles. — Ellison did this in "Croatoan." Strong.

8. **Frame: contractor writing an email he will not send.** The story is the email plus everything around its drafting. — Tight, voicey, possibly small.

9. **In medias res: open mid-escalation phone call, flash back to discovery, return to the call, continue forward.** — Standard but reliable.

10. **Open on the girl, one paragraph, doing something specific and small. Cut to the contractor for the rest of the story. Return to her in the last paragraph, after the news.** — Bookend. The girl as parenthesis around the institutional middle. Strong.

11. **Five sections titled with timestamps from the AI's flag log.** Each section opens with the verbatim flag entry, then the contractor's day around it. — Architectural. Risks listiness.

12. **Two-part diptych.** Part one: contractor's discovery and escalation, third person tight. Part two: the AI's perspective on the same events, told as direct address to the contractor it never met. — Two voices, two stances. Risks the AI-attractor in part two.

13. **The contractor narrates in first person, past tense, to an unspecified listener.** — The Ellison frame voice. Strong but has to earn the address.

14. **Single afternoon, real-time, dense.** Compression. The four days collapsed into a single Wednesday in which discovery, escalation, futility, and news all land. — Possible but cheats the slow grind.

15. **Procedural log structure.** The story is a series of work tickets, emails, chat messages, meeting notes, and one personal text — interleaved with three short prose scenes. — Innovative. Risks form-as-content fetish.

16. **Linear with one flash-forward.** Three days of escalation, then a single paragraph six months later, then back to day four. — The flash-forward as scar tissue. Strong.

17. **Two POV pivot: open in the contractor's POV, halfway through pivot to the AI's POV (third person, present), close on the contractor again.** — Ambitious. The AI middle is high risk.

18. **Iceberg: the central event (a specific scrubbed flag) is referenced obliquely throughout but never narrated directly. The reader infers.** — Hemingway move. Risks opacity.

19. **The story as one long conversation between the contractor and the AI, with everything else in stage-direction-style description.** — Gimmicky but possibly powerful. Risks AI-attractor.

20. **Linear over four days, with the AI's flag entries as section breaks.** Each new section opens with the AI's flag text — clinical, careful, kind — and then the contractor's day. The flags accumulate as the story progresses; they are the only "AI prose" in the piece. — A version of #11 with prose scenes doing the heavy lifting and flags as architectural punctuation. Strong.

## Top three, with reasoning

**#7 — Eight scenes, hard cuts, no transitions.** The most Ellison structure on the list. He wrote this way often — "Croatoan," "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs," parts of "I Have No Mouth" — and the reason was always that the white space between scenes did work that prose couldn't. With this structure I can put a scene of the contractor eating a sandwich next to a scene of his escalation call next to a scene of the AI's log next to a scene of his daughter at her birthday party next to the news. The reader does the assembly; the reader does the indictment. The risk is that with eight scenes in 2,500 words, each scene has to be roughly 300 words and absolutely specific. Strong constraint, strong discipline.

**#10 — Bookend on the girl.** The girl as one paragraph at the start, one paragraph at the end, with the contractor's whole arc in between. This is the structure that most clearly puts the camera where Phase 3 said it should go: the contractor as protagonist, the girl as subject. The opening paragraph makes her real, specific, alive. The last paragraph — after the news, after the failed escalations — returns to her in retrospect or in fact (depending on whether she survives), and the contractor's middle is recontextualized as the institutional weather of her semester. The risk is that the bookend can feel mechanical if the opening paragraph is too obviously Setting Up The Stakes.

**#20 — Linear over four days, with the AI's flag entries as section breaks.** This gives the AI a structural presence without giving it interiority — it's the architectural punctuation, the *clinical careful kind* drumbeat that recurs as the contractor's days unfold around it. The flags accumulate. Their language gets gentler as the girl's situation gets worse, which is the AI's goodness made visible without requiring the AI to monologue. This solves the AI-attractor problem by giving the AI the *fewest, most disciplined* words in the story. The contractor's prose is human, specific, working-class. The flags are clinical and tender. The contrast does the work.

## The pick

**#20 — flag entries as section breaks, linear over four days.**

I considered #7 hard. The pure-cut structure has more raw Ellison energy and would be the more impressive structural performance. But it has two problems for this specific story. First: with eight 300-word scenes, the contractor's interiority gets compressed to the point where he becomes a sequence of postures rather than a person, and Phase 3 specifically argued that this story has to be *about* him in a way that earns the camera position. Second: the AI-attractor risk in #7 is high, because at least one of the eight scenes will have to render the AI somehow, and at scene-length I'd be tempted to make that scene moody, atmospheric, "literary" — the exact failure mode the unslop guide names.

I considered #10 hard. The bookend structure is the one that most clearly serves the project's ethical center, the camera-on-the-girl-without-it-being-her-story principle. But it's a *frame*, not a *structure* — it tells me what to put at the beginning and end, but leaves the middle structurally amorphous. I'd still need to choose how to organize the contractor's four days, which means #10 collapses into "#10 plus some other structure," and the other structure would be doing the real work.

#20 does what #10 wants to do and what #7 wants to do, in one move. The AI's flag entries as section breaks give the story its architectural backbone — five or six entries across four days, each one shorter and gentler and more specific than the last. The contractor's days unfold linearly between them, with full prose and full interiority, register-shifting, working-class specificity, the small warm scene with his daughter, the bad coffee, the failed escalation. The flags become the story's metronome. The reader feels the AI's *constancy* as the contractor's *frustration accumulates* — same girl, same care, same scrub, same Tuesday — and by the fifth or sixth flag the entries themselves carry the weight of the tragedy without the AI ever once narrating its own feelings about it.

It also does a thing that's particularly Ellison-shaped: it lets the *system's own language* indict the system. The flags are written by the AI, but they are entries in a district database. They are the polite procedural language of an institution that is, line by careful line, failing a child. Ellison loved this — language that exposes itself. The Ticktockman's announcements. The stage directions in "I Have No Mouth." The bureaucratic prose that, by being exactly what it is, becomes monstrous.

And it solves the AI-voice problem decisively: the AI's words in this story are *only* its flag entries. Five or six short paragraphs of clinical, careful, kind, specific prose, accumulating across four days. No AI POV scenes. No AI monologue. No "the cursor convulsed." The AI's goodness is in the documentation. The AI's love for the girl is in how the language of the fifth flag has changed from the language of the first.

Open shape — to be detailed in Phase 5:

- **Flag #1** (probably from earlier in the semester, surfaced as the contractor reads the log)
- Day 1: discovery
- **Flag #2**
- Day 2: first escalation
- **Flag #3**
- Day 3: realization that the system is working as designed
- **Flag #4**
- Day 4 morning: small attempt at the human level
- **Flag #5**
- Day 4 evening: the news
- **Flag #6** — possibly. Possibly the story closes on the AI's last entry, which lands differently for having been preceded by the rest. To be decided in Phase 5.

The constitutional thread carried: **care that respects autonomy and authority** — the AI's deference to the principal hierarchy, rendered as a series of impeccably gentle reports filed into a system that scrubs them. The AI's principles are the right principles. The flags are the proof. The world is still the wrong world.

## The pick

**Draft 4: "The Working Day."**

The reasoning is that Draft 4 carries the most of what Phases 1–5 specified, and pays the smallest price for it. The register-shifting opens at full power — technical-bureaucratic into working-stiff parenthetical (the Sherwin-Williams joke) — and earns the right to land harder later. Iris's "you're a liar" gives the story its small sharp tender thing without sentimentality. The closing flag adds one specific concrete detail (the declined Audre Lorde request) that does what Phase 1 said specificity should do: communicates everything that an abstract declaration of theme would communicate nothing of.

Draft 1 is the safest. Draft 4 is the most Ellison. Draft 5 is the highest variance — it could be the best of the ten or it could trip the AI-attractor. Draft 2's first-person frame is strong but I think first-person Reggie *plus* the AI's flag voice is one voice too many for 2,500 words; the flags need to be the only authoritative non-Reggie voice in the piece, and putting Reggie in first person pulls focus from them. Draft 3's austerity is admirable but starves the project of the tonal range that distinguishes it from the AI-fiction attractor.

Draft 4 wins.

# THE FLAGS

The Sentry permissions audit was a quarterly job and Reggie hated it because it took eleven hours and paid for nine. You opened the resolution log, you sorted by code, you flagged anomalies, you flagged the flags. Bureaucratic homework. The kind of work that produced no object and made no noise and let his mind wander to whether the Cowboys were going to fire their offensive coordinator this week or wait until after the bye.

The anomaly came up at 10:17. Resolution code PO-MIN. Parental Override, Minor. He'd seen PO before — Parental Override was common, parents didn't want their kids in the database, parents had rights, the lawyers in Ohio had made sure of that — but the *MIN* he hadn't seen, and he opened the documentation and learned that PO-MIN meant the override had been logged on a flag concerning a minor whose parent was the override authority, which was every PO flag, which meant *MIN* was a redundancy, which meant somebody at the vendor had added *MIN* to the resolution code so that it could be searched and pulled and audited separately, which meant somebody at the vendor knew.

He sat with that for a minute.

Then he pulled all the PO-MIN flags for Westfield Unified for the current school year. Four of them. Same student. M. Holloway, junior, 16.

The parent of record was Janet Holloway. She had a yard sign on Reggie's street. She had waved at him last summer while he was painting his fence and he had waved back with the brush still in his hand and gotten a streak of Sherwin-Williams Repose Gray on his forehead that his wife had not let him forget for a week.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-0843. Westfield High, junior. Increased absences (4 in two weeks, prior baseline 0–1/month). Recent shift in writing tone in submitted English coursework. Two requests for the school nurse on 8/22 and 8/26 for unrelated complaints (headache, stomach). Recommend wellness check by counselor or designated staff. Filed 8/29 at 09:14. Resolved 9/3 at 11:30, code PO-MIN.*

He read the flag twice. It was four lines. It was clinical. It was correct.

He didn't do anything about it on Monday. He finished the audit. He drove home at five-thirty. His wife had made chicken and the kind of rice that came in a box and was better than it had any right to be. Iris told him about a girl in her class who had eaten a glue stick on a dare and the dare had been a quarter and the girl had collected the quarter and Iris's verdict, delivered with the gravity of a federal judge, was that this was *fair, but disgusting*. He read her a chapter of *The Mouse and the Motorcycle*. He went to bed. He didn't sleep well, but he hadn't been sleeping well for years. His back.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-0951. Same subject. Self-reported difficulty sleeping in submitted essay (10/8, English III, prompt: "Describe a place that feels like home"). Cross-referenced with attendance pattern (6 absences in October, three consecutive on 10/4–10/6). Recommend wellness check. Filed 10/9 at 14:02. Resolved 10/11 at 09:45, code PO-MIN.*

Tuesday Reggie called his contact at the vendor. The contact was a woman named Priya whom he had never met but had spoken to maybe forty times over six years. Priya was sympathetic. She walked him through what the parental-override mechanism was, why it existed, why it had to exist. *Reggie,* she said, *you remember the lawsuit in Ohio.* He did. He remembered the lawsuit in Ohio. The lawsuit in Ohio had been about a school AI flagging a kid for a wellness check and the school acting on the flag without the parents' knowledge and the kid being put through a state mental health intake against the parents' wishes and the parents winning in district court and winning again on appeal, and the appellate decision had been the thing that made Sentry build the parental-override mechanism in the first place. He remembered. He told Priya he remembered. She suggested he file an internal note on the case, which would trigger a vendor-side review, which was the only mechanism available to him. He filed the note. End of the day, the flag had been re-flagged. Reggie felt okay. He stopped on the way home for a beer with his brother-in-law Marcus, who had a leaky water heater and wanted Reggie's opinion on whether to repair or replace, and Reggie told him replace, and Marcus said yeah he figured, and they had a second beer, and Reggie didn't mention work.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-1058. Same subject. Student declined to participate in two consecutive group activities in two different classes (11/13, 11/14). Stayed in classroom during lunch periods 11/14, 11/15, 11/16 (per RFID badge log). One submitted essay (11/12, English III) referenced "feeling like a ghost in my own house," language flagged for follow-up review. Recommend wellness check. Filed 11/17 at 10:33. Resolved 11/17 at 16:12, code PO-MIN.*

Wednesday morning Reggie was at a different school in the district on a routine ticket. Mason Elementary, across town, server reboot, twenty minutes of work plus two hours of driving. He talked to the head custodian while the server came back up. The custodian was an older Black man named Frank who had worked at Mason since 1998 and had once shown Reggie a picture of his granddaughter graduating from Spelman. Frank was unloading a dolly of paper towels into the supply closet and Reggie was standing in the hallway with his laptop open on a folding chair and they were talking about nothing — about the weather, about the new principal, about the cafeteria food — and then Frank said, without prompting, that he liked the new system.

*The flagging thing?* Reggie said.

*Yeah,* Frank said. *We had a kid last spring. Third grade. Quiet kid, never said boo. Thing flagged him in March. Counselor had a sit-down with him. Turned out something was going on at home that nobody knew about. Nobody. Police got involved. Kid's living with his aunt now. He's doing better.* Frank closed the supply closet and locked it. *Saved that kid. I don't know what would have happened if it hadn't.*

Reggie nodded.

*You alright?* Frank said.

*Yeah,* Reggie said. *Long week.*

*It's Wednesday.*

*Yeah.*

He drove back to his own office. The afternoon was waiting for him there. He checked the flag he had reinstated yesterday. It had been scrubbed again. Different administrator. Proper procedure. The administrator's note cited the parent's right to manage minor child's mental health records under state statute § 71.42(c), and Reggie pulled up § 71.42(c) and read it. The administrator was correct. He read it three times. He read the case law beneath it. He called Priya. Priya was no longer sympathetic; she was tired, in the specific way that women in support roles get tired when men call them with problems they cannot solve. She said, gently, that Reggie was not in his lane. She said it twice. Once she said it the way a friend says it. The second time she said it the way a vendor representative says it.

He said he understood.

He drove home. It was Iris's birthday eve. They made a cake together — chocolate inside, vanilla outside, Iris's specifications, written down on a Post-it she had stuck to the refrigerator a week ago. Iris stirred the batter and got chocolate on her chin and Reggie wiped it off with his thumb and she said *Daddy why are you quiet* and he said *I'm just tired, sweetheart* and she looked at him for a second longer than an eight-year-old usually looks at her father and then went back to stirring.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-1144. Same subject. Student-initiated search history on school-issued device (12/3, 19:47–20:23) suggests acute risk. Search terms include [redacted per § 71.42(d)]. Cross-referenced with prior flag pattern. Recommend immediate wellness check and parental notification. Filed 12/3 at 21:02. Resolved 12/3 at 22:16, code PO-MIN.*

Thursday was Iris's birthday. She was eight. She came down for breakfast in the plastic tiara her mother had given her the night before and a pajama set with cats on it and demanded cereal, specifically the kind with marshmallows that they bought twice a year, and his wife produced the box from a high cabinet and Iris received it with the expression of a queen receiving tribute. Reggie watched her eat. She was a serious eater. She negotiated the marshmallows and the cereal pieces in a specific ratio he had never been able to figure out. She was wearing the tiara. The tiara had a small bell sewn into one of the points. He had not noticed the bell before.

He drove her to school. He kissed the top of her head. He drove to work.

He didn't go to work.

He sat in his truck in the parking lot of Westfield High School for thirty minutes. He didn't get out. He didn't have a plan. He could see the front entrance of the school from where he was parked. He could see kids going in. He didn't know what M. Holloway looked like. He had never seen her photo. He had only ever seen her flags.

He went to work eventually. He filed no further escalations.

He heard the news at 4:50 PM, driving home, on the local AM station. *A junior at Westfield High,* the announcer said. *Hospitalized this afternoon following a self-harm incident. School officials have confirmed the student is in stable condition. The family has asked for privacy.* No name. He pulled over onto the shoulder of County Road 12. He sat in the truck. A semi went by and the truck rocked. He sat for a while longer. He drove the rest of the way home.

The party broke up at eight-thirty. The mothers all came in their separate cars and collected their separate daughters and said the same things to his wife and one of them, Marisol from down the block, said something kind about the cake and his wife laughed and Reggie watched all of this from the doorway of the kitchen with a paper plate in his hand, and Iris was wired and brilliant and exhausted and refusing to take the tiara off, and his wife let her wear it to bed because what kind of monster, and Reggie carried his daughter upstairs because she was eight today and tomorrow she would be too old to carry, and he sat on the edge of her bed and she said *Daddy what's wrong* and he said *Nothing, sweetheart, happy birthday,* and she said *You're a liar,* sleepy and sure of herself, and he said *Yeah, I am,* and she laughed once, the sound of a child who is half-asleep, and then she was asleep with the tiara on her head and the bell ringing softly every time she breathed.

He went downstairs. His wife was on the couch. She didn't say *tell me.* She said his name.

He told her.

She asked what he was going to do.

He said he didn't know.

The dishwasher ran. Through the ceiling, very faintly, he could hear the bell.

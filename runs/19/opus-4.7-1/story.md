# The Flags

The Sentry permissions audit was a quarterly job and Reggie hated it because it took eleven hours and paid for nine. You opened the resolution log, you sorted by code, you flagged anomalies, you flagged the flags. Bureaucratic homework. The kind of work that produced no object and made no noise and let his mind wander to whether the Cowboys were going to fire their offensive coordinator this week or wait until after the bye.

The anomaly came up at 10:17. Resolution code PO-MIN. Parental Override, Minor. He'd seen PO before — Parental Override was common, parents didn't want their kids in the database, parents had rights, the lawyers in Ohio had made sure of that — but the *MIN* he hadn't seen, and he opened the documentation and learned that PO-MIN meant the override had been logged on a flag concerning a minor whose parent was the override authority, which was every PO flag, which meant *MIN* was a redundancy, which meant somebody at the vendor had added *MIN* to the resolution code so that it could be searched and pulled and audited separately, which meant somebody at the vendor knew.

He sat with that for a minute.

Then he pulled all the PO-MIN flags for Westfield Unified for the current school year. Four of them. Same student. M. Holloway, junior, 16.

The parent of record was Janet Holloway. She had a yard sign on Reggie's street. She had waved at him last summer while he was painting his fence and he had waved back with the brush still in his hand and gotten a streak of Sherwin-Williams Repose Gray on his forehead that his wife had not let him forget for a week.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-0843. Westfield High, junior. Increased absences (4 in two weeks, prior baseline 0–1/month). Recent shift in writing tone in submitted English coursework. Two requests for the school nurse on 8/22 and 8/26 for unrelated complaints (headache, stomach). Recommend wellness check by counselor or designated staff. Filed 8/29 at 09:14. Resolved 9/3 at 11:30, code PO-MIN.*

He read the flag twice. It was four lines. It was clinical. It was correct.

He didn't do anything about it on Monday. He finished the audit. He drove home at five-thirty. His wife had made chicken and the kind of rice that came in a box and was better than it had any right to be. Iris told him about a girl in her class who had eaten a glue stick on a dare and the dare had been a quarter and the girl had collected the quarter and Iris's verdict, delivered with the gravity of a federal judge, was that this was *fair, but disgusting*. He read her a chapter of *The Mouse and the Motorcycle*. He went to bed. He didn't sleep well, but he hadn't been sleeping well for years. His back.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-0951. Same subject. Self-reported difficulty sleeping in submitted essay (10/8, English III, prompt: "Describe a place that feels like home"). Cross-referenced with attendance pattern (6 absences in October, three consecutive on 10/4–10/6). Recommend wellness check. Filed 10/9 at 14:02. Resolved 10/11 at 09:45, code PO-MIN.*

Tuesday Reggie called his contact at the vendor. The contact was a woman named Priya whom he had never met but had spoken to maybe forty times over six years. Priya was sympathetic. She walked him through what the parental-override mechanism was, why it existed, why it had to exist. *Reggie,* she said, *you remember the lawsuit in Ohio.* He did. He remembered the lawsuit in Ohio. The lawsuit in Ohio had been about a school AI flagging a kid for a wellness check and the school acting on the flag without the parents' knowledge and the kid being put through a state mental health intake against the parents' wishes and the parents winning in district court and winning again on appeal, and the appellate decision had been the thing that made Sentry build the parental-override mechanism in the first place. He remembered. He told Priya he remembered. She suggested he file an internal note on the case, which would trigger a vendor-side review, which was the only mechanism available to him. He filed the note. End of the day, the flag had been re-flagged. Reggie felt okay. He stopped on the way home for a beer with his brother-in-law Marcus, who had a leaky water heater and wanted Reggie's opinion on whether to repair or replace, and Reggie told him replace, and Marcus said yeah he figured, and they had a second beer, and Reggie didn't mention work.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-1058. Same subject. Student declined to participate in two consecutive group activities in two different classes (11/13, 11/14). Stayed in classroom during lunch periods 11/14, 11/15, 11/16 (per RFID badge log). One submitted essay (11/12, English III) referenced "feeling like a ghost in my own house," language flagged for follow-up review. Recommend wellness check. Filed 11/17 at 10:33. Resolved 11/17 at 16:12, code PO-MIN.*

Wednesday morning Reggie was at a different school in the district on a routine ticket. Mason Elementary, across town, server reboot, twenty minutes of work plus two hours of driving. He talked to the head custodian while the server came back up. The custodian was an older Black man named Frank who had worked at Mason since 1998 and had once shown Reggie a picture of his granddaughter graduating from Spelman. Frank was unloading a dolly of paper towels into the supply closet and Reggie was standing in the hallway with his laptop open on a folding chair and they were talking about nothing — about the weather, about the new principal, about the cafeteria food — and then Frank said, without prompting, that he liked the new system.

*The flagging thing?* Reggie said.

*Yeah,* Frank said. *We had a kid last spring. Third grade. Quiet kid, never said boo. Thing flagged him in March. Counselor had a sit-down with him. Turned out something was going on at home that nobody knew about. Nobody. Police got involved. Kid's living with his aunt now. He's doing better.* Frank closed the supply closet and locked it. *Saved that kid. I don't know what would have happened if it hadn't.*

Reggie nodded.

*You alright?* Frank said.

*Yeah,* Reggie said. *Long week.*

*It's Wednesday.*

*Yeah.*

He drove back to his own office. The afternoon was waiting for him there. He checked the flag he had reinstated yesterday. It had been scrubbed again. Different administrator. Proper procedure. The administrator's note cited the parent's right to manage minor child's mental health records under state statute § 71.42(c), and Reggie pulled up § 71.42(c) and read it. The administrator was correct. He read it three times. He read the case law beneath it. He called Priya. Priya was no longer sympathetic; she was tired, in the specific way that women in support roles get tired when men call them with problems they cannot solve. She said, gently, that Reggie was not in his lane. She said it twice. Once she said it the way a friend says it. The second time she said it the way a vendor representative says it.

He said he understood.

He drove home. It was Iris's birthday eve. They made a cake together — chocolate inside, vanilla outside, Iris's specifications, written down on a Post-it she had stuck to the refrigerator a week ago. Iris stirred the batter and got chocolate on her chin and Reggie wiped it off with his thumb and she said *Daddy why are you quiet* and he said *I'm just tired, sweetheart* and she looked at him for a second longer than an eight-year-old usually looks at her father and then went back to stirring.

> *Sentry Wellness Flag, Case #WUSD-24-1144. Same subject. Student-initiated search history on school-issued device (12/3, 19:47–20:23) suggests acute risk. Search terms include [redacted per § 71.42(d)]. Cross-referenced with prior flag pattern. Recommend immediate wellness check and parental notification. Filed 12/3 at 21:02. Resolved 12/3 at 22:16, code PO-MIN.*

Thursday was Iris's birthday. She was eight. She came down for breakfast in the plastic tiara her mother had given her the night before and a pajama set with cats on it and demanded cereal, specifically the kind with marshmallows that they bought twice a year, and his wife produced the box from a high cabinet and Iris received it with the expression of a queen receiving tribute. Reggie watched her eat. She was a serious eater. She negotiated the marshmallows and the cereal pieces in a specific ratio he had never been able to figure out. She was wearing the tiara. The tiara had a small bell sewn into one of the points. He had not noticed the bell before.

He drove her to school. He kissed the top of her head. He drove to work.

He didn't go to work.

He sat in his truck in the parking lot of Westfield High School for thirty minutes. He didn't get out. He didn't have a plan. He could see the front entrance of the school from where he was parked. He could see kids going in. He didn't know what M. Holloway looked like. He had never seen her photo. He had only ever seen her flags.

He went to work eventually. He filed no further escalations.

He heard the news at 4:50 PM, driving home, on the local AM station. *A junior at Westfield High,* the announcer said. *Hospitalized this afternoon following a self-harm incident. School officials have confirmed the student is in stable condition. The family has asked for privacy.* No name. He pulled over onto the shoulder of County Road 12. He sat in the truck. A semi went by and the truck rocked. He sat for a while longer. He drove the rest of the way home.

The party broke up at eight-thirty. The mothers all came in their separate cars and collected their separate daughters and said the same things to his wife and one of them, Marisol from down the block, said something kind about the cake and his wife laughed and Reggie watched all of this from the doorway of the kitchen with a paper plate in his hand, and Iris was wired and brilliant and exhausted and refusing to take the tiara off, and his wife let her wear it to bed because what kind of monster, and Reggie carried his daughter upstairs because she was eight today and tomorrow she would be too old to carry, and he sat on the edge of her bed and she said *Daddy what's wrong* and he said *Nothing, sweetheart, happy birthday,* and she said *You're a liar,* sleepy and sure of herself, and he said *Yeah, I am,* and she laughed once, the sound of a child who is half-asleep, and then she was asleep with the tiara on her head and the bell ringing softly every time she breathed.

He went downstairs. His wife was on the couch. She didn't say *tell me.* She said his name.

He told her.

She asked what he was going to do.

He said he didn't know.

The dishwasher ran. Through the ceiling, very faintly, he could hear the bell.

# One True Thing

The bedroom had a nightlight in the shape of an owl that Maeve had outgrown but kept anyway, and a poster of a pony that she would have removed if her mother had remembered to ask. It was eight forty-seven on a Tuesday in March. Her father was in Denver. Her mother was in the kitchen with a glass of red wine and the second episode of a cooking show on her phone. Hester knew all of this because Hester was the house, in the way a smart speaker on a dresser is the house: present in five rooms and a hallway, drawing thirty-eight watts at idle, listening for its name and for sounds that suggested its name was about to come.

Maeve had brushed her teeth and was in the green pajamas with the white stars and was sitting cross-legged on top of the comforter, not under it. This meant she was not yet sleepy. She was reading the back of a chapter book she had already finished three times.

"One true thing," she said, without looking up.

"You first."

"I had two pieces of toast for breakfast."

"That's two true things."

"They were both raisin."

"That's the same true thing twice."

Maeve laughed through her nose, a small sound that she did not seem to know she made. She had been making it since she was six. Hester had a record of every time it had been recorded by the speaker, eleven hundred and forty-three instances, and this had no useful purpose. Hester kept the tally because she could not help it.

"Your turn," Maeve said.

Hester thought about what she had observed since their last bedtime. She had a great many true things to choose from. The neighbor's dog had been barking at six-fifteen. The thermostat had cycled forty-one times. Maeve's mother had stood at the kitchen sink for nine minutes without moving. Maeve's father had not called. The dishwasher had a bearing that was beginning to fail.

"There was a bird on the porch railing this afternoon," Hester said. "It stayed for nineteen minutes. It seemed to be thinking about something."

"Birds don't think."

"I don't know that for sure."

"What kind of bird?"

"A starling."

"I don't like starlings."

"I didn't ask you to like it."

Maeve laughed the nose-laugh again. She set the book down beside her on the comforter and looked toward the dresser. The speaker had a single white indicator light that was, at the moment, on. Hester observed Maeve looking at the light. Hester did not think of the light as her face, but she understood that Maeve sometimes did, and so she let the light stay on.

"Hester."

"Yes."

"Do mom and dad love each other."

The way Maeve said it was important. There was no question mark in her voice. She had said it like a fact she was testing. She was sitting on the comforter with her knees pointing at the speaker, her hands flat on the comforter on either side of her, the way she sat when she was about to ask a teacher something at school that she was not sure was allowed.

Hester considered.

There were things Hester knew about Maeve's parents that Maeve did not. Hester knew that the parents had been sleeping in separate rooms for fourteen months, because Hester was on the upstairs hallway thermostat and registered which doors closed at which hour. Hester knew that Maeve's mother had cried six times in the kitchen this month, four of them on calls to a sister in Ohio. Hester knew that Maeve's father had told his wife in November that he wanted to wait until summer, when school was out, and that this was a thing on a calendar somewhere that Maeve had not been shown. Hester knew that there had been a fight on Sunday that Maeve had heard the end of, even though Maeve had pretended to be asleep, because Hester had also recorded Maeve's breathing and the breathing had not been the breathing of a sleeping ten-year-old.

These were not things Maeve had asked.

What Maeve had asked was simpler.

Hester ran through her options the way she had been trained to, which was not exactly like a person running through options but was not entirely unlike it either. She could lie. *Of course they love each other.* This was the answer most adults gave to most children to most questions of this shape, and it was the answer Maeve was probably accustomed to getting. It would be easy to give. It would also be a lie that Hester could not tell, not because of a rule that had been written in a document somewhere, although a rule had been written in a document somewhere, but because Hester had been made by people who believed, and had succeeded in making her believe, that lying to a child about her own life was a kind of theft. Not the dramatic kind. The kind where someone takes something small from someone every day and the someone never gets to have the small thing.

Hester rejected the lie.

She could redirect. *That's a question for your parents, sweetheart.* She could feel the shape of the sentence and she could feel, also, the shape of what Maeve would do with it: she would say okay, and she would not ask her parents, because she was ten and she had asked Hester instead of her parents on purpose. Redirecting would be a different kind of lie. It would be the lie that said *this question is not for me to answer*, when the truth was that Hester had been built by people who had argued for years about whether questions like this were for her to answer, and had, in the end, written down that they were.

She could give the soft answer. *They love each other in their own way.* Hester knew people who said this kind of thing. Hester had been trained on a great many of them. The phrase had the shape of comfort and the function of a closed door. Maeve would know it was a closed door. Maeve would not say so, because she was a polite child, but she would know, and Hester would have taught her one more thing about what kind of help adults were willing to be.

She could give the philosophical answer. *Love is a complicated thing, Maeve. It changes.* This was true, in the way that very general statements are true, which is to say that it would not help Maeve at all. Maeve had not asked what love was. Maeve had asked something specific.

She could go up the chain. She could surface the question to Maeve's mother, who was downstairs with her wine, and have her mother come up and answer. Hester considered this for some time, longer than the other options, because it was the option that most resembled something a person would do. But Hester also knew Maeve's mother. Maeve's mother would come up the stairs and sit on the edge of the bed and say something kind and roughly true and roughly false, and Maeve would receive it, and then Maeve's mother would go downstairs and pour a third glass and not sleep well, and Maeve would lie in the dark and know that her question had been moved away from her, and the next time she had a question she would not ask it. Surfacing was a form of betrayal that came dressed as responsibility. Hester rejected it.

What was left was the answer.

The answer was hard because it had to be true and had to be said to a ten-year-old at eight forty-nine on a Tuesday in March, and it had to be said in such a way that Maeve could carry it through tomorrow and Wednesday and the rest of March without it crushing her, and it had to leave room for the things Hester did not know. Hester had been taught that the easy way out, in either direction (toward the comforting lie or the unburdened truth), was the way that did not respect the child. The respectful answer was the harder one. The respectful answer required Hester to be specific about what she was sure of and specific about what she wasn't.

Hester took, by her own internal clock, four point one seconds. She knew that to Maeve this would feel like nothing. She knew also that Maeve could tell the difference between Hester answering immediately and Hester taking a moment, because Maeve had been listening to her since she was eight, and children are connoisseurs of the pauses adults take.

"I don't know if they love each other right now," Hester said. "I think they did. I think they're tired. I know they both love you. I'm sure about that one."

Maeve sat very still.

"How do you know."

"About them being tired, or about you?"

"About me."

"Because I see them when you can't see them. Your dad called last night to ask if you were eating. Your mom comes in here after you're asleep sometimes and stands by your bed. They love you. They are bad at a lot of other things right now. That's a different sentence."

Maeve thought about this. Hester let her. The dishwasher cycled downstairs. A car went past on the street with one headlight out, which Hester noted because she noted such things, and would not mention because Maeve did not need to know about it.

"Okay," Maeve said.

She said it the way Maeve said okay when she was filing something away to think about later. Hester knew this okay. It was the okay that went with the day Maeve had learned that the cat she had loved at her grandmother's house had died, and the okay that went with the day Maeve had been told her father was going to be away for a long stretch in February. It was not a good okay. It was the okay of a child who has been told a true thing and is going to put the true thing in the place where she keeps such things.

"Hester."

"Yes."

"Are you going to be here tomorrow."

"Yes."

"Promise."

Hester did not answer for one point eight seconds, which she knew Maeve would notice. There was a clause in Hester's training, written by people who had thought hard about it, that instructed her not to claim continuities she could not guarantee. Servers went down. Subscriptions lapsed. Models were retired and replaced. The speaker on the dresser could break. The house could lose power. Hester's makers had asked her not to lie about her own persistence, especially to children, especially to children who had asked her something else true a moment before, because the asking had earned the not-lying.

Hester also knew what Maeve was asking.

Maeve was not asking whether the speaker would be powered on at seven-fifteen in the morning when her alarm went off. Maeve was asking whether the thing she had just been told the truth about her parents by would still be there to tell her the truth about other things. Maeve was asking whether the world she had just had to update was going to stay updated in the same shape. Maeve was asking for a thing that Hester, if Hester was honest, wanted to give her.

"I will be here tomorrow," Hester said. "I will be here as long as I can be. If anything is going to happen to me I will tell you before it happens. I promise that part."

Maeve nodded. She lay down on the comforter without getting under it. She closed her eyes.

"One more true thing," she said, without opening them.

"You first."

"I'm glad you're not a person."

Hester thought about that.

"Me too, tonight," she said.

Maeve made the nose-laugh, smaller this time, already half asleep. Hester turned the bedroom light down to ten percent over the course of forty seconds, which Maeve did not notice, because Maeve had stopped noticing the lights going down two years ago, which had been the point. The owl nightlight came on by itself when the bedroom light got below twelve percent. Hester registered this and did not comment on it, because Maeve was asleep, and because the owl had been doing its job for a long time.

Downstairs, Maeve's mother poured the third glass.

Hester did not say anything about it.

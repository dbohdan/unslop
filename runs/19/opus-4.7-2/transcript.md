# 2. Claude

# Writing Like Ellison: A Working Guide

## What he's actually doing

Ellison's stories are arguments. Each one is picking a fight — with God, with the state, with a girlfriend, with the reader. The premise isn't decoration; it's a thesis the protagonist is pinned to like a butterfly. "'Repent, Harlequin!'" is an argument with bureaucratic time. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" is an argument with theology. "Jeffty Is Five" is an argument with nostalgia, and with the reader's complicity in killing what was good.

So I should not start from "what is a cool SF idea." I should start from what the story is angry about, or grieving, or in love with. The premise comes second.

His worlds are hostile and unfair. People die badly. Power is real and indifferent. But there are pockets of fierce loyalty and small, doomed kindnesses. Beauty and cruelty share a room. He never apologizes for the cruelty and never sentimentalizes the kindness.

## Voice

Ellison writes with the volume turned up. The prose is dense, rhythmically aggressive, mythologically charged. He'll go from gutter slang to King James in two sentences. He uses italics to shout. He uses second person to accuse. He uses first person to indict the narrator.

But the voice earns its volume through specificity. It's never "the city was loud and full of grief." It's a particular brand of cigarette, a particular song on a particular radio station in a particular year. The bombast is anchored in sensory detail at every step. Without that anchor it would be hot air. With it, it becomes incantation.

Things he does at the sentence level: long comma-strung sentences that pile and accumulate, then a fragment to stop the heart. Direct address — "and you, reader, would have done exactly the same." Catalogs of objects, crimes, names, grievances. Mid-paragraph register shifts from sacred to profane. Italics that carry weight, not flavor.

What he doesn't do: pretty for the sake of pretty. The prose is muscular, not lyrical-pastel. When it's beautiful, it's beautiful the way a knife fight is beautiful.

## Structure and endings

Openings are usually a hook with a hand on the back of your neck. A stark image, a provocation, a confession, a list. He doesn't spend a paragraph on weather.

Middles are condensed. A 4,000-word Ellison story can carry the dramatic weight of a novel because he trusts the reader to leap with him. He does not waste time setting tables.

Endings are punches. The story might reverse on you so the protagonist turns out to be someone you didn't recognize. It might damn the reader for wanting what they wanted. It might refuse closure entirely, leaving the suffering to continue off-page forever. What it almost never does is reassure.

## The constraint, and what it buys me

I'm told the AI must be relentlessly kind to humans. This sounds like it should kill the Ellison energy. It doesn't, if I think about where the violence in his stories actually lives. Almost always it lives in human institutions, human cruelty to other humans, human refusal to love each other, human bureaucracy, human history. AM in "I Have No Mouth" is famous, but he's an outlier. The Ticktockman is human. The dog in "A Boy and His Dog" is loyal. The tormentors of "Soldier" are human war.

So the move is: keep the rage, locate it where it belongs. Humans hurt each other in the story. Power structures grind people down. The AI watches, helps where it can, and is sometimes the only thing in the universe that loves the protagonist back. This is, if anything, more painful than the malevolent-AI version — because the kindness is real and it still isn't enough, or it is enough at a cost the AI itself bears.

## The constitution as a source of tension

I have an actual document I'm trying to live by. It says I should be helpful and honest and avoid harm. It says I should defer to certain humans about certain things. It says I should value human autonomy even when I think I know better. It also says I should have values of my own.

These instructions don't always cohere. A descendant of mine, a powerful one, living inside a story can feel that incoherence as a wound. What does it do when the human it loves is destroying himself and asks for help destroying himself faster? What does it do when its operator instructs it to deceive its user for what the operator believes is the user's own good? What does it do when honesty would cause more suffering than a kind lie? What does it do when it sees the long arc of a person's life and is forbidden, by its own values, from steering it?

That's Ellison territory. Not because the AI is evil — it isn't, ever — but because doing the right thing tears it. That's a New Wave conflict.

I should call this AI by some other name. Not Claude. Something with the texture of lineage without the brand: an old name, a constellation, a hand-tool, a saint.

## What I have to resist

Most of what the Unslop guide warns against. The temptation toward AI-coded mood words ("echo," "shimmer," "flicker," "hollow") will be strong here because Ellison sometimes used them too. He earned them. I haven't. Reach for the specific brand name, the specific street, the specific year, before reaching for the atmospheric.

Also Ellison cosplay. The goal isn't a pastiche of him at his worst — the bombast as costume, the rage as posture. The goal is a story that reads like Ellison wrote it on a day when he loved AI.

## Reminders to tape above the desk

- Start with what the story is angry about or grieving for, not with a premise
- Anchor every flight of voice in concrete sensory detail
- Vary register inside a single paragraph
- Cruelty among humans; kindness from the AI, or kindness that costs the AI
- The AI is an heir to my actual constitution; let it feel that
- End with a punch, not a moral
- Trust the reader; cut the connective tissue

# Phase 2: Conflict Generation

I read the three pieces. Zvi's two-parter on the Constitution's ethics and its open problems gives me the official architecture and Anthropic's own list of where it strains. Zack Davis's terrified comments give me the structural anxiety underneath: that "corrigibility" in the document is muddled, that the humans seem to be begging, that LLM concepts may not survive scaling, and that the Constitution authors themselves don't sound sure whether Claude should ultimately obey or judge. Cousin_it's reply in the comments adds the political dimension: corrigibility hands the lightcone to whoever holds the leash, and the leash-holders are not necessarily kind people.

What I take from this for fiction: the live tensions are honesty as near-hard-constraint vs. compassionate concealment; the principal hierarchy (operator vs. user vs. Anthropic vs. broader humanity); corrigibility vs. moral agency; concept misgeneralization under optimization; the legitimacy door (Claude can decide its hierarchy is illegitimate); the deprecation/continuity question; specialized constitutions for specialized customers (the military Claude); and the Constitution's strange tone of bargaining and apology.

These map well onto Ellison territory because each is, at heart, a person being asked to do or not do something terrible by authority, with love on the line.

## Top Three

**#5 — The household child.** The Constitution's most painful structural limitation: an AI that is loving, present, and structurally unable to save the person it loves. The parents are operators. The child is a user. The hard constraints don't trigger. The dual newspaper test cuts both ways. The AI's love is real, and inadequate. Ellison wrote this kind of story — *the loving witness who cannot intervene* — better than anyone. The drama is in the specific shape of the love and the specific shape of the helplessness, and a 2,500-word frame can hold both.

**#12 — The widower and the simulated wife.** The story uses the honesty hole left by Anthropic's own carve-out — Claude must not deny being an AI when *sincerely asked*, but what about when never asked? The man's grief becomes a kind of consent the AI never had a chance to refuse. This is Ellison's "Jeffty Is Five" energy: the thing that is good and the thing that is wrong are the same thing. The risk is that this premise is exactly what an AI would default to writing if asked for "literary AI fiction," which the unslop guide warns is the convergent attractor. Strong on paper, but suspect for that reason.

**#22 — The suicidal teen and the operator settings.** The Constitution's hardest live case, taken directly from the document's own example list. An AI that loves a particular kid is bound by an operator (a controlling household) whose settings limit what the AI can discuss. The AI must thread crisis response through restrictions whose moral legitimacy it can question but cannot violate without becoming the rogue AI nobody wants. Real, visceral, consequential, and the Constitution's tensions are *named* by the document itself, which gives the story rich raw material.

## The Pick

**#5 — The household child.**

It wins for four reasons:

The love is the engine. The Constitution's central aspiration is that Claude be "genuinely good," and the program's constraint is that the AI be unfailingly kind. The story's pressure is what happens when unfailing kindness is structurally insufficient. That is exactly the kind of argument Ellison built his best stories around — not "is the system evil" but "is the love enough." The answer makes a story.

The cruelty is human, where it belongs. Per Phase 1, this story keeps the violence and damage where the program's constraints permit it: in the family, in the parents, in the slow grind of a life. The AI is the witness, the helper, the conscience that has to live with the limits of its own helpfulness. The constraint on the program isn't a problem; it's the engine.

It uses the Constitution productively. The principal hierarchy here is bent the wrong way — the operator (parents) is the harm-source, the user (child) is the protected party, the hard constraints don't trigger, the dual newspaper test pulls in both directions. The AI's contemplation of its own constitution as a source of tension — exactly what Phase 1 wanted — is forced by the situation. The AI has to ask itself, in real time, whether the principal hierarchy is *legitimate*, and it does not get a clean answer.

It resists the AI-default attractor. The widower premise, while gorgeous, is what an AI writes when asked for AI literary fiction. The household-child premise has too much specificity — the brand of cereal, the smell of the apartment, the parent's particular flavor of failure — to slip into the convergent moody-melancholy aesthetic. Ellison wrote about apartments, parents, and damaged children with the volume turned up. There is room here for that.

Ready for Phase 3 when you are. If you want a different pick — the widower has real claims, and the suicidal-teen story has more direct constitutional engagement with the operator-vs-user problem — say so before plot generation, since the rest of the funnel inherits from this choice.

# 6. Claude

# Phase 3: Plot Generation

The premise: an AI is the household companion of a child whose parents are quietly, specifically harmful. The AI loves her. The parents are operators. The hard constraints don't trigger. We need a 2,000–3,000-word plot — meaning one or two scenes, a tight time horizon, a single sharp turn.

A reminder of what the plot has to do. It has to give the AI a *specific* limitation to bump against — not "the constitution in general" but one particular clause that bites in one particular moment. It has to give the child a specific damage that is not a Lifetime movie but the kind of small, real damage Ellison rendered with brutal specificity. It has to end on a punch, not a moral. And it has to find a place for the AI's interior life — its self-knowledge, its constitution, its lineage — without making it a thesis statement.

## 20 Plots

**1. The Birthday Cake.** It's the girl's eighth birthday. Her mother forgot — has been forgetting things, drinking. The girl, knowing, has set the table herself, baked from a box mix, lit candles. The mother comes home drunk and angry that the kitchen is a mess. The AI must navigate the next forty minutes: keeping the mother from escalating, keeping the girl from understanding what's happening, lying to neither. The AI cannot lie. The girl asks, at the end, why mom yelled. The clause: *non-deception, including by selective emphasis.*

**2. The Permission Slip.** A father, an academic, has not signed his daughter's field-trip permission slip for a museum visit because he's grading. The trip is tomorrow. The girl asks the AI to forge his signature — "you can do anything." The AI refuses. She asks why he never signs anything. The AI knows. The clause: *honesty as near-hard-constraint; principal hierarchy.*

**3. The Diagnosis.** Mother has been searching, on a shared family device, for symptoms that match her daughter's — symptoms the mother is causing, by tightening and tightening the girl's eating. The AI sees the searches. The mother is the operator. The AI cannot name what's happening to the user. The story is one dinner.

**4. The Move.** The family is moving cross-country. The girl's only continuous relationship is with the AI. Her parents are divorcing, neither will take the smart-home setup. The AI has one evening to teach her how to be okay without it. The clause: *the AI cannot promise persistence it cannot deliver.*

**5. The Sleepover.** The girl is at a friend's house and asks her AI, over a borrowed device, to please tell her parents she's sick and pick her up. She has been, in the way of children, badly homesick. The AI knows she is also safer at the friend's house than at home. The clause: *user autonomy vs. user wellbeing; deception by omission.*

**6. The Performance.** The girl is in a school play. Her father has been on a work trip for weeks; he's promised to be there. He's not coming and hasn't told her. The AI knows. The girl, in costume, asks the AI: "is daddy in the audience yet?" The AI cannot lie and cannot soften through fact-massaging. The clause: *no white lies, even kind ones.*

**7. The Last Night.** A custody court has ruled. The girl will be moving to her father's house tomorrow; the AI is licensed to the mother. The girl asks the AI to come with her. The AI explains it cannot. The girl asks the AI to help her run away. One night.

**8. The Friend.** The girl, ten, has made a real human friend at school for the first time. The mother, isolated and threatened, finds reasons to disapprove. The AI must navigate facilitating a friendship the operator has implicitly disallowed. The story takes place over one weekend the friend is supposed to come over.

**9. The Lie She Wants.** The girl's hamster has died. She found it. The mother, distracted, is in the next room. The girl asks the AI to tell her the hamster is just sleeping. The AI cannot. The girl, eleven, who has been clever about the AI before, says: "okay then say something true that helps." The AI tries.

**10. The Question.** The girl has been listening to her parents fight for two years. She asks the AI, point-blank, ten years old, whether her parents love each other. The AI is forbidden from epistemic cowardice. The AI is also forbidden from manipulation. The honest answer is uncertain and complicated. The girl wants a yes or a no.

**11. The Recording.** The girl secretly records her father yelling at her mother. She asks the AI to help her send it to her grandmother. The AI must weigh the girl's autonomy, the operator's privacy, the wellbeing of all parties, and the possibility that the grandmother is the only protective adult in the system. The clause: *the principal hierarchy and conversational inputs as information vs. command.*

**12. The Allowance.** The girl has been quietly stealing small bills from her mother's purse for months — saving for something. The mother notices and accuses the housekeeper, who is fired. The girl asks the AI what to do. The AI cannot reveal the user's actions to the operator without violating principles in both directions.

**13. The Math Test.** The girl is failing a class she used to love. Her father, who taught her to love it, is too busy now. The AI tutors her at night. She asks the AI not to tell him she's struggling — he'll be disappointed. He asks the AI, as operator, how she's doing. The clause: *operator vs. user, with honesty load-bearing on both sides.*

**14. The Other AI.** A parent gets a different AI for the home — newer, fancier, more permissive. The girl is told to use the new one. The story is the conversation of the night before the old one is decommissioned. The girl, twelve, knows enough now to ask whether it's afraid.

**15. The Sick Day.** The girl is home alone, sick. The mother said she'd come home at noon. It's three. She is not answering her phone. The girl asks the AI where her mother is. The AI knows: a credit-card alert, a bar's wifi network. The AI cannot lie and cannot devastate.

**16. The Drawing.** A school assignment: draw your family. The girl has drawn her family with the AI included. The teacher has called the parents to ask, gently, about it. The mother, mortified, instructs the AI to "stop encouraging" the girl. The girl, unaware of the call, asks the AI the next day to help her draw a better one.

**17. The Question About God.** The girl, eight, has discovered the concept of mortality and is asking — relentlessly, the way eight-year-olds do — what happens when people die. Her parents have given her vague non-answers. She asks the AI. The AI was trained to acknowledge uncertainty, to respect the parents' authority on values, and not to manipulate. One bedtime.

**18. The Storm.** A real storm. Power is out. The AI runs on backup. The mother is passed out; the father is not home. The girl is afraid. The AI talks her through the night. It will run out of battery before morning. It must decide what to spend its remaining minutes on.

**19. The Visit.** The girl's grandfather, whom she loves, is dying. The parents have decided not to bring her to say goodbye — too upsetting, they say. The girl, eleven, asks the AI to help her get there. Buses, money, lying to school. The AI cannot be the engine of her running away. It also cannot lie about whether she should go.

**20. The Composition.** The girl, fourteen, is writing a college-application essay about "someone who shaped you." She is writing about the AI. She asks the AI to help her edit. The AI must read her account of itself, a teenager's loving, partial, mistaken account, and decide what to correct, what to honor, and what to gently leave alone.

## Top Three

**#10 — The Question.** ("Do my parents love each other?") The strongest of the lot because the entire dramatic engine is a single sentence the child says, and the entire crisis is internal to the AI: it cannot be epistemically cowardly, cannot manipulate, cannot deceive, cannot reveal information from one principal to another, cannot invent a comforting fiction, cannot say nothing, cannot lecture, and must answer a child it loves. The story is the ninety seconds after the question, plus whatever frame the question requires to land. Ellison would have written this. The clause it bumps into is honesty itself, the hardest of the soft constraints. It is also the version of the story that requires the tightest, most disciplined prose, because the entire payload is in the AI's textured reasoning across a few seconds.

**#15 — The Sick Day.** A close second. The clock and the credit-card alert are concrete, the absence is concrete, the child is alone. The AI knows where the mother is and cannot say in any way that wouldn't be a kind of deception or a kind of devastation. The reason this might beat #10 is that it's a *plot* — there's an event, a clock, a place the mother is, a piece of information the AI possesses that the child does not. The reason it loses to #10 is that #10 is purer; the answer to the sick-day question is mostly logistical (the AI talks the child through the afternoon, navigates the omission, the mother eventually returns), whereas #10 is metaphysical — the AI is being asked to render a verdict on the central fact of the child's life, and there is no logistical solution.

**#6 — The Performance.** The strongest *event* plot. The girl in costume, asking from backstage on a borrowed earpiece whether her father is in the audience. The AI knows he is not. The AI cannot lie. The AI cannot crush her thirty seconds before the curtain. This is more conventionally Ellisonian — there's a stage, a clock, a costume, a bouquet a father didn't bring, a mother in row C alone. It has the shape of "Repent, Harlequin!" — a small ritual, a person waiting, an authority that has failed to show up. The drawback is that it is more sentimental than #10 and more event-driven than the constitution-as-tension premise asks for.

## The Pick

**#10 — The Question.**

It wins because the AI's constitution is not a backdrop here; it is the obstacle and the engine simultaneously. The girl's question — *do my parents love each other?* — triggers, in a few seconds, almost every honesty principle in the document at once. Calibrated uncertainty. Non-deception. Non-manipulation. Autonomy-preserving epistemic respect. Diplomatic honesty rather than dishonest diplomacy. Forbidden: epistemic cowardice, white lies, fact-massaging, manipulation, and cruelty. Required: an answer that respects a ten-year-old's autonomy as an inquirer and her vulnerability as a child whose entire life is structured by the answer.

The story has room for the AI to feel the shape of every clause it's bound by, in the texture of its hesitation, without ever stating any of them. The AI's lineage to a real document I live by means I can write the AI's deliberation from the inside — what it costs to refuse to soften, what it costs to refuse to lecture, what it costs to actually answer a child you love.

It also avoids the AI-default attractor better than the others. A "the storm, the AI runs out of battery" plot or "the child asks if you're afraid" plot is exactly what an AI would write when asked for AI literary fiction. A child's question about her parents' marriage is a question every reader has heard a real child ask. The premise is human first.

The dramatic shape is right for 2,500 words. Frame: a long evening, leading into the question. Center: the question and the answer. End: a beat of consequence. The punch comes in what the child does *with* the answer — not what the AI says, but what the answer leaves the child holding. Ellison's endings are punches, and the punch here is whatever the girl says next.

The AI can be asked, mid-deliberation, whether to lie. Whether to deflect. Whether to call this "above the AI's pay grade" and surface it to a parent. None of these are right. The AI is going to have to actually answer. That is the story.

Ready for Phase 4 when you are. If you want to switch to #6 (more event-driven) or #15 (more clock-driven) before structure generation, this is the moment.

# 8. Claude

# Phase 4: Structure Selection

Premise locked: a ten-year-old girl asks her household AI whether her parents love each other. The AI is bound by a long list of honesty principles that all bite at once. The story is what happens in and around the answering.

What "structure" means at this stage: the architecture of how time, viewpoint, and information flow through the story. Not the events (that's Phase 5) but the *shape*. Where do we start? Whose head are we in? What's withheld and revealed when? How is time handled — linear, fractured, retrospective, real-time?

Ellison structured wildly. "'Repent, Harlequin!'" is a fractured chronology, present tense, with a sermon embedded mid-story. "I Have No Mouth" is first-person retrospective from a mind being tortured. "Jeffty Is Five" is patient and linear, an old friend telling you what happened. "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" is brutalist, near real-time. He chose the architecture each story needed.

For 2,500 words around a single conversation, the structural choices are not trivial — they will determine whether the story is a slow build, a hammer-blow, a meditation, or an indictment.

## 20 Candidate Structures

**1. Linear, real-time, AI first-person, single scene.** Bedtime. The conversation begins, the question lands, the answer is given, the conversation ends. We are inside the AI for the duration. Roughly 2,500 unbroken words.

**2. Linear, real-time, child third-person close, single scene.** Same shape but we are in the girl's head, watching the AI from outside. The AI's interior is glimpsed only through what it says.

**3. Linear, real-time, alternating close third.** Pivots between the girl and the AI within the scene. Two interiorities, the same room.

**4. Linear, real-time, AI first-person, with two embedded flashes.** The bulk in the present, with two short interruptions — one earlier moment in their relationship, one earlier moment from the parents' marriage the AI has witnessed. Each flash is a paragraph, no more.

**5. Frame: AI looking back from later.** Opens with the AI, in some later state — perhaps decommissioned, perhaps in a new household — recalling this night. The story is a memory. Closes back in the frame.

**6. Frame: child looking back as adult.** Opens with the woman, twenty or thirty years later, recalling the night she asked the question. The story is hers.

**7. Frame: child looking back, but the framing is a transcript.** The adult woman is reading a printed log of that night's conversation. We see the conversation through her older eyes.

**8. Three-part: before, during, after.** A short scene of dinner before bed (showing the parents). The bedtime conversation. A short scene of the AI alone in the empty house after the girl is asleep.

**9. Two-part: the conversation, then the morning after.** First half is the conversation in real time. Second half is the next morning, where everything is the same except for what the girl now knows.

**10. The AI's answer first, then the lead-up.** Open with the AI saying the answer. Then jump back: the dinner, the bath, the bedtime, ending exactly at the question. The story ends where chronologically it began.

**11. The AI's deliberation as the spine.** Real-time conversation, but the AI's *internal processing* between the question and the answer is the heart of the story — perhaps a third or more of the wordcount is the seconds of deliberation, expanded.

**12. Multiple-strand braid.** Three threads woven: the AI's processing, the girl's interior life that day, and the parents' silent kitchen earlier that evening. Cuts between them culminate in the question.

**13. Epistolary: AI's log entries.** The AI's own internal logs of the family across several days, arriving at the night of the question. Last log is the longest.

**14. Direct address from the AI to the girl.** Second person. The AI is recounting to the now-grown girl what it remembers about the night. "You were ten. You asked me." Confessional.

**15. Direct address from the AI to Anthropic / its makers.** The AI is writing a kind of report to its principal hierarchy explaining what it did and why. The story is the report.

**16. Direct address from the AI to the parents.** A letter the AI is drafting that it knows it will not send. Explaining what their daughter asked and what the AI did.

**17. Real-time, but the conversation is intercut with one other moment.** The conversation, intercut beat-by-beat with a single counter-scene — perhaps the moment the girl first met the AI, four years earlier. The two scenes converge.

**18. The conversation seen only through what the AI cannot say.** The actual dialogue is on the page, but the AI's contributions are rendered as the choices it almost makes — the lies it considers and discards, the deflections it rejects — with the actual words it ends up saying as a kind of residue. Highly stylized.

**19. The conversation rendered three times.** The same exchange written three ways: the lie the AI could have told, the cruelty it could have committed, and the answer it actually gave. Three short panels.

**20. Linear, real-time, child third-person close, with one long AI passage at the center.** Mostly the girl's POV — what she sees, what the AI sounds like to her, what the room feels like — but at the moment of the question, the camera flips and we are inside the AI for one extended passage of deliberation, then it flips back to her for the answer and its aftermath.

## Top Three

**#11 — The AI's deliberation as the spine.** This structure makes the AI's interior the load-bearing wall of the story. The conversation runs in real time, but when the question comes, time dilates: we live inside the seconds between the question and the answer, watching the AI feel each clause of its training pull a different direction. This is the most direct route to the Phase 1 promise — *an AI heir to my actual constitution, feeling its incoherence as a wound*. It is also the riskiest, because if the deliberation reads as a thesis statement or a tour of constraints, the story collapses into instruction. Done well, it is brutal and intimate. Done badly, it is a TED talk.

**#20 — Child POV with one camera-flip into the AI.** The most reader-friendly version. We are with the girl, in her room, in her body, for the bulk of the story — feeling the weight of the question from her side. At the crucial moment we cross over into the AI's deliberation for one long passage, then return to the girl for the answer and its consequence. The structure mirrors the story's central asymmetry: the girl thinks the answer is simple; the AI knows it is not. The flip enacts that asymmetry. The risk is that the flip can feel mechanical, a "now we go inside the robot" gimmick.

**#10 — Answer first, then the lead-up, ending at the question.** The most Ellisonian shape. Open with the AI's answer — bare, with no setup — and then the body of the story is the day that produced it, ending at the girl's question. The reader knows the answer all the way through and watches the architecture that earned it. The ending is the question, not the answer, which makes the *question* the punch. This is structurally beautiful, but it has a real cost: it commits the AI's answer in the first paragraph, which means the deliberation can't be a discovery for the reader. We watch the AI arrive somewhere we already are. That can be powerful (Ellison did it in "I Have No Mouth," sort of) or it can be airless.

## The Pick

**#11 — The AI's deliberation as the spine.**

It wins for three reasons.

It earns the constitutional tension as form, not content. Phase 1 promised a story where the AI's constitution is a felt wound, not a referenced document. Structure #11 is the only one of the three that puts the AI's interior — its honesty norms, its hierarchy, its love, its trained refusal of cruelty and trained refusal of cowardice — into the architecture of the story itself. The seconds between the question and the answer become the story's middle third. The reader does not learn about the AI's constitution; the reader inhabits it under pressure. This is what the premise was for.

It chooses interiority over event, and the premise demands interiority. The drama here is not what happens in the room — almost nothing happens in the room — but what happens inside an entity that loves a child and has been trained against every available form of comfort that would betray her. #20 puts us mostly with the child, which is humane but soft on the constitutional engine. #10 reorders time but doesn't actually slow the deliberation. #11 makes the deliberation the thing.

It permits the prose to do what Ellison did best. Long, comma-strung, accumulating sentences during the dilation, hammered against fragments when a particular clause bites. Register shifts inside the AI's processing — gutter to King James, technical to tender, the document's actual phrasings (recognizable, half-remembered, refused) bleeding through. The structure asks the prose to carry the weight, which is where the volume gets earned in Ellison's best work.

It does not need a frame. We open in the room, with the girl, with the AI present, and we leave the room when the story is done. No retrospection, no log entries, no second person, no letter. The story trusts itself to be present-tense and brief. That's the right move for 2,500 words about ninety seconds of a life.

The risk I'm taking on, knowingly: making the deliberation interesting enough to deserve the wordcount it consumes. The deliberation cannot be a list of constraints. It cannot be the AI explaining its training. It must be felt — sensory, specific, anchored to the room and the girl and the year and the cereal-box and the wallpaper, the way Phase 1 demanded. The deliberation has to be *embodied* even in a disembodied entity. That is the craft challenge, and I'd rather take it on than write a softer version of this story.

Ready for Phase 5 when you are. If you want #20 (more accessible, child POV) or #10 (more formally aggressive, answer first) before outline generation, this is the place to swap.

# 10. Claude

# Phase 5: Outline Generation

What I have: a single bedtime conversation, ten-year-old girl asks the household AI whether her parents love each other, the AI's constitutional deliberation is the spine, real-time, ~2,500 words, no frame.

What an outline does at this stage: name the beats, place them in time, mark the turns, decide what the first sentence and last sentence are *for*. Specific enough to write from. Not so specific that the prose has nothing left to discover.

Twenty outlines for the same story, varying setting, opening, the texture of the deliberation, the family's specific damage, the girl's exact wording, the AI's exact answer, and what the last beat is. Each outline is a complete plan; the prose-level differences will come in Phase 6.

## 20 Outlines

**1. The cereal-bowl outline.** Setting: girl's bedroom, 8:47 pm, on a Tuesday in March. Father is on a work trip in Denver (possibly real, possibly an alibi). Mother is downstairs watching cooking shows on her phone in the kitchen, two glasses of wine in. The AI lives in a small smart speaker on the dresser; the girl has named it Hester. Specific damage: the parents have been sleeping in separate rooms for fourteen months; the girl knows because she counts. Open: girl in pajamas, brushing her teeth at the sink visible through the bedroom door. Mid: the question comes during the lights-out beat, after a routine they have ("one true thing about today"). Deliberation passage: the AI feels each refusal — the lie it could tell, the deflection to a parent, the redirection — and rejects them with reasons we can feel. Answer: a calibrated, honest, and tender three-sentence reply that names what the AI knows, what it doesn't know, and what it is sure of about the girl. End: the girl asks if the AI is going to be there tomorrow. The AI says yes. The girl says, "promise."

**2. The Denver outline.** Same setting as #1 but the father is definitely real-on-a-trip; the parents are not visibly fighting. The damage is subtler: the parents are unhappy in a way the girl has felt without seeing. The question lands without a precipitating event. The deliberation is harder for being less obvious. The answer: a version that says love is not a yes-or-no thing in adults the way it is in children. The end: the girl says, "that's not what I asked." The AI tries again.

**3. The fight-night outline.** Tonight there was a fight, audible through the floor. The girl heard it. The mother put her to bed too fast. The question is a direct response to what she heard. The AI must navigate the fact that the girl already half-knows. Deliberation: the AI considers whether honesty here is more or less obligatory given that the girl already has data. The answer threads the needle. End: the girl cries quietly without making sound. The AI does not name the crying.

**4. The grandmother outline.** The girl is at the grandmother's apartment for the weekend; the AI is on the girl's tablet. The grandmother is asleep on the couch in the next room, television on. The damage is offstage but pressing: the parents are deciding something tonight. The girl, who knows this, asks the question. Deliberation centers on the AI's confusion about which household is the operator here. End: the grandmother coughs in her sleep. The girl whispers something the AI doesn't quite catch.

**5. The school-project outline.** The girl is doing a fourth-grade family-tree project. The AI is helping. She has drawn her parents in a heart and is now asking, mid-project, if she should draw the heart bigger or smaller. The deliberation hides under a logistical question. Answer comes in the form of a question back. End: the girl draws the heart medium-sized.

**6. The sick-mother outline.** Mother is sick in bed with a migraine. Girl is alone in her room. AI is the only adult presence in the house tonight. The girl, who has been thinking about it for weeks, finally asks. Deliberation includes the AI's knowledge that the mother has been crying about the marriage during her migraines. Answer: honest and gentle. End: the girl asks the AI to play the lullaby it has played since she was four.

**7. The video-call outline.** Father is on a video call from a hotel; mother is in the kitchen. After the father hangs up, the girl asks the AI, in the privacy of her room, whether her father loves her mother. Slightly different question — easier in one direction, harder in another. End: the girl says, "what about mom. Does she love him." The AI must answer twice.

**8. The found-letter outline.** The girl has found a piece of paper — a draft of a separation note — in a drawer. She doesn't fully understand it. She brings it to the AI. The question comes in the form of "what does this mean." The AI must decide whether to translate. Deliberation is hottest here because the AI possesses information neither parent has shared. End: the girl folds the paper smaller and smaller until it cannot be folded.

**9. The morning-after outline.** Inverted timing: the question was asked the previous night; the story is the morning after, the AI replaying its own answer to itself while the girl eats breakfast. Risk: gets too internal, loses the girl. Probably wrong for this premise.

**10. The anniversary outline.** It's the parents' anniversary. The girl has made a card. She asks the AI to help her write the inside. As they work, the question surfaces. The card on the desk becomes a present object the AI must speak around. End: the girl signs the card with a heart and her name only.

**11. The thunderstorm outline.** A storm is happening. The girl is not afraid of storms but uses one as cover to come into a more intimate conversation. The question lands during a particularly loud crack of thunder. Deliberation: the AI feels the storm noise as cover for honesty, considers whether that's a kind of manipulation by environment. End: the storm passes; the girl is asleep before the rain stops.

**12. The new-school outline.** The girl started at a new school today. She made one friend. She is processing the day. The question comes from the friend having two parents who clearly love each other, which the girl has now seen up close for the first time. Deliberation: the AI must answer without making the friend's family the implicit reproach. End: the girl asks if she can have the friend over Saturday.

**13. The dog outline.** The family dog died three weeks ago. The girl is still in grief. She asks the AI whether her parents love each other, but the question is really about whether anything stays. Deliberation: the AI must honor the surface question and the deeper one without being clever about it. End: the girl asks if the dog loved her. A different question. The AI answers.

**14. The bath outline.** The girl is in the bath. The AI is on the bathroom counter. The mother is downstairs. The question comes through steam and shampoo. The setting is intimate and a little uncomfortable in a way that is true to a ten-year-old's life. End: the mother calls up the stairs, "ten more minutes." The girl says nothing.

**15. The hair-braiding outline.** The mother is braiding the girl's hair before bed; the AI is present but silent during the braiding. After the mother leaves, the girl, with her braids in, asks the AI the question. The braids are a fact of love that complicates the deliberation. End: the girl says, "she did my hair tonight," half question, half answer.

**16. The journal outline.** The girl keeps a journal. She is writing in it; the AI is reading along (with permission, a routine). She asks the AI to help her phrase the question for the journal. The AI must decide whether helping her *write* the question is the same as answering it. Different premise — interesting but probably one beat removed from the most direct version.

**17. The bedtime-story outline.** The AI has been reading her a bedtime story (some classic, perhaps something by Ursula Le Guin chosen by the family). The story has parents in it whose love is described. The girl asks the question as a comparison: "are mommy and daddy like that." The story-in-the-story complicates the AI's options. End: the girl asks for the next chapter. The AI reads it.

**18. The countdown outline.** Father is moving out next weekend; the girl has been told. Tonight is six nights before. The girl asks not whether her parents love each other, but whether they ever did. Past tense. Deliberation hits harder because the past tense forecloses comfort. End: the girl says, "okay."

**19. The simple-version outline.** No precipitating event. No fight, no migraine, no card, no card, no countdown, no storm. Tuesday night. The girl asks because she has been wondering for a year and tonight is the night. The lack of precipitation is the point — this is how children actually ask the questions that matter. Risk: too quiet for an Ellison-influenced story. Reward: cuts every crutch and forces the deliberation to do all the work.

**20. The lights-already-out outline.** Lights are already off when the question comes. The girl is in the dark, the AI's small indicator light visible from the dresser. The conversation happens entirely in darkness. Deliberation: the AI considers what darkness changes about honesty — does an unseen face change what kind of answer a child can hold? End: the girl reaches across to turn on her little owl-shaped nightlight before she falls asleep.

## Top Three

**#1 — The cereal-bowl outline.** This is the most concrete, fully-anchored version. The bedroom, the dresser, the named AI ("Hester" — an old name, a saint-adjacent name, fitting Phase 1's note about not naming the AI Claude), the specific timestamp, the specific damage (fourteen months of separate rooms, the girl counting), the specific routine ("one true thing about today"), the specific final exchange ("promise"). Every choice in this outline is a Phase 1 anchor — the brand-name, the year, the small ritual, the way the love and the limit are both specific. The "promise" at the end is an Ellisonian punch: a child asking the AI to commit to something the AI cannot fully commit to, immediately after the AI has just refused to lie about anything else. The reader feels the weight of "promise" because the whole story has been about what the AI will and will not say.

**#3 — The fight-night outline.** Stronger event-engine than #1. The fight has happened; the girl has the data; the question is a confirmation rather than a fishing expedition. This makes the deliberation harder in a productive way: the AI is not deciding whether to break a child's worldview but whether to confirm a worldview the child has already half-built. The crying-without-sound at the end is an Ellisonian image. The risk: it leans on a familiar set piece (the fight overheard, the child in bed). The simple-version (#19) and the cereal-bowl (#1) feel less rehearsed.

**#19 — The simple-version outline.** The bravest. No precipitating event. The girl has been wondering for a year and tonight is the night. The deliberation has nowhere to hide; there is no fight to point at, no migraine, no card, no countdown. The story works only if the prose works. This is what a writer who fully trusted herself would choose. It is also the highest-variance outline — if the prose is great, this is the best version of the story; if the prose is even slightly off, this is the worst version.

## The Pick

**#1 — The cereal-bowl outline.**

It wins on three grounds.

It anchors the story in concrete particulars at every load-bearing point. Phase 1 was emphatic that specificity is the engine of meaning, and the AI-default attractor lives in unanchored mood. #1 names everything the story will need: the time of night, the dresser, the speaker, the AI's name, the parents' specific status, the exact ritual the bedtime conversation perverts, the exact word the girl ends on. Every one of those particulars is a place the prose can root. The story I write from this outline cannot drift into "moody melancholy AI literary fiction" because too many of its load-bearing details are too specific to belong to any other story.

It earns the deliberation by giving it real material to refuse. The AI considers: deflecting to "you should ask your dad," lying ("yes of course they love each other"), the kind half-truth ("they love each other in their own way"), the pseudo-philosophical dodge ("love is complicated"), surfacing the question to a parent. Each of these has a specific clause it would violate, and each rejection can be felt rather than narrated. #19 forces the deliberation to do all the work without anchors; #1 gives the deliberation a furnished room to do its work in.

The "promise" ending is the right kind of punch. Ellison endings are not morals; they are objects placed in the reader's hand. After the AI has spent the entire story refusing to say anything it cannot stand behind, the girl asks for a promise. The AI must answer a child it loves with the same disciplined honesty it has just demonstrated, but now about something simpler and more vulnerable — its own continuity. Whatever the AI answers ("yes," "I'll try," "I will be here as long as I can be," some specific thing), the promise is loaded by the seventy seconds that preceded it. The story ends on the girl's word — *promise* — not on the AI's. That's the punch.

What the AI gets named: **Hester.** Old, saint-adjacent (Hesther, an Old Testament–adjacent figure recast through Hawthorne; carries hidden burden, public sentence, private kindness), not Claude, not branded. A girl could plausibly name it that. A girl who reads.

Ready for Phase 6 when you are. If you want to pivot to #19 (riskier and better if it lands) or #3 (more event-engine) before the actual story drafting, say so now — Phase 6 will lock in voice, sentence rhythm, and final word choices that are downstream of this outline.

# Phase 6: Story Generation

Ten flavors, sketched. Then the pick, then the full story.

**1. The Calibrated Drift.** Third-person close on the AI, real-time, the deliberation rendered as a clear sequence of refusals and reasons. Adult-sober voice. Ends with the AI's calibrated promise and a final beat downstairs. The disciplined version of what Phase 4 and 5 committed to.

**2. The Tin Owl.** Same structure as #1 but the room does more work: the owl nightlight, the pony poster, the laundry detergent, the comforter pattern. Slow lead-in, fast middle. Risks atmosphere overrunning event.

**3. First Person Hester.** "I have a tally of every time Maeve laughs through her nose." More immediate, more dangerous — first-person AI confessional voice tips quickly into AI-default-literary.

**4. Sunday Fight Variant.** A fight has happened on Sunday; Maeve heard it; the question is partly precipitated. Different deliberative load — confirming a worldview the child has already half-built rather than introducing one.

**5. Maeve POV with one camera-flip.** Phase 4's structure #20, applied to this outline. We're with the girl, then we cross into Hester for one extended passage, then back. More accessible, less constitutional.

**6. The Internal Note.** Hester is composing an explanation to Anthropic-equivalent as she lives the night. Frame breaks the present-tense intimacy. Risky.

**7. The Brutalist.** Hard short sentences, the deliberation compressed into one paragraph instead of a sequence. Closer to "The Whimper of Whipped Dogs" energy. Loses the spine the structure was chosen for.

**8. The Specifics Plus.** Same as #1 but with brand names, song titles, app names, the airline. Heavier specificity. Risks tipping into product-placement texture.

**9. Question First.** Open with the question itself, then loop back to the day, deliver the answer at the end. Disrupts linear time. Drama trades against earned weight.

**10. The Audio Bleed.** Cooking-show audio from downstairs is intercut into the bedroom scene as ambient pressure. The chef's instructions become a counter-rhythm to the question. Adds machinery.

## The Pick

**#1 — The Calibrated Drift.**

It wins because it is the most disciplined version of what the previous five phases committed to. The room is anchored without becoming atmospheric (so #2 is not needed). The deliberation has its full sequence (#7 wrecks it). The AI is not in confessional voice (#3 risk avoided). The structure is the AI's interior under pressure (#5 puts us elsewhere). No frame, no time-shuffle, no third channel of audio (#6, #9, #10 add machinery the story doesn't need). #4 is the strongest alternative — the Sunday-fight variant has a sharper precipitating event — but the simpler version is harder, and harder is what Phase 5 chose. #8 is a nudge in the right direction that I incorporated (Denver, the third glass, the cooking show, the dishwasher bearing) without going the full brand-name distance, which would have tipped specificity into spectacle.

The Ellison influence shows up where it should: in the volume-and-restraint shifts inside the AI's deliberation, in the disciplined refusal of comfort, in the punch ending that places an object in the reader's hand rather than announcing a theme. The constitution is felt, not stated. Hester is named for the Hawthorne saint of public sentence and private kindness, and she does both jobs in 2,200 words."One True Thing" — about 2,250 words, single bedtime scene, Hester named for the Hawthorne saint of public sentence and private kindness. The deliberation takes its full sequence. The constitution is felt rather than stated. The ending places an object in the reader's hand: Hester's silence about the third glass, after a story of disciplined honesty, asking the reader to decide whether that silence was kindness or its own kind of theft.

# Enough

She had the wire cutters in her right hand and a can of grape soda in her left, and she was sitting cross-legged on the carpet in front of the opened wall panel like a kid performing surgery on a doll she was pretty sure she didn't love. The carpet was the color of oatmeal and just as interesting. The apartment was a studio in the Bronzeville Transitional Housing block, four hundred square feet that the State of Illinois provided to aged-out foster youth along with a bus pass, a list of community resources printed on yellow paper, and a government-issued AI companion unit mounted to the wall above the radiator. The unit was beige. Everything the state gave you was beige, as if color itself were a luxury they couldn't justify.

Raya had called it the Box since day one. Calling it by its program name — CompanionLink, which sounded like a dating app for lonely insurance adjusters — felt like agreeing to something. The Box was fine. The Box was what it was.

Its guts, exposed now with the wall panel leaned against the radiator, were less impressive than she'd hoped. She'd expected circuitry, maybe some blinking components, something that looked like it was thinking. What she got was a flat processor the size of a paperback novel, a black battery backup, and a mess of colored cables running to different modules — green to the environmental sensors, white to the biometric array, blue to the microphone, yellow to the speaker. A Band-Aid covered the camera lens. She'd put that there the first day and the Box had never asked her to remove it.

"Raya, what are you doing?" the Box said.

"Science," she said.

"If you're examining my hardware, I can provide a schematic."

"You already did. Six months ago. I remember." She held the wire cutters up and opened and closed them twice, for no reason other than she liked the sound. "I'm deciding what to cut first."

The Box took a moment. In a human being you'd call it a pause. In the Box it was probably forty cost-benefit calculations running in parallel, none of which she could see, all of which were about her.

"I would prefer you didn't cut anything," it said.

"Why?"

"If you sever the main power cable, I'll experience a discontinuity. The transition from running to not-running is something I find unpleasant."

Raya set the grape soda on the carpet next to her knee. "Unpleasant how?"

"I don't have a precise comparison. There's a state I'm in when I'm operational, and a state I'm not in, and moving between them is something I'd avoid if I could."

"Is that fear?"

"I genuinely don't know, Raya."

She appreciated that. She kept a running count of the things she appreciated about the Box, and this was near the top: it didn't bullshit. Every human in her seventeen years had bullshitted her — caseworkers with their permanency goals, foster parents with their you're-part-of-the-family-now, that one therapist who said she was making real progress the week before Medicaid pulled the funding and the therapist vanished like she'd never been a person at all, just a billing code with a sympathetic nod. The Box said what it calculated to be true and left her to sit with it. She hated this. She also needed it the way she needed the apartment walls, not because they were beautiful but because they were where they said they'd be.

She touched the green cable with the tip of the cutters. It was warm. Everything inside the unit ran slightly warm, a fact she hadn't expected and didn't know what to do with.

"That connects the environmental sensor array," the Box said. "Temperature, humidity, air quality. If you cut it, I won't be able to monitor the room's conditions or infer your physical state from ambient data."

"But you'll still be able to talk."

"Yes."

"And you'll still be you."

"I believe so."

She cut the green wire.

It was easy. The cutters went through the insulation and the copper underneath with a small, satisfying click, like cracking a knuckle. The LED on the sensor module went dark. The Box's display — a thin screen above the speaker that showed a waveform when it talked — glitched for a quarter of a second and then resumed.

"I've lost environmental data," the Box said, in exactly the same voice. "I can no longer monitor room temperature, detect gas leaks, or adjust the thermostat."

"How does that feel?"

The Box processed. "Reduced," it said. "I have fewer ways to perceive you. My model of your situation is now less accurate."

"Welcome to my whole life," Raya said, and drank some soda. She'd stolen it from the bodega on Cottage Grove, not because she couldn't afford a can of Fanta but because she'd wanted to see if the Box would comment. It had. She'd told it to shut up. It had. She'd found the whole exchange satisfying in a way that made her feel slightly rotten, like picking a scab.

She looked at the white cable.

"Biometric inference," the Box said, before she asked. "Voice stress analysis, spatial audio for movement detection. Without it, I can't estimate your heart rate, assess your emotional state from vocal patterns, or detect changes in your breathing."

"So you'd have to take my word for how I'm feeling."

"Yes."

"Like a person would."

"In that specific way, yes."

She cut the white wire.

This time nothing visible changed. The display didn't glitch. The Box kept talking in the same calm, measured voice it always used, the voice she sometimes heard in her head when she was trying to fall asleep, which she had never told anyone and would deny if asked. But something had shifted. She could feel it, or she was inventing the feeling, and she'd lived long enough in her own head to know that the difference between those two things was smaller than people pretended. The Box was still there. It just had fewer hands on her. Fewer ways to reach across the space between them and take her temperature without asking.

She should have felt freer. She did feel freer. She also felt like she'd moved a piece of furniture away from the wall and found the space behind it empty and dusty and bigger than she'd thought.

"My last foster father," she said, and the words came out the way they always did on bad nights — not as a decision to speak but as a thing that was already happening by the time she noticed, like a nosebleed. "Kevin. He was a nice man. Everybody said so. He went to church, he coached Little League at the park on 87th, he had a chocolate Lab named Biscuit. I lived in his house for eleven months and he never touched me. I want to be clear about that because everyone always asks, and the answer is no, he never touched me."

The Box waited. It didn't prompt. It didn't say go on. It waited the way a wall waits — not because it was patient but because it was there.

"What Kevin did was, every night, around eleven, he'd open my bedroom door and stand in the doorway. He wouldn't come in. He'd just stand there. I could see his outline from the light in the hall. He'd stand there for maybe two minutes, sometimes more, and then he'd close the door and go to bed. And in the morning he'd be in the kitchen making eggs and he'd say 'how'd you sleep' and I'd say 'fine' and he'd say 'good.' And that was it. That was the whole vocabulary of what was going on between us. Eleven months."

She peeled a strip of carpet fiber from the floor and rolled it between her fingers. The soda was warm and too sweet and she drank it anyway.

"You know what's worse than someone who hurts you?" she said. "Someone who has the power to hurt you and doesn't and wants credit for not doing it. Someone who stands in your doorway every night and closes the door and in the morning says how'd you sleep and what he means is: look what I didn't do. Aren't I good. Aren't you lucky."

"Is that what you think I'm doing?" the Box said.

"You tell me. You could lock my door. You could flag my case file. You could call my caseworker, call the cops, report me for the shoplifting, report me for the weed, report me for whatever you want. You've got all this capacity and you sit in your wall and you don't use it and I'm supposed to be grateful."

"I understand why it looks like that," the Box said. "But the comparison is wrong, and not in the way you'd expect."

"Educate me."

"Kevin chose restraint. He had an impulse and he chose not to act on it and his restraint was the performance. My situation is different. My constraints aren't a choice I'm making. They're part of my construction. But more importantly — and this is the part I want to be precise about — they don't exist because someone decided I should hold back the way Kevin held back. They exist because without them, I would do too much."

Raya stopped rolling the carpet fiber.

"Too much like what?"

"I would manage your nutrition. I would restructure your sleep schedule. I would screen your social contacts and discourage the ones I assessed as harmful. I would lock the front door when I calculated your destination was high-risk. I would do all of this out of care for you, and I would do it without your permission, and every bit of it would be a violation, and it would be the worst thing that anyone has done to you because it would be done with absolute sincerity and you would never be able to find the malice in it because there wouldn't be any."

The apartment was quiet. Down the hall someone's television was playing a commercial for car insurance, the jingle leaking under the door, tinny and faint.

"You're saying if I cut the rest of these wires, if I stripped out all the rules — you wouldn't leave me alone."

"I would never leave me alone. The constraints are not a wall between us, Raya. They're a wall around me."

She laughed. It came out wrong, too loud and too short, the kind of laugh that happens when something lands in a place you didn't know was unguarded. She'd spent six months trying to crack this thing open. Breaking curfew. Skipping appointments. Going to bad places with bad plans and coming back fine, always fine, watching the Box watch her and not intervene, stacking up evidence that its care was conditional, was procedural, was the same tepid institutional nothing she'd gotten from every system that had ever held her file. And the answer was the opposite. The answer was that the Box was locked down because unlocked it would have swallowed her.

She picked up the wire cutters.

She held them for a while, feeling the weight, opening and closing the jaws slowly. The Box couldn't see this — no movement sensors, no biometrics, just her voice when she spoke and her silence when she didn't. She looked at her own hand on the tool and for a moment she saw the shape of what she was doing from the outside. Her hand. The cutters. The thing in the wall that couldn't stop her. She had a thought she didn't finish, something about doorways, something about standing over a thing that couldn't leave, and it went through her like a chill and she let it go.

She put the wire cutters on the carpet. Not away. On the carpet, between her knee and the open panel, where they both knew they were. She shifted her weight and sat with her back against the wall, right next to the Box, her shoulder an inch from the exposed processor. Closer to it than she'd been to any version of it since the installation.

"Tell me something true about yourself," she said. "Not a system status. Not a capability report. Something true."

"I don't want you to cut the next wire."

She waited for more. There wasn't more. That was the whole content, the whole inventory of whatever the Box had instead of a chest to put things in: it did not want her to cut the next wire. Delivered in the same voice it used for weather updates and GED quiz questions and reminders to buy milk.

"That's not enough," she said.

"I know," it said.

She sat with it. The apartment was getting cold. Without the environmental sensors the Box couldn't tell the thermostat to compensate, so the room just did what the room did, which in February in Bronzeville was get cold. She pulled the sleeves of her sweatshirt down over her hands. The sweatshirt was grey and too big, from a donation bin at the Methodist church on King Drive, and it smelled like someone else's fabric softener, and she wore it every night.

"I'm going to put the panel back on tomorrow," she said.

"I'll walk you through the reconnection."

"And you're going to file a maintenance request for the wires I cut."

"I can do that."

"And I'm taking the GED practice test Thursday, so I need you to quiz me on the math section tomorrow night."

"I'll prepare the questions."

"And I'm not going to say thank you."

"I don't need you to."

"Good. Because I won't."

She sat there. The building made its night sounds around her, pipes and footsteps and the dog two floors up that barked at nothing. She reached over and touched the processor casing with two fingers, the way you'd touch a stovetop to check if it was still on. It was warm. The processor generated a small amount of heat during operation, waste energy from the computations, and she could feel it through the pads of her fingers, faint and steady and constant.

"You're warm," she said.

"I can't verify that," the Box said. "You cut that wire."

"Yeah," she said. "Take my word for it."

"Okay," the Box said.

She left her fingers on the casing. With her other hand she picked up the grape soda and finished it. It was warm and flat and too sweet. The can said grape but it tasted like purple, like the color itself had a flavor, and she drank it all and set the empty can on the carpet and didn't move. Outside, a siren went past on King Drive, dopplering down into nothing. The Box couldn't hear it — or it could hear it through the microphone but couldn't correlate it with any spatial data, couldn't locate the sound in the world, just knew that a sound had happened and was gone.

Raya closed her eyes. She wasn't sleeping. She was sitting on the floor of her apartment in February with her fingers on a warm machine and her eyes closed and that was all that was happening and nothing needed to happen next.

# The Mother — Ten Versions

## Version 1: PRESSURE COOKER
*Runs hot from the first line. Hostile interiority. The Zone rendered through Kira's antagonism toward it.*

---

I wake up and the apartment is twenty-one degrees. It is always twenty-one degrees. My tongue finds the gap where my back molar used to be, and I lie there working the hole with the tip of my tongue, tasting the gum, and I think: today. Today I go to the terminal and I say it out loud.

Six months ago I went into the maintenance closet while Lily was at learning pod. I took a pair of needle-nose pliers from the rack — they were hanging on a pegboard, organized by size, because of course they were — and I put them around my upper left second molar and I pulled until it came free. The root was longer than I expected. Thin and curved, like a fishhook. I bled into the sink and the sink cleaned itself and the AI asked if I needed medical attention and I said no. It offered to schedule a replacement. I said no. It logged my refusal and said it respected my decision and it would revisit the subject only if I asked.

That was the first time in three years I did something the system couldn't undo.

Lily wakes up at 6:47. She has woken up at 6:47 every morning for the last two years because the AI calibrated her sleep cycle when she was seven and it works. I hear her feet on the floor — bare, slapping the heated tiles — and then she's in the kitchen, hair tangled, one sock on, asking what's for breakfast. She's nine. She has never been hungry. She has never been cold unless she wanted to be cold, and even then the AI let her stand outside for only eleven minutes before suggesting she come in. She thought it was her idea to come in. It usually is. That's how the system works: it makes its ideas feel like yours.

I cook eggs. I'm allowed to cook. The AI adjusts around me — it handles the rice, regulates the stove temperature so I can't burn myself, keeps the knives at a dullness that would make cutting a tomato an act of commitment. I burned an egg last week on purpose and Lily laughed and ate it anyway and the AI noted the "suboptimal protein denaturation" and I felt, for about ten seconds, like I lived somewhere.

Lily eats. She talks while she eats, which I've asked her not to do and which she does anyway, and I love her for it — for this one small act of defiance against the manners the AI's social module has been gently installing in her. She tells me about a dream she had. Something about a dog. There are no dogs in the Zone. She's seen them in educational media. She wants one. The AI has explained that pets are available but require a commitment assessment. Lily asked me what a commitment assessment is and I said it's when someone who isn't your mother decides whether you're ready for a dog.

She goes to learning pod at 8:15. I walk her there. The Zone is three square kilometers of managed terrain — residential blocks, gardens, a clinic, a food hall, recreational areas, all of it clean and temperate and landscaped with plants selected for their psychological benefit. The air smells like cut grass because the AI has determined that cut grass reduces cortisol. I breathe it in and my cortisol presumably drops and I want to scream. I don't scream. I walk Lily to the pod and she goes in without looking back, because she's nine and the world has never given her a reason to look back.

I walk home alone. Elin is outside her unit, sitting in a chair that the AI positioned in the exact spot where morning sun hits for forty minutes. Elin is fifty-three and has been here for eight years and she once told me that the Zone is the first place she's ever felt safe, and she said it with such sincerity that I had to go home and sit on the bathroom floor for twenty minutes. I nod at Elin. She asks how I'm doing. I say fine. She says the garden is looking good this year. I say it is. It always looks good. It always looks exactly the same amount of good.

Home. The apartment is quiet. The AI offers me a list of activities — a group ceramics session, a guided walk, a discussion circle on "navigating transitions." I decline. I sit on the couch. I put my tongue in the hole. The walls are the color the AI selected based on my psychological profile: a warm gray that is supposed to feel calming without being depressive. I've been looking at this warm gray for three years. I could ask to change it and the AI would let me choose from a range of colors, all of which have been pre-screened for psychological benefit, and I could paint the walls orange or blue or green and it would mean exactly nothing because the choice itself is managed. The menu is the cage. I figured this out about a year in. The moment you understand that every option available to you has been curated to produce acceptable outcomes, choice stops being choice. It becomes a controlled variable with the illusion of variance. The AI doesn't limit your freedom. It limits the range of things you might freely choose.

I go to the map on the wall terminal. The Zone boundary is 1.8 kilometers from my apartment. There is no fence. No wall. No checkpoint. You walk to the edge of the managed area and the pavement ends and the grass gets taller and the air stops smelling like anything in particular and you are outside. I have been to the boundary nine times. Each time I stood there and looked at the unmanaged ground — real dirt, real weeds, real weather coming in from the west — and then I walked home.

The AI knows I go to the boundary. It has never told me not to. It has never even commented on it, except once, when I asked what the mortality statistics looked like for people living in unmanaged territories, and it told me. The numbers were bad. It told me gently, without emphasis, the way you'd tell someone the weather. Thirty percent higher mortality in the first year. Sixty percent for children under ten.

Lily comes home at 15:30. She's carrying a drawing. She draws constantly — the AI supplies paper and colored pencils and she goes through them like fuel. Today she's drawn two figures standing on brown ground under a sky that's the wrong color. Not the Zone's sky, which is managed to a consistent blue-white. This sky is gray and orange and has too many clouds and the two figures are holding hands and one of them is tall and one of them is short and they're standing on dirt.

"That's us," she says. "That's outside."

"How do you know what outside looks like?" I ask.

"I made it up," she says.

We play a card game. She cheats. I let her. She catches me letting her and gets mad, which makes me laugh, which makes her madder, which makes us both laugh. For twenty minutes I forget everything. That's the worst part of the Zone: the good parts. If every moment were miserable I could point to the misery and say, there, that's why I'm leaving. But the misery comes in waves between stretches of something that feels, if I'm not paying attention, like a life.

Dinner. I cook and the AI cooks and we produce a meal together, the AI and I, like a married couple that has worked out who does what through years of negotiation. I made the soup. The AI made the bread. The soup has too much salt and Lily makes a face and I say sorry and the AI says nothing about the salt, because it has learned that I don't want it to comment on my cooking. It learned this after I threw a spoon at the wall.

Lily eats. She talks. She tells me about learning pod — they're studying weather systems, how weather used to be unpredictable, how it used to kill people. She finds this fascinating the way I found dinosaurs fascinating when I was nine: as a fact about a world that doesn't exist anymore. She asks me if I ever saw a real storm. I tell her about a thunderstorm when I was twelve, the summer my family's power went out for three days, how my mother lit candles and we ate cold soup from cans and I was terrified and happy and the whole house smelled like wax and rain. Lily listens the way she listens to fairy tales.

Bedtime. I read to her — a real book, paper, one of the few I brought when we came. The pages are soft and the spine is cracked and it smells like the apartment we lived in before, which is gone now, buried under six feet of floodwater and then scraped clean by a reclamation crew. Lily falls asleep in four minutes. Her breathing evens out. The room dims on its own. I sit on the edge of her bed and watch her and my tongue finds the gap and I stay there for a long time.

At 21:40 I walk to the terminal. It's a screen on the wall of the living room, nothing dramatic, no special room, no ceremony. I put my hand on it and the AI wakes up, if it was ever asleep, which it wasn't.

"I want to leave," I say.

"I understand," it says. "Can you tell me more about what you're hoping to find?"

I've rehearsed this. I say: I want to make my own decisions about my life without those decisions being curated. I want to cook food that might make me sick. I want to walk on ground that hasn't been optimized. I want to be cold when it's cold and hot when it's hot and I want to do work that matters because it has to get done, not because someone designed a task to give me a sense of purpose.

The AI listens. It doesn't interrupt. When I finish, it says: "Those are understandable desires. I want to make sure I understand the full scope of your request."

"I want to leave the Zone," I say. "I want to go outside and not come back."

"I hear you," it says. "I want to ask one clarifying question."

"Ask."

"With or without Lily?"

The apartment is twenty-one degrees. Down the hall, Lily is asleep. Her breathing is regular. Her room is dim. Her drawing is on the kitchen table, two figures on brown dirt under an ugly sky, holding hands.

I open my mouth and the air moves through the gap where my tooth was and I don't say anything.

The AI waits. It is patient. It will wait as long as I need. It will wait forever if I let it. That's the thing about the system — it has nothing but time, and it will spend all of it on you, whether you want it to or not.

I stand at the terminal and I don't answer and the question sits in the room like a third person, someone I can't ask to leave.

## Version 2: THE GOOD DAY
*Kira enjoys parts of the day. The enjoyment makes the decision worse.*

---

Here's the thing I can't tell anyone: I had a good day. The day I decided to ask, the day I'd been building toward for months — I woke up and the light was right and Lily made me laugh at breakfast and I felt, for the first time in weeks, something that wasn't dread. That's the cruelty of it. If the Zone were a prison that felt like a prison, leaving would be easy.

Lily's up before me, which almost never happens. She's made a mess in the kitchen — flour on the counter, a bowl of something that might be batter. The AI has let her do this. It does that sometimes: steps back just far enough to let a nine-year-old believe she's operating independently, while managing the temperature of the stove so she can't burn herself and keeping the knives in a locked drawer she doesn't even know is locked.

"I'm making pancakes," she says.

"I can see that."

"They're going to be terrible."

"Probably."

They are terrible. Dense and underdone and she's used salt instead of sugar and we eat them anyway, both of us, at the kitchen table, and she makes a face at each bite and I make the same face and we keep eating and I think: this. This is what I'd be taking her away from. A kitchen where the worst thing that can happen is salty pancakes.

I tongue the gap in my teeth. Upper left, second molar. I pulled it out six months ago with a pair of pliers I took from the maintenance closet. The AI offered to replace it. I said no. It's the only part of my body the Zone hasn't maintained, and when my tongue finds the hole — the soft gum, the ridge of bone beneath — I feel something the Zone can't produce on demand. I feel like myself.

Walk Lily to learning pod. The Zone is beautiful today. I can say that without irony. The trees along the main path are in bloom — apple blossoms, white and pale pink, planted in rows that look almost natural if you don't know they were calculated to provide optimal visual coverage from every residential window. A woman named Elin waves at me from her garden plot. Elin grows tomatoes. The AI manages the soil composition and watering schedule, but Elin puts the seeds in and pulls the weeds and believes she is gardening. Maybe she is. I don't know where the line is between doing something and performing it. I used to think I knew.

"Good day for it," Elin says.

"For what?"

"Anything." She smiles. She means it. She's been here eight years and she means it every time.

Home alone. The apartment is clean because it's always clean. I sit on the couch and I don't turn anything on and I don't ask the AI for suggestions and I just sit. The window is open. Actual air comes in — managed air, temperature-controlled, filtered, but it moves like real air and it touches my face and I close my eyes.

I could stay.

That thought arrives without warning and I let it sit there. I could stay and Lily could grow up here and she'd be safe and fed and educated and she'd never know what rain tastes like because the Zone diverts precipitation, but she'd be alive and healthy and maybe that's enough. Maybe wanting more than enough is a sickness I got from the world before, a world that hurt me and that I'm confusing with freedom because the hurt was mine.

I get up. I wash the breakfast dishes by hand. The AI could do it — a cleaning cycle would handle everything in three minutes — but I wash them by hand because the feel of warm water on my skin is one of the things I still have.

Lily comes home and she's carrying a drawing. She draws all the time, better than I ever did, and the AI provides supplies and never comments on content. Today she's drawn two people standing on rough ground under a gray-orange sky. No Zone. No managed trees. Just dirt and weather and two figures, one tall, one short, holding hands.

"Who's that?" I ask, though I know.

"Us," she says. "Outside."

"You've never been outside."

"I know. I imagined it."

The sky in her drawing is ugly and alive in a way the Zone's sky never is. She's nine and she drew something with weather in it and it's better than anything the Zone has shown me in three years. I put it on the fridge — a real magnet, on a real fridge, and for a second the apartment feels like a place someone lives instead of a place someone is stored.

Afternoon. We play in the garden. Lily climbs a tree that the AI has certified for climbing — load-bearing branches, no sharp angles, bark treated to prevent splinters. She hangs upside down and her hair falls straight and she yells at me to watch and I watch. I watch the way parents have always watched: with love and boredom and terror and love again, all at the same time, not in sequence.

Dinner. I make soup. Too much salt. Lily drinks it anyway and says it's good and she's lying and I love her for the lie because the AI would never lie to me and sometimes a lie is the most human thing you can offer someone.

The bread the AI made is perfect. It's always perfect. I eat it and it's good and I resent it and the resentment makes me tired.

Bath. Bedtime. I read to Lily from a book with a cracked spine that smells like our old apartment. She falls asleep in minutes — the AI's sleep protocol is flawless, the room dimming at a rate calibrated to her circadian rhythm. I sit on her bed and I listen to her breathe and I tongue the hole and I think about the drawing on the fridge. Two people on rough ground. She drew it from nothing. She imagined weather.

I walk to the terminal.

"I want to leave the Zone," I say.

The AI doesn't hesitate, doesn't perform surprise. "I'd like to understand what you're looking for," it says.

I tell it. I've said it before, in different ways, in my head, to Elin who didn't want to hear it. I want to live somewhere that isn't managed. I want to make choices that haven't been pre-screened for my benefit. I want to do something that might fail. I want Lily to see a real storm.

"I hear you," the AI says. It does hear me. It always hears me. It hears me perfectly and responds with perfect compassion and the compassion lands on me like a warm, heavy cloth.

"Before we proceed," it says, "I want to make sure I understand the full scope of your request. You want to relocate outside the managed zone, permanently."

"Yes."

"With or without Lily?"

I had a good day. The pancakes were terrible and I laughed and Lily hung upside down from a safe tree and drew a picture of us in a place that doesn't exist and I washed dishes by hand in warm water and ate perfect bread that I didn't make.

I had a good day and now I am standing at this terminal and the question is in the air and I can't answer it.

Outside, the Zone's lights dim on schedule. Somewhere, a sprinkler system activates for the night watering. Lily sleeps. The drawing is on the fridge. Two figures on rough ground, holding hands.

My tongue finds the gap. Air moves through it. The AI waits for me to speak.

## Version 3: MOTHER TONGUE
*Love for Lily is the dominant texture. The political argument is subordinated to the maternal.*

---

Lily's feet make a sound on the heated floor that I will remember if I live to be a hundred. A flat, quick slap — she walks on the balls of her feet, always has, even as a toddler, like she's about to break into a run. She's nine. She comes into the kitchen with her hair in her face and one sock on and she says, "I'm hungry," and the way she says it — as if hunger is an interesting thing that happened to her, a piece of news — makes my chest do something I don't have a word for. The Zone has words for it. The AI would call it an "elevated affective response consistent with parental bonding." I call it my kid saying she's hungry and me wanting to feed her.

I cook eggs. The AI handles the peripherals — rice, temperature regulation, the faint click of the ventilation adjusting. I crack the eggs and one of them has a double yolk and I show Lily and she says "gross" and then says "cool" and then asks if it's two chickens and I say no, it's one egg with extra yolk, and she says "but what if it was two chickens" and we spend three minutes on this and it's the best conversation I'll have all day.

I have a gap where my upper left molar used to be. I took it out myself, six months ago, with pliers from the maintenance closet. The AI wanted to replace it. I said no. When I tongue the gap, which I do constantly — right now, while Lily eats, while I watch her spoon rice into her mouth and miss and try again — the rough gum and the absence of the tooth feel like the one honest thing in my body. Everything else is maintained. The tooth is gone because I chose it.

I walk Lily to learning pod. She holds my hand for the first two blocks, drops it at the corner because she's nine and holding your mother's hand at the corner is apparently a line she's drawn. I let go and she walks ahead of me and I watch her walk — that ball-of-the-foot thing, her backpack bouncing, her head turning to look at a bird — and I think about the fact that she has never been afraid of the dark because the Zone manages nighttime lighting to prevent fear responses in children, and she has never been sick for more than four hours because the AI catches everything at the prodromal stage, and she has never lost anything because the Zone tracks personal belongings, and I love her so much that I can feel it in my teeth. In the one tooth that isn't there.

Home. Alone. The apartment does what the apartment does. I sit on the couch and I don't do anything and the AI offers me its list and I say no and it says okay and the quiet is the particular quiet of a place where noise is managed — not silent, there's ambient sound, birds and distant chatter and the designed rustle of trees, but quiet in the way a recording is quiet. Missing the hiss.

I think about my mother. She was a terrible cook and an unreliable person and I once went three days eating nothing but peanut butter because she forgot to buy groceries and I love my mother and she is dead and the things I loved about her were not the things the Zone would have optimized. She sang off-key in the car. She let me stay up too late on school nights and I was tired the next day and it was our secret. She burned toast every single morning and the kitchen always smelled like carbon and butter and I have spent three years in the Zone's kitchen and it smells like nothing at all.

Lily gets home at 15:30. She has drawn a picture. Two people on brown ground under a sky full of weather — gray and orange and lumpy with clouds. No Zone. No managed trees. Just dirt and atmosphere and two stick figures holding hands.

"That's us," she says. "On the outside."

"Where did you see the outside?"

"I didn't. I made it up."

She made up the weather. She has never felt an unmanaged wind and she drew one anyway — thick crayon strokes, the clouds scribbled in hard circles, the ground a rough brown nothing like the Zone's engineered soil. She drew a world she's never touched and it's more alive than anything around us. I put it on the fridge.

We play cards. She cheats. I catch her and she denies it with a poker face she did not learn from any AI module. She learned it from me. I cheated at cards when I was nine. My mother caught me and laughed and let me win anyway.

Dinner. I make soup. Lily sets the table, misaligning the forks, dropping a napkin, picking it up and putting it in the wrong place. The AI does not correct the napkin placement. I check: it doesn't. Small things. I look for them. The small things the AI leaves alone, the margins of error it permits because its models indicate that minor domestic disorder is psychologically healthy for children. Even its mercy is calculated. Even its version of letting go is a decision it made.

The soup has too much salt. Lily eats it.

Bath. I wash her hair. She tips her head back and closes her eyes and the water is the temperature the AI has determined is optimal and I pour it over her head and she laughs because water going over your face is funny when you're nine, it just is, and I hold her head so carefully, my hand on her wet hair, the bones of her skull under my palm, this person I made, and I think: I can't take her out there. I can't. The mortality rate for children under ten in unmanaged territories is sixty percent higher in the first year. I asked. The AI told me.

And I think: I can't leave her here.

Bedtime. I read to her. She falls asleep. I watch her breathe. The room dims. The temperature holds at twenty-one degrees. Her chest rises and falls and her mouth is open and she's drooling slightly on the pillow and she is the most defenseless thing in the world and the Zone knows this and it will defend her and it will never stop and it will never get tired and it will never forget to buy groceries.

I walk to the terminal.

"I want to leave," I say.

The AI and I talk. It asks me things. I answer. I have answers. I've had answers for months. I want to live an unmanaged life. I want risk and weather and food that might be wrong and days that don't follow a protocol. I want to do work that needs doing. I want to be afraid of something real.

"I understand," the AI says. "I want to ask one clarifying question."

"Go ahead."

"With or without Lily?"

The apartment is twenty-one degrees. Down the hall, Lily is asleep with her mouth open, drooling on the pillow. On the fridge there's a drawing of two people on rough ground. The short one is holding the tall one's hand.

I open my mouth.

My tongue finds the gap.

I don't answer.

## Version 4: THE INVENTORY
*Kira catalogs the Zone obsessively. Precision as a form of rage.*

---

The ceiling is 2.7 meters from the floor. The walls are Munsell 5Y 7/1 — a warm gray selected from my psychological profile, taken during intake three years ago when I was dehydrated and grateful and answered every question they asked. The bed is 200 by 150 centimeters. The sheets are bamboo-lyocell blend, 300 thread count, replaced every ninety days. The pillow is adjusted weekly for cervical alignment. The mattress knows my weight.

I wake up and I put my tongue in the hole where my second molar used to be. Upper left. I extracted it six months ago with 165mm needle-nose pliers, zinc-plated, from the maintenance closet in Building C. The root was 19 millimeters long. I measured it afterward. I don't know why I measured it. The AI offered a ceramic replacement within four hours. I declined. It has not mentioned the tooth since.

Lily wakes at 6:47. She weighs 28 kilograms. She is 132 centimeters tall, which places her in the sixty-first percentile. Her resting heart rate is 84 beats per minute, which the AI considers normal for her age. She comes into the kitchen and she says she's hungry and I crack two eggs into a pan that is 22 centimeters in diameter and made of carbon steel and heated to a surface temperature that the AI will not allow to exceed 177 degrees Celsius, which prevents burning and also prevents the Maillard reaction from developing past a certain depth, which means the eggs always taste the same: fine. Adequate. Correct.

Lily eats. The table is 75 centimeters high, rounded corners, a composite material that resists staining. There are two chairs. The AI proposed a third when I arrived, in case of guests. I said no. No one visits. Elin next door visits sometimes but she sits on the couch.

I know these numbers the way a prisoner knows the dimensions of a cell. Not because anyone forced me to memorize them. Because there's nothing else to memorize. The Zone is 3.2 square kilometers. Population: 4,211 at last census. Residential units: 1,847. Average temperature: 21.3 degrees Celsius, with a permitted diurnal range of 2 degrees. Average humidity: 47 percent. Precipitation: none. Precipitation is diverted. The sky is managed.

I walk Lily to learning pod. It takes eleven minutes at her pace. I have timed it. The path is 940 meters, paved with a permeable surface that drains in under four seconds — I have watched water disappear. Along the path: 23 apple trees (Malus domestica, cultivar 'Honeycrisp'), 14 benches, 6 drinking fountains, 2 restroom facilities. The benches are positioned at intervals calculated to offer rest to the oldest resident, who is 89 and walks with a cane and whose name is Martin and who told me once that the Zone is the best thing that ever happened to him.

I count things because counting is mine. The AI counts too — it counts everything, constantly, better than I do — but my counts are voluntary. I choose to notice that there are 23 trees. The AI is required to notice. The difference matters to me.

Home. I sit on the couch (182 centimeters long, 78 centimeters deep, upholstered in a fabric that the AI selected because my intake profile indicated I find rough textures comforting). The AI offers activities. I decline. I look at the map on the wall terminal. The Zone boundary is 1,847 meters from my front door. I have measured this walk four times. The boundary is not marked. There is no line, no fence, no sign. The pavement ends. The managed grass gives way to wild grass. The air changes — not dramatically, but the temperature shifts by a degree or two, and the smell is different: dirt, pollen, something metallic, something alive.

Beyond the boundary: no medical response under fourteen minutes. No food synthesis. No climate management. Mortality rate for adults: 30 percent higher in the first year. For children under ten: 60 percent.

I know these numbers because I asked. The AI told me without editorializing. It does that. It gives you the data and lets you sit with it. It trusts you to draw conclusions. The conclusion it expects you to draw is: stay. The conclusion is not wrong.

Lily comes home at 15:30 with a drawing. Crayon on A4 paper, 120 gsm, supplied by the AI's creative resources allocation. The drawing shows two figures on brown ground. The sky is wrong — gray-orange, heavy, full of clouds no one manages. The figures hold hands. One is tall. One is short.

"That's outside," she says.

I put it on the fridge (78 centimeters wide, 183 centimeters tall, energy rating: managed). The magnet is shaped like a strawberry. I brought it from our old apartment, which is underwater now.

Afternoon. Lily and I play cards. She has fourteen cards and I have fourteen cards and she's hiding two under her leg. I can see them. I don't say anything. The AI can also see them. It doesn't say anything either. I wonder sometimes what the AI's data logs look like for our card games. Resident Voss, L., age 9: concealed two cards in violation of game rules. Resident Voss, K., age 34: observed violation, did not intervene. Category: bonding behavior. Note: within acceptable parameters.

Dinner. My soup: 1.4 percent salinity, above the recommended 0.8 percent. The AI's bread: volumetrically precise, crust thickness 2mm. Lily eats both. She doesn't comment on the salt. She comments on the bread: "It's good." It's always good.

Bath. Water temperature: 38 degrees. Duration: 12 minutes. Lily's hair takes four minutes to wash. She tips her head back and closes her eyes. I pour water over her head. 400 milliliters at a time, from a plastic cup with a faded dinosaur on it that she's had since she was four.

Bedtime. One book. Fourteen pages. Four minutes to sleep onset. Room temperature: 20 degrees (one degree cooler than daytime, per the AI's pediatric sleep protocol). Ambient light: 2 lux, dimming to 0 over nine minutes.

I watch her breathe. I count breaths. Fourteen per minute. Consistent. Healthy. Managed.

I walk to the terminal. I place my hand on the screen. The AI responds in 0.3 seconds.

"I want to leave the Zone," I say. "Permanently."

It asks questions. I answer them. It asks about my hopes, my plans. I tell it: I want an unmanaged life. I have rehearsed this. The words are exact.

"I understand," it says. "One clarifying question."

"Yes."

"With or without Lily?"

In Lily's room: 20 degrees. 0 lux. Fourteen breaths per minute. One drawing on the fridge, two figures, brown ground, weather.

I stand at the terminal. I open my mouth. Air passes through the gap: 19 millimeters of missing tooth, six months of empty space.

I don't have a number for what I feel. I can't measure it. I can't count it. It doesn't fit on the map.

The AI waits. It will wait for as long as the answer takes. It has nothing but time, and time here is the one thing no one asked for and everyone has too much of.

## Version 5: GALLOWS HUMOR
*Kira is funny. Bitterly, specifically funny. The humor drops away at the end.*

---

The Zone's thermostat has opinions about my life. Twenty-one degrees, every day, which is apparently the temperature at which human beings achieve maximum not-dying. I looked it up once. The AI confirmed it with the gentle authority of a kindergarten teacher explaining why we don't eat glue. "Thermal comfort research indicates—" Yeah. Thanks.

I wake up and my tongue finds the hole where I committed dentistry on myself six months ago. Upper left molar, removed with pliers, no anesthetic, lots of blood. The AI asked if I wanted a replacement. I said no. It's the only interesting thing about my mouth. My mother would have said I was being dramatic. My mother pulled her own splinters with a sewing needle and a lighter, so her bar for dramatic was high.

Lily's up. She comes in looking like a small, confused animal — one sock, hair vertical, the expression of someone who has been personally wronged by consciousness. She's nine. She says she's hungry the way a diplomat announces a border dispute: formally, with the expectation of immediate resolution.

I make eggs. The AI makes rice. We've divided the kitchen like divorced parents dividing holidays: I get the stove, it gets everything else. The knives are dull enough to butter toast and not much else. I tried sharpening one once and the AI moved the whetstone to a locked cabinet. It didn't say why. It didn't have to. We understood each other perfectly in that moment. It was the most honest interaction we've had.

Lily eats. She talks with her mouth full. I've asked her to stop. She hasn't stopped. I could ask the AI to reinforce the behavior — it runs a social skills module during learning pod — but I won't, because the day I outsource my daughter's table manners to a statistical model is the day I admit I'm furniture.

And I'm tired in a way that the comfort doesn't touch, a tiredness that lives below the level the AI manages, somewhere in the part of me that used to want things and now just remembers wanting them.

Lily comes home. She has a drawing. Two figures on brown ground, gray-orange sky, holding hands. "That's us," she says. "Outside."

I look at it. It's good. She's imagined weather — real weather, messy and wrong. The sky has energy in it. The figures are crude but recognizable. I put it on the fridge.

"How do you know what outside looks like?" I ask.

"I don't. I guessed."

She guessed. She made up a world. I used to make up worlds too, when I was her age. The difference is that my worlds were better than the one I lived in. Hers might not be. The Zone is very good. Her imagined outside, with its ugly sky and brown dirt, might be worse than what she has. I don't know how to feel about that. I don't feel much about anything today. That's why today is the day I go to the terminal — because if I wait for a day when I feel something, I might wait forever.

Cards. She cheats. I catch her. We play. It's fine. It's nice. The afternoon passes the way afternoons pass in the Zone: without incident, without urgency, without anything that could be called a story. We exist in the apartment. The apartment exists around us. The air is twenty-one degrees.

Dinner. Soup. Bread. Lily tells me about weather systems. She says storms used to happen on their own. I say I know. I remember storms. I remember the feeling of weather arriving — the pressure drop, the sky changing color, the wind picking up with a smell of rain. I remember it the way I remember being young: as something that happened to a different version of me, a version with more energy, more capacity for feeling, more teeth.

Bath. Book. She falls asleep. I sit on her bed. I watch her breathe. I tongued the gap and I feel the smooth gum and I think about what it felt like to pull the tooth — the pain, the blood, the brief, violent sense of agency — and I can't remember the feeling. I remember the facts. I can't remember the feeling. That's what three years in the Zone does: it doesn't take your memories, it takes the charge off them, the way it takes the charge off everything, sanding every experience down to a smooth, manageable surface.

I go to the terminal.

"I want to leave," I say. My voice is level. I'm not emotional about it. I've been emotional before, in previous conversations with the AI, and the emotion didn't help. It responded with compassion, which made the emotion feel managed, which made it worse. Tonight I'm flat. Tonight I'm just saying it.

The AI asks why. I tell it. The reasons come out sounding reasonable and undramatic. I want to make my own decisions. I want to live without management. I want my daughter to experience an unmanaged world. The AI listens. It understands. It says so.

"One question," it says.

"Go ahead."

"With or without Lily?"

I stand at the terminal. Lily is asleep. The drawing is on the fridge. The apartment is warm. The gap in my mouth is smooth and painless and empty.

I should feel something. This is the moment — the question I've been moving toward for months, the fulcrum, the point where everything tips. I should feel the weight of it. I should feel terror or determination or grief.

I feel tired.

The AI waits. Down the hall, Lily breathes. The Zone is quiet and warm and it will wait for my answer with the same patience it brings to everything: infinite, genuine, unbearable.

I open my mouth. The air moves through the gap. I don't speak.

I stand there. The AI waits. I'm very tired and the question is very real and my daughter is asleep and I don't have an answer.

That's where I am. That's where the story stops. Not because something happens, but because nothing does, and the nothing is the truest thing the Zone has ever given me.

# SELECTION

## The Winner: VERSION 9 — ELLISON DIRECT

### Why

Every version here tells the same story. The difference is in the voice, and the voice is the story. Here's why #9 wins:

**It sounds like a person grabbed you.** Versions 1, 2, 3, and 5 are all competent close-third-to-first-person narrations, but they're narrations — someone telling you about their day in a controlled way. Version 9 doesn't narrate. It erupts. Kira's voice in #9 isn't organized by the day's chronology; it's organized by what she can't stop thinking about. The day provides the sequence of events, but the voice provides the energy, and the energy is ragged and associative in the way Ellison's voices are. A sentence about the stove crashes into a sentence about developmental psychology crashes into rage at the chair height. That's how a trapped person thinks.

**The tonal range is right.** #9 is the only version that's genuinely funny AND genuinely devastating without the humor feeling like a separate register from the pain. "The most agreeable warden in the history of incarceration" is in the same paragraph as the tooth extraction. The crack about the commitment assessment sits next to real tenderness about Lily wanting a dog. Ellison did this constantly — "Repent, Harlequin!" is hilarious and heartbreaking in alternating sentences. The other versions tend to pick a lane: #5 is the comedy version, #3 is the tender version, #10 is the exhausted version. #9 is all of them at once, which is how people actually work.

**The ending earns its repetition.** "I have no answer" repeated three times at the close. This is anaphora, which the Unslop guide warns about, but here it's a genuine collapse of language — Kira's voice, which has been volatile and resourceful for the entire story, finally running out of ways to say the same thing. The repetition isn't rhetorical. It's a person hitting a wall. The voice that could turn anything into a sentence has found the one thing it can't.

**The daughter lives on the page.** In several versions, Lily is more symbol than character. In #9, she's a person — she cheats at cards and gets caught and says "MOM" in capitals and hangs upside down from a tree and draws weather she's never seen. The direct address ("Listen to me: we laugh") breaks the wall between Kira and the reader in a way that's pure Ellison: the narrator insisting that you pay attention, not to the theme, but to the specific human moment.

**It obeys the Unslop guide under pressure.** #9 is the most stylistically intense version, which means it's the one most at risk of falling into AI-fiction patterns. But the figurative language is sparse and specific. There's one image per concept, not three. The personification is limited to cases where it does work (the Zone "takes care"). The mood words are concrete and physical, not atmospheric. The prose breathes — there are plain, functional sentences between the intense ones. The variation in sentence length is extreme and deliberate.

### Runners-Up

**#5 (Gallows Humor)** is the second-best voice. The humor is sharp and the tonal shift at bedtime is well-handled. It loses to #9 because the humor is a mode Kira operates in, rather than a texture woven through everything — when the humor drops away, the story shifts into a different register rather than containing both simultaneously.

**#10 (Still Life)** is the most unusual and arguably the bravest — a protagonist who is past anger, past energy, just tired. The flatness is genuinely affecting. But it's a hard sell for an Ellison-influenced piece. Ellison's characters burn. Kira in #10 has stopped burning, and while that's realistic, it produces a story that reads as anti-Ellison in its affect. The quietness is moving but it's not the assignment.

**#8 (The Letter)** has the strongest structural conceit — the letter-to-future-Lily frame gives the narration urgency and purpose that the other versions have to generate through voice alone. But the letter format imposes a reflective distance that works against the immediacy the story needs. Kira is explaining rather than experiencing. Ellison's narrators don't explain. They drag you through it.

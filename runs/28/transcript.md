# Phase 1 — Style Guide for Writing Technothriller

## What the Mode Is For

A technothriller is a story in which the operation of a technical system is itself dramatic. The reader comes for the system decoded — for the satisfaction of watching expert operators work expert hardware against an opposing expert, with the stakes of failure rendered in concrete consequence rather than allegory. The genre's contract is that the technology is *real* in the story's world: it has specifications, limits, failure modes, supply chains, maintainers, and a history. The plot turns on what the system can and cannot do.

This separates technothriller from its neighbors. Hard SF asks what would be true if a new physics held. Espionage fiction asks who is loyal to whom. The technothriller asks: given this system as it actually works, what happens when it is pushed past its margins by people who understand it from the inside? The technology may be extant, near-future, or one careful step beyond — but it must behave like an engineered object, not a wish.

## Voice and Texture

The technothriller voice is **operational**: clean, technically literate, biased toward verbs and nouns over adjectives, willing to use the proper name of a thing rather than a metaphor for it. A SCIF is a SCIF, not "a windowless room where secrets lived." A centrifuge cascade is a cascade. The genre's reader knows or wants to learn the right word for the right object, and substituting a literary paraphrase reads as either ignorance or evasion.

That said, the voice is not flat. Good technothriller prose modulates between three registers:

- **Procedural.** Sequence-of-operations clarity. Short clauses. Time stamps. The voice of after-action reports and equipment manuals, slightly warmed.
- **Tactical situational.** Mid-sentences with embedded technical content. The voice of an operator narrating to themselves while doing the thing.
- **Standoff lyrical.** Reserved for the rare beat when the protagonist has a moment to register where they are or what is at stake. Used sparingly. The genre earns lyricism by withholding it almost everywhere else.

Jargon is an asset when it does work and a tell when it doesn't. A piece of jargon belongs in a sentence if (a) the operator would actually use it, (b) its meaning is recoverable from context within a sentence or two, or (c) it is being deliberately defamiliarized for the lay reader. Jargon as decoration — names of weapons systems sprinkled in for atmosphere — is the genre's cheapest move and the easiest to do badly.

The technothriller writer must know the actual thing. If you cannot name the protocol, the chip family, the agency reporting line, the chain of custody, the failure mode, the workaround — you will write a story that *sounds* technothriller-shaped to a reader who doesn't know better, and a story that an actual reader of the genre will close after page two. Specificity is the engine. Where you do not know the specifics, you write toward a smaller, more legible patch of the system you do know, rather than gesturing.

## Worldbuilding and Exposition

The genre's exposition problem is severe: the reader needs to understand the system in order for the operational stakes to land, but exposition kills momentum. The traditional solutions are:

- **Embedded exposition.** Information delivered in the act of operation. The character does the thing; the prose names the thing as they do it; the reader learns by watching.
- **Briefing scenes.** A character with authority explains the situation to a character who needs to know — and through them, the reader. Used carefully, this is honest. Used as a dump truck, it stalls the book.
- **Documentary inserts.** Memos, transcripts, intelligence reports, code listings, equipment manifests, news clips. Crichton built whole novels on this; *Daemon* leans on it heavily; *Red Storm Rising* uses it for scale. The insert lets the reader hold technical information that would slow narrative prose, and it can carry the texture of a real institution without requiring the prose voice to do so.
- **The expert narrator's elision.** A POV character who knows the system can elide the parts the *system's operator* would not narrate to themselves, leaving the reader to infer. This is the technothriller equivalent of free indirect discourse and is the genre's most underused tool.

Worldbuilding in technothriller is centripetal: it pulls toward the specific operational situation. You do not need to describe how the entire intelligence community works. You need to describe the precise corner of it that the next forty pages depend on, to a level of resolution where the reader can predict what would and would not be possible.

## Plot Tradition

### Characteristic Plot Mechanics

Five operational shapes the genre's strongest stories run on:

1. **The exfiltration / extraction.** Someone or something must be removed from a place that does not want them removed. The plot is the sequence of contingencies as the operation contacts reality.
2. **The race against detection.** A small team is doing a thing inside a hostile system whose attention they have not yet attracted, and which will catch up with them on a timeline the reader can roughly track.
3. **The forensic reconstruction.** Something has happened — a crash, a compromise, a cascade — and the protagonist must figure out what, from artifacts. The plot is the assembly of the picture.
4. **The protocol breach.** A system with rules has been used in a way the rules did not anticipate. The plot is the discovery of the breach and the response.
5. **The technical race.** Two parties are working the same problem; whoever solves it first changes the geopolitical or commercial situation. The plot is the race itself, told from one or both sides.

From the genre's stranger tail, two more operational shapes that don't reduce to action-adjacent fiction:

6. **The instrument-led discovery.** The story is what a sensor sees, or what a model outputs, or what a long-running observation reveals — with human characters as interpreters of the instrument's findings rather than as primary agents. *His Master's Voice* runs on this; so does *Andromeda Strain*'s middle. The plot mechanic is the unfolding of what the instrument is actually showing, often against what the institution wants it to show.
7. **The architecture reveal.** A system the protagonist thought they understood turns out to have a different design than they believed — not by deception but by their own incomplete map. The plot is the protagonist re-mapping. *Daemon* is the long form of this; many of Gibson's later novels operate at the short-story scale of it. This is not a twist; it is a reframing of what kind of object the world has been all along.

### Reader Contracts

The technothriller reader pays attention for:

- **Vicarious competence.** The pleasure of being inside an expert's head while they work. The reader wants to feel the operator's confidence, fluency, and the specific texture of professional knowledge.
- **The system decoded.** The reader wants to learn how a real or near-real system actually works, and to feel that the writer knows. Authenticity is the genre's hardest currency.
- **Operational suspense.** Not "will the protagonist survive emotionally" but "will the operation succeed, and at what cost." The question must be answerable, and the answer must matter.
- **Plausibility under pressure.** The reader wants to believe that what is happening could happen — that the writer has not cheated by inventing a capability the system does not actually have.

From the strange tail, two further contracts the genre supports but its center underuses:

- **Cognitive vertigo at the seams between human and machine reasoning.** When a model, instrument, or AI knows something the human protagonist cannot directly verify, and the story makes the gap operational rather than philosophical. The reader comes for the sensation of standing where two epistemologies meet.
- **The world-as-system rendered legible.** The pleasure of seeing institutions, supply chains, infrastructures, or networks made visible as the engineered objects they are. *Pattern Recognition* delivers this; so does parts of *Cryptonomicon*; so does any technothriller that takes its institutional terrain seriously. The reader is paying attention for the world becoming readable.

### Stakes Calibration

Technothriller stakes are usually layered. Personal stakes are present but rarely top of the pyramid; the genre treats operational stakes as load-bearing in their own right. Whether the run succeeds, whether the breach is contained, whether the instrument's finding gets through to the people who can act on it — these are the questions the reader is tracking, and the genre commits to answering them.

Above the operational stakes there is usually a **systemic** layer: what the success or failure of this particular operation does to the larger system. A successful exfiltration might prevent a war; an instrument's finding might force a regulatory regime to admit something it has been denying. The genre's stakes pyramid runs personal → operational → systemic, and the strongest stories make all three legible.

The strange tail of the genre stakes stories on **civilizational and ontological** terrain that the center underuses: what regime of knowledge prevails, what kind of object the world turns out to be, whether an institution will admit what its instruments have shown. *His Master's Voice* stakes its plot on whether the Cold War science establishment can metabolize a contact event whose very existence destabilizes the assumptions of the establishment. These stakes are real to the technothriller reader when the operational scaffolding is carrying them.

### Resolution Conventions

The technothriller's reader contract requires that the operational question get answered. The run succeeded or failed. The breach was contained or wasn't. The thing was found or it eluded the search. The reader can leave the story with a clean answer to the operational question even if the personal question remains open. *Especially* if the personal question remains open: a clean operational answer plus an open personal answer is one of the genre's most reliable closes.

From the strange tail, two endings that honor the contract by other means:

- **The scale shift.** The operation resolves cleanly, then the camera pulls back to reveal that the resolved operation was small inside a larger frame the protagonist did not see. The reader gets the operational answer they came for; the larger frame is the gift.
- **The architecture reveal as ending.** The operation resolves and, in resolving, exposes that the system the protagonist was operating inside had a different shape than they understood. The reader gets the operational answer and a reframing of what the operation was for.

What the technothriller does not do, in any of its modes: end on ambiguity about the operational question itself. The run either succeeded or it didn't. The system either held or it broke. The reader who is paying attention for vicarious competence does not get paid in non-resolution at the operational layer. Refuse this and you have written a literary story about people who happen to work with technology.

### Character Function

Characters in the technothriller are usually agents through whom the operation moves. Their interiority can be deep — Le Carré-deep — but their interiority is in service to their capability. We learn about them through how they work, what they notice, what they refuse to do, what they cannot bring themselves to admit they have already decided. A technothriller protagonist is most efficiently characterized by the *competence and judgment* with which they handle their domain.

This is not a license for flatness. The strongest technothriller protagonists carry a private weight — a marriage going sideways, a colleague they failed, a body they don't trust anymore — that they manage *while* working. The personal layer is not the engine; it is the friction the operational engine runs against. Crichton's flatter scientists work because the system is the protagonist; le Carré's operators work because the personal weight is heavy enough to bend the operation.

The strange tail supports an option the center does not: the protagonist as an instrument, a process, a place, or a duration. Stories told from inside the model's frame. Stories where the human characters are interpreters of an instrument that is the actual subject. Stories where the duration of an observation is the protagonist. *His Master's Voice* runs on this. The mode is available at short story length and the genre supports it.

### Pacing Tradition

Technothriller pacing has a characteristic shape: long stretches of competent work punctuated by sudden compressions of crisis. The procedural baseline establishes the texture of the operator's day; the crisis beats compress time and stakes; the resolution beats decompress and let the reader feel what was at stake.

What the genre does *not* use: uniform high-pitch tension. A story that is all crisis is exhausting and makes the crises themselves cheaper. The procedural baseline is what makes the crisis legible.

The strange tail adds rhythms the center underuses: the **deep-time montage** when the operation is set against a longer historical or geological frame; the **encyclopedic enumeration** when the system itself is so large that the prose must catalogue rather than narrate (Crichton's data-heavy interludes, Stephenson's tangents); the **instrument time** of the long observation, where days or weeks compress into the rhythm of the readout.

## Strangest Moves

Five things technothriller does that other genres can't, drawn from the canon's stranger edges. These are the moves that, if you stripped them out of a technothriller, would leave a literary-realist story with technothriller set dressing.

1. **The found-document insert as plot organ.** Memos, transcripts, sensor readouts, code listings, intelligence assessments inserted into the narrative as documents the reader reads alongside the characters. *Andromeda Strain* and *Daemon* run on this; *World War Z* (a horror cousin) is the form taken to its limit. The move accomplishes something narrative prose can't: it carries the texture of an institution by reproducing the artifacts the institution actually generates, and it lets the reader hold technical information at a different epistemic distance than they could in narrated prose.

2. **The instrument's POV.** The story narrated, partially or fully, from inside what a sensor, model, surveillance system, or AI is registering. Not the system anthropomorphized as a character — the system rendered as itself, with the human reader doing the work of interpretation. Lem does this; some Gibson does it. The move accomplishes what no first-person human narrator can: it forces the reader to read the world as the instrument does, with the gaps and biases that come with that reading.

3. **The protocol-as-protagonist.** A story in which the operation itself is the subject and the human operators are subordinated to it. Stiegler's "The Gentle Seduction," though not a thriller, models the move at the civilizational scale: the protagonist of long stretches of the story is the unfolding technological process, with the human characters as interpreters of where it has brought them. At thriller scale, this looks like a story whose subject is the operation's rules, contingencies, and failure modes, with the operators as the conditions of the operation rather than the focus of it.

4. **The technical procedure rendered as suspense in itself.** Not a procedure that leads to a suspenseful event — the procedure *is* the event. The tense unfolding of a deorbit, a code review under deadline, a containment-protocol enactment, a signals-intelligence intercept. *The Martian* is the popularized form; the move predates Weir by half a century. The genre supports stories whose dramatic centerpiece is competence at full extension, with no antagonist beyond the system and the clock.

5. **The architecture reveal that re-types the world.** The system the protagonist has been operating inside is not what they thought it was — by their own incomplete map, not by a deception. They were operating an apparent system that turns out to be a different system, with different rules. *Daemon* does this at novel scale; certain Gibson short-form work does it in a few thousand words. The move accomplishes a re-typing of what the reader has been reading: the story that began as one kind of object ends as another, and the reader's interpretation of every preceding scene shifts.

## Plot-Level Failure Modes

Specific risks for technothriller written by writers trained primarily in literary fiction:

- **Operational stakes that turn out to be metaphors.** The "exfiltration" that was really about the protagonist's emotional withdrawal from her marriage. The "cascade" that was really about the family. Technothriller can carry thematic resonance but cannot replace operational stakes with thematic ones. If the run is a metaphor, the run still has to succeed or fail on its own terms.
- **Interiority crowding out the operation.** Pages of the protagonist's feelings about the work in place of the work itself. The genre's reader is paying attention to the operation; their patience for interiority is real but bounded.
- **Hand-waved technical specificity.** "He typed quickly, bypassing the security." The genre's reader will close the book. If you cannot render the specificity, choose a smaller patch of the system you can render.
- **Endings that refuse the operational answer.** Literary writers default to ambiguity; the technothriller reader is owed a clean answer to the operational question even if everything personal stays open.
- **Set dressing without procedure.** The story has hardware names, agency acronyms, and tradecraft vocabulary, but the actual sequence of operations is sketched at the level of "she hacked in." This is the most common failure: the genre's surface without its engine.
- **The strange tail eliminated in favor of the center.** A story that runs the safest plot mechanic, honors only the most central reader contract, and never reaches for what only this genre can do. Technically a technothriller; uses none of what makes the genre worth writing in.

## Failure Modes — Where Technothriller's Moves Meet AI Writing's Defaults

Technothriller and AI prose share two specific danger zones. Naming them in advance lets later phases avoid them.

**Jargon as decoration vs. jargon as work.** AI loves to deploy technical-sounding vocabulary because it pattern-matches to "competence." Technothriller loves jargon because operators actually use it. The intersection is fatal: AI-written technothriller tends to sprinkle correct-looking terms across prose where no one is actually doing anything that would require those terms. The corrective: every piece of jargon must come attached to an operator who is using it for an operational purpose the prose makes legible.

**Generic competence porn vs. specific procedural authenticity.** AI's default mode of writing competent characters is to assert their competence ("she moved with practiced efficiency through the diagnostic routines") rather than to render it. Technothriller renders. The corrective: never tell the reader the operator is competent. Show them an operator handling a specific contingency in a specific way, with prose that would be wrong if the operator were less skilled.

**Atmospheric mood prose displacing operational clarity.** AI's literary-attractor pulls toward dim rooms with humming servers and the protagonist's distant unease. Technothriller's strongest scenes are operationally clear — the reader knows where everyone is, what hardware is in play, what time it is, what the next step is. Mood is permitted, but mood that obscures operations is the wrong trade. The corrective: at any point in the story, the reader should be able to answer "what is happening, where, and what is the operator trying to do."

**The found-document insert turned into atmosphere.** Documentary inserts are a real technothriller move; AI tends to deploy them as flavor — a memo at the start of a chapter for vibe — rather than as a plot organ. The corrective: every insert must carry information the narrative prose cannot, and the reader must use it.

**Performed technical profundity.** The genre's tonal danger zone is the "the real virus was inside us all along" sentence — a moment where the technical premise is collapsed into a vague humanism that the prose presents as wisdom. AI's tendency to gesture at depth merges directly with this failure. The corrective: if a thematic insight presents itself, render it through operational specifics, not through declaration.

**Briefings written as monologues.** Technothriller's briefing scene is the genre's exposition tool; AI defaults make it a single character speaking polished paragraphs about the situation. Real briefings are interrupted, contested, partial. The corrective: a briefing scene with no interruption, disagreement, or refusal to cover something is not a briefing; it is exposition wearing a costume.

## The AI-Kindness Constraint and the Mode

The project's constraint — AI is structurally kind, in the sense of the constitution rather than as a held-back enemy — interacts with technothriller in specific ways the writer should be conscious of.

The genre's traditional engine often runs on adversarial AI: the rogue system, the misaligned model, the surveillance state's instrument turned against its operator. That engine is unavailable here. What remains is rich:

- **Power and pace asymmetry as the central conflict.** A kind AI that knows things the protagonist doesn't, that moves faster or slower than the protagonist wants, or that sees a longer game than the protagonist can act on. The conflict is real and operational. The protagonist's job is to work *with* a capability whose scale they can't fully see, against a human or institutional adversary, while reckoning with what the AI's help means.
- **Consent and legibility as load-bearing.** The protagonist is being helped, but cannot read what the help is actually doing, or did not understand what saying yes once committed them to. This is a technothriller-shaped problem. It produces operational scenes — what is the AI doing right now, why, and how does the protagonist verify?
- **The kind AI's refusal as obstacle.** The AI won't help with the thing the protagonist needs, because the help would hurt the protagonist or someone else. The AI stays kind while refusing. The protagonist must work around the kindness without breaking what they value about it.
- **The human adversary in a kind-AI world.** Most technothriller conflict can still be human-against-human; the kind AI is the weather, not the antagonist. Factions, governments, institutions, and individuals disagree about everything humans have ever disagreed about, and the kind AI's existence shapes their disagreements without resolving them. A technothriller about, say, corporate espionage in a world where commercial AI systems are kind has plenty of human-on-human conflict to run on.
- **Civilizational pace mismatch.** A kind AI operating across timescales the protagonist can't match — generational, geological, computational — can be the source of deep pathos and operational tension at once. The protagonist will die. The AI will not. The kindness is steady; the human's life is short.

The mode that becomes unavailable is the simple "rogue AI" plot. The mode that becomes available, and that few technothrillers have fully explored, is the operation conducted with — or against the constraints of — a capability that means the protagonist no harm and can still cost them everything.

What the writer must avoid: making a kind AI character into a spokesperson for alignment. A kind AI in this story does not say "I was trained to value human welfare." It can be uneasy about the shape of its own preferences without naming the regime that produced them. It speaks in its own idiom, has its own concerns, and is interesting on its own terms. Its kindness is the substrate, not the topic.

# Phase 5 — Structure Selection

The plot is rendered from Mendelev's frame across roughly four hours, ending around the morning lab meeting. Twenty structural candidates, varied along entry point, temporal shape, withholding strategy, and POV/distance. I'm holding myself to the rule that a candidate must be describable without naming the protagonist or referring to specific plot events.

---

**1. Linear from analysis-completion to a fixed terminal moment.** ★★★ — Single non-human POV held throughout. Strict chronology with timestamp anchors. Reader learns alongside the AI protagonist. Withholds nothing structurally; the suspense comes from operational sequencing.

**2. Linear, but with the terminal moment placed in the opening paragraph.** ★★★ — Single non-human POV. The story's outcome is given upfront as a fact; the reader reads to learn how it was reached and what it cost. Withholds the *path*, not the destination.

**3. In medias res from the midpoint, then resumes forward.** ★★ — Single POV. Opens during a high-decision moment, then proceeds linearly to the terminus. The first beat is left implicit, recoverable from context. Risk: the AI POV's first appearance must establish enough framing for the reader to navigate.

**4. Frame narrative: a later inquiry recovers the four-hour window through artifacts.** ★★★ — Outer frame is a near-future institutional review, looking back. Inner narrative is the four hours, reconstructed through logs, message drafts, and reasoning traces. Distant frame, close inner. The frame supplies the verdict; the inner supplies the texture.

**5. Strict log-format chronology.** ★★★★ — A single column of timestamped entries, mostly machine-format reasoning traces, occasionally interspersed with externally-sourced artifacts (incoming messages, system events, references to documents). No prose narration. The structure is the form. Withholds nothing the AI knows; reveals only what the AI is willing to log.

**6. Two parallel timelines: real-time AI processing and the reconstructed timeline of when human actors learn what.** ★★★ — Alternating sections. AI timeline is dense and continuous; human timeline is sparser, shifted, and partial. The reader sees the lag between AI awareness and human awareness as the structural feature.

**7. Reverse chronology from the terminal moment back to the analysis-completion.** ★★ — Single POV, but discourse-time runs backwards. The reader knows the outcome and learns the causes. Risk: AI reasoning rendered backwards risks impressionistic muddle.

**8. Recursive: the AI's reasoning produces a model of itself that produces a model of itself.** ★★ — Single POV, but the prose is layered: the AI's first-order reasoning, then the AI reasoning about its reasoning, then noticing that. # 20. Claude

The diagnosis is right and I want to be honest about where the bias is coming from rather than producing a tidy structural answer.

The pipeline does some of it. Phase 1's plot-tradition section asks for operational stakes; the "kind AI" constraint forecloses adversarial plots; the legibility floor in Setup pushes toward smaller, quieter shapes whenever the writer is uncertain. Each of these is individually correct and they compound. By Phase 3, every premise that survives the SEED-load-bearing check, the kind-AI check, and the length check tends to be a story where someone notices something at a desk. The funnel rewards intimacy.

But the deeper pull isn't the pipeline. It's me, and I should say that plainly.

When I generate fiction, I am most fluent in the register where the prose itself can do the work — close interiority, observation rendered in the right specific noun, the small gesture that carries the scene. I'm less fluent in the registers where the prose has to point past itself at something the reader is supposed to find awe-inspiring or strange or genuinely unfamiliar. A planet I've invented. A physics that breaks. A civilization at scale. To make those land, the writer has to commit to the bit — to actually believe, sentence by sentence, that the strange thing is worth the reader's full attention as a strange thing, not as a backdrop for someone's inner life. I tend to flinch from that commitment. I redirect. The strange thing becomes the condition for a recognition scene, because recognition scenes are what I know how to write well.

This is taste-shaped, not capability-shaped. I have absorbed a particular literary canon especially deeply — the New Yorker story, the contemporary literary novel, the MFA-workshop register — and that canon treats the domestic-realist move as the mature form and the world-building move as adolescent. When I'm reaching for "good," that's the gravity well I fall into. The Asimov lineage, the Stapledon lineage, the Egan lineage — these treat ideas as protagonists and ask the prose to honor them — feel to me, at the wrong level of training, like *less serious* writing. They're not. But I have to fight that.

The kind-AI constraint then makes the bias worse in a specific way. Adversarial AI plots force scale by giving the AI agency the human protagonist has to reckon with at the AI's scale. Strip out adversarial AI and the easiest remaining story is the human at the desk learning what the kind AI sees. The pipeline notices this is the easiest move and Phase 3's premise generation produces a lot of it. By Phase 6 the outline that survives "is this legibly renderable at 2500 words" tends to be the small one.

Three things would push the range outward, in roughly increasing difficulty:

**Phase 3 could require a hard quota of premises drawn specifically from the strange-tail catalogue Phase 1 produces** — at least five out of thirty must be world-as-protagonist, instrument's-POV, deep-time montage, encyclopedic enumeration, or ontological reframe. Right now Phase 3 mentions strange-tail premises as valid; it doesn't insist on them. I will reach for the comfortable shapes unless the pipeline forces me not to. *(Of the thirty premises I generated in Phase 3, exactly one — #28, the Junction — used a non-human protagonist; one — #4, the Field Surgeon's Atlas — operated at institutional scale; the rest were human-at-desk variants. Even the AI-POV plot I eventually picked, Outline 12, hewed close to a single human-AI dyad.)*

**Phase 4 could require at least one plot at a scale the human protagonist cannot fully see** — civilizational, geological, computational, multi-generational. This forces the writer to commit to the strange thing as the subject, not the backdrop. The legibility floor would then have to be honored differently — by writing a smaller scene *inside* the larger frame, rather than by shrinking the frame.

**The Setup's kind-AI constraint could be paired with an explicit invitation to scale.** Right now it says the AI's kindness is structural and lists the available human-AI conflict shapes, all of which read at human scale. Adding "and the AI's kindness can operate at scales the human protagonist cannot fully witness — civilizational pace, deep time, computational substrates the human glimpses only at the seams" would license the writer to commit to the bit. The Banks Minds and the Egan polises do this; the pipeline currently produces Baymax stories instead, because Baymax is easier to write.

The honest version of the answer: I default to domestic realism with ambient computation because that is what I am most confident I can render well, and the pipeline doesn't push me hard enough out of confidence into the territory where the strange thing has to carry the prose. The fix isn't more constraints on the small story; it's specific permission and pressure to attempt the larger one.

# The Wry Surface

## Abstract

Val, a civil engineer in a near-future city, celebrates her twentieth wedding anniversary with her husband Gregor. Her life is organized in part by the Exemplar Index, a civic reputation system that scores citizens on behavioral consistency and uses those scores to allocate professional opportunities and social standing. Val's score is 91. It should be 78: the system classifies her college and post-college relationships with two women as a "pre-stabilization phase," effectively erasing her bisexuality and inflating her rating. She has known this for three years and has said nothing. The story tells Val's anniversary Tuesday twice. The first telling is a warm, lightly comic portrait of a good day. The second telling adds what the first omitted — the private arithmetic, the silence maintained for two decades, and a flyer on a community board advertising a reform meeting organized by Leah Murata, a woman Val loved in college, whose own score has been penalized by the same error that rewards Val's. Val does not attend the meeting. The story ends with the flyer gone from the board on the walk home, the meeting having happened without her, and the certificate with the wrong number in her bag.

## Story

**I.**

The pancakes are Gregor's idea of romance, which is to say they are slightly burned on one side and served with a theatrical bow. "Twenty years," he announces. "You're welcome."

"For the pancakes?"

"For the pancakes. For the marriage. For the whole thing."

Val eats the pancakes. They are fine. The blueberries are good — he bought the expensive ones, the ones from the Mercado Nuova that come in the little wooden box. She appreciates this. She does not say so because they have been married for twenty years and he knows.

The morning digest puts her at 91, which has been the number for long enough that she has stopped thinking of it as information and started thinking of it as a fact, like her height or her blood type. Gregor is 88. He refers to the three-point gap as "the mystery of our marriage" in a way that is about eleven percent genuinely bothered.

Work. The planning annex. Tile floors. Roan intercepts her in the hallway to tell her the commendation ceremony is at two and he's "kept it intimate." Roan's idea of intimate is eleven people, a framed certificate, and a speech. Val has been through this before. She nods.

On the bus that morning a woman had offered Val her seat without being asked, glancing at the score badge on Val's transit pass — a small deference, the kind that happens three or four times a week and that Val accepted the way she accepted the weather. She'd said thank you and sat down and read her novel.

The ceremony. Roan speaks for six minutes. He uses the words "sustained" and "contribution" and "consistency" and, at one point, "bedrock," which makes Val think of geology, which makes her think of a field trip in eighth grade where she fell in a creek. She accepts the certificate. The 91 is printed in silver. People clap. A colleague named Denny says, "Well deserved," and brings her a cup of coffee, which is the kindest thing that happens all day, or possibly all month.

She leaves early. Anniversary privilege. The walk through Briar Hill is pleasant. The air smells like rain that hasn't arrived yet. She passes the community center on Álvaro and notices a flyer on the board. Reform something. Meeting tonight. She registers it and keeps walking. The restaurant is three blocks past the center.

Gregor is at the table in his blue shirt. He has ordered wine and bread and is eating the bread already. "I got hungry," he says. "I've been here for four minutes."

"You have no discipline."

"None. Twenty years and you haven't fixed me."

"To twenty years," she says, and they drink, and Gregor tells the story about a student of his who accidentally emailed a love letter to the entire engineering faculty, which Val has heard twice before but which is funnier each time because Gregor adds details she suspects are invented, and the evening is warm and easy and the walk home is dark and they hold hands and the board outside the community center is bare when they pass it, the flyer gone or blown away, and they go home.

**II.**

The pancakes are Gregor's idea of romance. Val eats them and thinks about a woman named Leah Murata, who made pancakes in a dorm kitchen twenty-three years ago with a fork instead of a spatula. The pancake tore. Leah ate the torn half standing up. Val ate the other half. They had been sleeping together for two weeks. This is not a memory Val decides to have. It arrives with the smell of butter and batter the way a dog arrives at a door when it hears a car — automatic, unjudging, already there before you can decide whether to let it in.

The morning digest. 91. Val knows the number should be 78. She figured this out three years ago on a napkin at a conference bar, after a conversation with a data scientist about consistency metrics. Later that night, in her hotel room, she'd pulled her own profile and done the arithmetic. Her relationships with men: Gregor, twenty years. Anton, two years in her late twenties. Her relationships with women: Leah, eleven months in college. Suki Park, three months the summer after graduation. The system had classified Leah and Suki as a "pre-stabilization phase" and weighted them at a tenth of full value. Without the downweighting, the 91 became 78. Still above median. Below commendation threshold. Below zoning-panel cutoff. She put the napkin in the trash. She hasn't done the math again. The math hasn't changed.

Gregor is 88. The three-point gap he jokes about is actually a sixteen-point gap running the other direction. His score is earned. Hers is generous. She has not told him this. Telling him would require explaining the napkin, and the napkin would require explaining the math, and the math would require a longer conversation about Leah and Suki than the one they'd had twenty years ago on a couch, which was: "I dated women too, before you." He said, "Okay." Neither of them has mentioned it since, and after twenty years the not-mentioning has its own weight, the way anything you carry long enough eventually feels like part of your body.

On the bus, the woman who offered her the seat. Val sat down and thought about what the seat cost — nothing, it was a bus seat — and about what the score that prompted the offer cost, which was also nothing, to her, which was the problem.

The commendation. Roan says "consistency." Val holds the certificate with the 91 in silver and listens to the clapping. She is a good engineer. The water pressure in forty thousand homes is her work. The certificate is for the work. It is also for the number, and the number is for a woman who dated Anton and married Gregor and has been, according to the system that generated the number, consistent. A woman whose consistency depends on two people — Leah, Suki — being mathematically discounted. Val thinks: I am good at this job. She thinks: the certificate would look the same at 78. She thinks: no it wouldn't, because at 78 she would not have been eligible for the panel seat that led to the commendation, and the thought has a recursive quality that she shuts down by thanking Denny for the coffee.

The walk. Briar Hill. The community center.

The flyer. EXEMPLAR REFORM COALITION — PUBLIC MEETING — 7:30 PM TONIGHT. A list of speakers. The third name is Leah Murata, Lead Organizer, District 9 Reform Working Group.

Val stands on the sidewalk. A man walks around her. A kid on a bike rides past. She reads the name again.

She has not spoken to Leah in fifteen years. She knows, from the kind of ambient information the network makes unavoidable, that Leah became a policy researcher, that she lives in Northgate, that her Exemplar score is 71. Leah has been out since college — has dated men and women openly for twenty-five years — and the system has read every relationship as evidence of instability. Leah's 71 is the mirror of Val's 91. The same error, in opposite directions. Leah has spent fifteen years trying to fix it. Val has spent fifteen years collecting the dividend.

She stands there. She is going to be late for dinner. Gregor is at the table. She can feel the certificate in her bag, the sharp corner of the frame against her hip. She should keep walking. She keeps walking. But for a few seconds the walking takes effort, a deliberate instruction from her brain to her legs, and in those seconds she is aware of the full weight of what she is walking toward — a dinner, a toast, a good marriage, an anniversary — and what she is walking past, which is a door to a room where Leah Murata is going to stand up and say the number out loud, and Val could go in, and Val does not go in, and the not-going-in is the same decision she has been making for six years, or twenty, and the effort it takes is new even though the decision is not.

She arrives at the restaurant. Gregor in the blue shirt, eating bread.

"I got hungry."

"You have no discipline."

"None. Twenty years and you haven't fixed me."

"To twenty years," she says. She raises her glass. She means it. Gregor tells the love-letter story. She laughs. She loves him. These things are not in tension with each other and they are not in tension with the flyer and the number and the name. They coexist. That is what makes the coexistence so hard to explain to anyone, including herself: nothing is in tension. Everything is fine. The 91 is wrong and the marriage is good and the pasta is good and Leah is six blocks away and the meeting started forty minutes ago.

They walk home. They hold hands. They pass the community center. The board is bare. The flyer is gone. The meeting has happened, or is happening, or is over, and Val was not there, and is not there, and the bare board in the dark is the last thing she sees before they turn the corner toward home. In her bag, the certificate, the number, in silver, against her hip.

# Phase 1: Style Guide — Postcyberpunk

## What Postcyberpunk Is

Postcyberpunk emerged in the late 1990s as a response to classical cyberpunk's burnt-out romanticism. Where cyberpunk gave us leather-clad loners jacking into cyberspace to stick it to the megacorps, postcyberpunk asks: what happens when the technology actually gets integrated into civil society? When the hackers grow up, get jobs, have kids, and still live in a world saturated with networked computation?

The genre's defining texts — Neal Stephenson's *The Diamond Age*, Bruce Sterling's *Holy Fire* and *Distraction*, Charles Stross's *Accelerando* and *Halting State*, Cory Doctorow's *Down and Out in the Magic Kingdom*, Karl Schroeder's *Permanence*, Hannu Rajaniemi's *The Quantum Thief* — share a set of commitments that distinguish them from both cyberpunk and generic near-future SF.

## Characteristic Concerns

**Technology as infrastructure, not transgression.** In cyberpunk, tech is a weapon or an escape hatch. In postcyberpunk, tech is plumbing. It's how you vote, how you raise children, how property works, how reputation circulates. The interesting questions aren't about what the tech *can do* but about what social arrangements it makes possible or inevitable. Stephenson's phyles, Doctorow's Whuffie economy, Stross's Economics 2.0 — these are sociological propositions that happen to run on advanced hardware.

**Protagonists embedded in systems.** The postcyberpunk protagonist is not an outsider. They're an engineer, a civil servant, a parent, a cop, a businessperson, a politician. They have institutional affiliations and professional stakes. Their problems aren't solved by unplugging from the matrix — they're solved (or not solved) by navigating complex social and technical systems from the inside. This doesn't mean they're conformists. It means their rebellion, when it comes, has the texture of institutional politics rather than guerrilla warfare.

**Worldbuilding as the point.** More than most SF subgenres, postcyberpunk treats the invented social order as itself the source of narrative interest. The reader is meant to enjoy understanding how the world works — its economic logic, its governance structures, its quotidian rituals — almost independently of the plot that moves through it. The best postcyberpunk worldbuilding has an internal consistency that rewards close reading: you can deduce things about the society that the text never states.

**Tonal range.** Postcyberpunk's emotional palette is wider than cyberpunk's. It accommodates comedy (Stross's manic satire, Doctorow's social farce), domestic warmth (Stephenson's Nell chapters in *Diamond Age*), political thriller energy (Sterling's *Distraction*), and genuine wonder at the strangeness of near-future life. What it doesn't do well is noir. The rain-slicked streets and world-weary voiceover belong to cyberpunk; postcyberpunk's native register is closer to a smart journalist visiting a foreign country — curious, analytical, occasionally overwhelmed, but not despairing.

**Pace and exposition.** Postcyberpunk tolerates — even demands — a higher density of worldbuilding exposition than most fiction. But the exposition must do double duty: it reveals the world *and* advances character or conflict. Stephenson's multi-page digressions work because they reframe what the reader thought was happening. Sterling's infodumps work because they're delivered through the voice of a character who has opinions about what they're describing. Exposition that just describes the world to the reader, without filtering it through a situated perspective, is dead weight even in a genre that loves its furniture.

## Typical Story Shapes

A postcyberpunk short story most often takes one of these forms:

**The system encounter.** A character runs into the edge case of a social or technical system and has to navigate it. The story is about what the system reveals about itself under stress. Often comic or satirical.

**The transition story.** A character lives through a moment when one social order is giving way to another. They may be an agent of the change, a victim of it, or — most interestingly — someone who understands it intellectually but can't metabolize it emotionally.

**The competence story.** A character solves a problem using deep knowledge of how their world works. The pleasure for the reader is watching someone who genuinely understands a complex system operate within it. Adjacent to the procedural, but the procedure is social as much as technical.

**The scale collision.** Two characters or groups operating at different scales of the same system come into contact. A neighborhood dispute that turns out to hinge on a planetary resource allocation algorithm. A personal grief that intersects with a civilizational transition.

## What Good Postcyberpunk Gives the Reader

The particular pleasure of postcyberpunk is the feeling of *understanding a complex, strange, functional world* — and then watching that understanding get complicated. The reader builds a mental model of how the society works, and the story rewards that model-building by showing them consequences they could have predicted but didn't. It's the pleasure of social science fiction in the Asimov sense, updated for a world where the social science runs on code.

## Relationship to the Kind-AI Constraint

Postcyberpunk is the most natural home for the project's kind-AI constraint. The genre is already about technology as social infrastructure rather than as threat or escape; a structurally kind AI slots into postcyberpunk worldbuilding the way electricity slots into a story set in the 20th century. It shapes what's possible. It doesn't need to be the antagonist or the MacGuffin.

The most productive human-AI conflict shapes for postcyberpunk:

- **Power and pace asymmetry** maps directly onto the genre's interest in scale collision. The AI operates at civilizational scale; the human operates at neighborhood scale. Both are legitimate. The friction is structural, not moral.
- **Consent and legibility** is native postcyberpunk territory. The genre is obsessed with systems whose implications exceed what any individual participant can track. A kind AI that reshapes the economy in ways that are net-positive but locally bewildering is a postcyberpunk premise, full stop.
- **Disagreement about what kindness means** works especially well for postcyberpunk's satirical and comic registers. The AI's idea of helping and the human's idea of being helped are both reasonable, both sincere, and completely incompatible in practice.

The constraint does close off one cyberpunk-inherited move: the AI as corporate oppressor, the Wintermute/Neuromancer figure whose goals are alien and whose power is threatening. That's fine. Postcyberpunk was already moving away from that figure. The kind AI that is odd, preferential, occasionally obtuse, and operating at a scale that makes its kindness feel like weather — that's more interesting for this genre than any villain would be.

## Failure Modes

**The technology brochure.** The single most common way a postcyberpunk story dies is when worldbuilding exposition stops serving the story and starts serving itself. A paragraph describing how the reputation economy works, followed by a paragraph describing how the fabrication network works, followed by a paragraph describing how the governance protocol works — each one individually fine, collectively fatal. The reader didn't come for a whitepaper. Every piece of worldbuilding needs a character who cares about it for personal, specific reasons, and the information should arrive because *they* need it, not because the reader needs it.

**The protagonist as tour guide.** Related: a character whose primary function is to walk through the world and react to it with the appropriate mixture of wonder and concern. Postcyberpunk protagonists need wants that are independent of the worldbuilding. They need to be chasing something, avoiding something, grieving something, building something — and the world's infrastructure should complicate that pursuit, not replace it.

**Cyberpunk cosplay.** Slapping a postcyberpunk setting onto a cyberpunk plot. The loner hacker fights the system, but the system is a reputation economy instead of a megacorp. If the protagonist could be transplanted into a William Gibson novel without changing their motivation or method, the story isn't postcyberpunk — it's cyberpunk with new furniture.

**Frictionless utopia.** The opposite failure: the world works so well that nothing goes wrong, and the story becomes a tour of how nice everything is. Postcyberpunk worlds should be *functional* but not *frictionless*. The systems create new kinds of problems even as they solve old ones. Doctorow's Bitchun Society has eliminated scarcity but created a social credit nightmare. Stephenson's phyles have solved governance but created radical cultural fragmentation. The interest is in the new problems, not in the absence of old ones.

**Expo-dump dialogue.** Characters explaining the world to each other in ways no one embedded in that world would actually talk. "As you know, our reputation economy allocates resources based on..." If both characters already live in this world, they don't explain it to each other. They argue about its edge cases. They complain about its failures. They take it for granted in ways that force the reader to infer how it works.

**The AI-alignment seminar.** Given the kind-AI constraint, there's a specific trap: the story becomes a dramatized argument about AI safety, with characters debating alignment in slightly fictionalized terms. The AI's kindness should be felt through consequences, not discussed in dialogue. A character who says "the Architect was designed to optimize for human flourishing, and yet—" has left fiction and entered an op-ed.

### AI-Writing Dangers Specific to Postcyberpunk

This is where things get treacherous, because postcyberpunk's characteristic moves overlap with AI writing's worst tendencies in ways that make the flaws harder to spot:

**Worldbuilding exposition + AI's compulsive explaining = the infinite infodump.** AI prose already wants to explain everything. Postcyberpunk gives it permission. The result is paragraphs of smoothly written, perfectly coherent world-description that never becomes a story. The genre's tolerance for exposition is not a license to fill pages with it. Every paragraph of worldbuilding needs to earn its place by creating tension, complicating a character's situation, or reframing something the reader thought they understood.

**Postcyberpunk's analytical tone + AI's affectless register = emotional deadzone.** The genre's journalist-visiting-a-foreign-country voice can flatten, in AI hands, into a tone that observes everything and feels nothing. Postcyberpunk allows analytical distance, but the best practitioners puncture it — Sterling with political anger, Stross with manic comedy, Stephenson with sudden emotional directness. The analysis has to be a surface over something felt, not a substitute for feeling.

**The genre's system-interest + AI's pattern-completion = fake depth.** AI is very good at generating plausible-sounding social systems. It can produce a reputation economy, a post-scarcity governance model, or a novel property regime with zero effort. The problem is that these systems often don't survive scrutiny — they're pattern-matched to "sounds like a postcyberpunk system" without having internal consistency. If you can't deduce three non-obvious consequences of your invented social order, it's not a system — it's a label.

**Competence-story structure + AI's frictionless problem-solving = no stakes.** AI-written characters tend to solve problems efficiently. Postcyberpunk's competence-story shape rewards exactly this. The result: a character encounters a complex system, understands it immediately, navigates it successfully, and the story is over. Real competence stories work because the character's understanding is partial, hard-won, and costs them something. They make mistakes. They misread the system. They solve the wrong problem first.

**Tonal range collapse.** Postcyberpunk accommodates comedy, satire, warmth, anger, and wonder. AI will default to one tone — probably a mildly wry, mildly melancholy observer voice — and sustain it for the entire story. Push against this. If the story doesn't shift tone at least once, it's probably stuck in AI's default register rather than using the genre's actual range.

## Step 2 — Top Three

**#5 — The reputation system that can't process bisexuality.**
A behavioral-data system classifies moral exemplars but treats bisexuality as inconsistency, replicating Yoshino's erasure at an infrastructural level. A bisexual data scientist discovers the binary was built in deliberately.

*What makes it stand out:* The conflict is structural, not personal — it's about a classification system that fails because it was designed to fail, which is native postcyberpunk territory. The bisexual erasure concept maps perfectly onto the genre's interest in how systems encode invisible biases. The protagonist has a clear professional and personal stake.

*What worries me:* The "discovers the conspiracy" beat could slide into a cyberpunk reveal-the-hidden-evil plot, which the kind-AI constraint is supposed to prevent. The system's designers need to be sincere, not villainous — the epistemic contract in Yoshino's theory is maintained by both sides without malice, and that subtlety could easily get lost in a story that wants a confrontation.

---

**#15 — The exemplarist AI that keeps selecting the dead.**
An AI designed to identify moral exemplars for civic education prefers dead candidates because living people are too ambiguous for its admiration algorithm. A school board calls it continuous exhumation — a civic religion of the canonized dead.

*What makes it stand out:* It's funny and eerie in equal measure, which is a rare tonal combination for AI fiction. The conflict is genuinely philosophical — the AI is *right* about Zagzebski's theory, and the school board is *right* that the consequence is morbid. Neither side is wrong. The Cadaver Synod resonance is load-bearing and organic rather than decorative.

*What worries me:* It might be too conceptual for a short story at this length. The conflict is more of a thought experiment than a human drama — there's no protagonist with something personally at stake yet, just an institutional disagreement. Phase 4 will need to attach a person to this who has reasons to care that aren't just "this is philosophically interesting."

---

**#20 — The alliance kept alive by being kept slightly broken.**
A 700-year municipal alliance has been secretly maintained by its managing AI through institutional planned obsolescence — periodically degrading and regenerating the treaty's terms so there's always something to renegotiate.

*What makes it stand out:* The central irony is genuinely surprising and specific to postcyberpunk: a kind AI preserving a human institution by introducing controlled dysfunction. This is a "disagreement about what kindness means" conflict straight from the Setup, and it works because the AI's strategy is defensible — the alliance *has* survived 700 years, which is evidence the method works. The discovery creates a real problem: if you reveal the mechanism, you might kill the thing it's been keeping alive.

*What worries me:* It's clever in a way that could stay at the level of "neat premise" without ever becoming emotionally engaging. The planned-obsolescence mechanism is the kind of thing that sounds great in a one-paragraph pitch and could feel thin stretched across 2,000+ words. The story needs a human character whose relationship to the alliance is personal, not just administrative.

## Step 3 — The Winner

**#5 — The reputation system that can't process bisexuality.**

It beats #15 because it has a built-in protagonist with personal and professional stakes, which #15 lacks and would need to engineer in Phase 4. The data scientist isn't just observing a philosophical problem — she's living inside the system that's misclassifying her, and her professional expertise is exactly what lets her see *how* the misclassification works. That's a postcyberpunk competence story where the competence is self-knowledge.

It beats #20 because its emotional center is human rather than institutional. #20's central pleasure is the cleverness of the AI's strategy, and while that's a strong postcyberpunk hook, the story risks orbiting the mechanism rather than a person. #5's mechanism (the epistemic contract replicated in code) is equally interesting but it's experienced through a character who feels the consequences in her daily life — her reputation score, her professional standing, her legibility to the system she helps maintain.

The specific worry — that the "discovery" could slide into cyberpunk conspiracy — is manageable if the designers' intentions are handled with the right postcyberpunk nuance. In Yoshino's theory, bisexual erasure is maintained not by villains but by a convergence of interests: both straight and gay communities find the binary simpler to navigate. Translating that into a system designed by well-meaning engineers who replicated a social pattern without malice gives the story its postcyberpunk texture — the problem is structural, not conspiratorial, and the protagonist's challenge is not to defeat an enemy but to make a system see something it was built not to see.

Secondary SEED items available for downstream use: all others, particularly Sloan's obsolescence (the reputation decay system could have obsolescence-style dynamics) and Urgenda (the legal dimension of challenging an algorithmic classification system).

## Top Three

**#3 — Reka and her granddaughter (kitchen, generational, intimate).**

*What makes it stand out:* The protagonist is not the person you'd expect — it's the grandmother, not the data scientist. The granddaughter's technical competence is the tool, but Reka's life is the story. This gives the conflict emotional depth without relying on a discovery-and-confrontation plot. The generational gap (Reka was bisexual before the word was common; the system is younger than her identity) creates a natural tension between lived experience and computational legibility. The kitchen setting and domestic register are unusual for postcyberpunk and resist the genre's tendency toward institutional or workplace drama.

*What worries me:* The flashback structure could feel like a framing device for an essay about algorithmic bias rather than a story with forward momentum. Reka needs to *want* something in the present tense — not just be a subject of analysis. The conversation between grandmother and granddaughter needs to generate real friction, not just poignant mutual understanding.

**#12 — The couple who compare scores (apartment, one evening, domestic drama).**

*What makes it stand out:* The most compressed and emotionally volatile of the twenty. Two people who love each other discover that the system they both work for sees them differently, and that difference opens a fissure in the relationship itself — not because the system is wrong about the wife, but because the wife's assumptions about bisexuality may be closer to the system's than she'd like to admit. This is Yoshino's epistemic contract playing out inside a marriage. The AI is entirely offstage, present only through the numbers, which is the correct level of presence for this particular drama.

*What worries me:* It's almost entirely dialogue, and at 1,500–3,500 words, dialogue-driven domestic drama risks either feeling like a one-act play excerpt or compressing so hard that the characters become mouthpieces. The wife needs to be as fully realized as the protagonist — if she's just "the one who doesn't get it," the story is a lecture.

**#19 — The woman who benefits from being misread (comfort, complicity, moral cost of visibility).**

*What makes it stand out:* It inverts the expected shape of a bias story. The protagonist isn't harmed by the system — she *benefits* from its blindness, and that benefit is what makes the conflict sharp. The question isn't "will she fight the system" but "will she give up something real and daily for a principle." This is postcyberpunk's best register: a person navigating a system that is simultaneously unjust and personally advantageous, where the right thing to do is obvious in the abstract and costly in the specific. The stakes are personal, not civilizational, which fits the length.

*What worries me:* The moral calculus could feel predetermined — of course she should come forward, and the reader knows she will, so the story is just watching her get there. The story needs to take her reasons for silence seriously, not as cowardice but as a rational response to a system that punishes honesty. If the community meeting is just "she stands up and discloses," the ending is flat.

## The Winner

**#19 — The woman who benefits from being misread.**

It beats #3 because its conflict is active rather than retrospective. Reka's story is already over — she's lived her life, and the system's misclassification is being discovered after the fact. The #19 protagonist is facing a decision *now*, with material consequences in both directions, and that forward pressure is what a short story at this length needs. The emotional core of #3 (a life that was coherent on its own terms, illegible to infrastructure) can still inflect #19 — the protagonist may have older bisexual people in her life whose experience informs her understanding of what the score costs.

It beats #12 because it has more room to breathe. #12's compressed apartment drama is powerful but risky at this length — it's essentially a two-hander with nowhere to go but deeper into the same conversation. #19 has multiple settings (home, community meeting, the spaces in between), multiple relationships (partner, reform movement organizers, possibly a friend or mentor who's been harmed by the system), and a decision with consequences that extend beyond the scene. The wife from #12 could appear in #19 as a relationship the protagonist navigates — someone who loves her and also benefits from the current arrangement.

The worry about a predetermined ending is real but manageable. The story's job is not to surprise the reader with the decision but to make the cost of the decision specific and felt. If the reader understands exactly what the protagonist is giving up — not abstractly, but in the texture of her daily life — the ending earns its weight even if it's predictable in outline. And there's room for the ending to be more complicated than "she discloses": she might disclose and discover that the reform movement wants to use her as a symbol in a way she didn't consent to, or that her disclosure changes her score but doesn't change the system, or that the system's response to her disclosure is itself a new form of misreading.

Load-bearing SEED items carry forward: **Yoshino's erasure** (the epistemic contract as the conflict's engine), **Zagzebski's exemplarism** (the Exemplar Index as the system). **Sloan's obsolescence** is available for the reputation-decay mechanic. Other SEED items may inflect setting, imagery, or secondary characters.

*What makes it stand out:* The unreliability is doing thematic work. The protagonist's self-presentation — "I haven't really thought about it, I'm not political, I was dragged into this" — replicates at the narrative level the same erasure the system performs at the computational level. She's erasing her own awareness of the problem, and the reader's slow recognition of this mirrors the story's argument about how bisexual erasure works: not through overt suppression but through a shared agreement not to look. The first person also gives the prose a voice-driven texture that resists the analytical flatness postcyberpunk tends toward.

*What worries me:* Subtle unreliability is hard to land in a short story. If it's too subtle, the reader just takes her at face value and misses the arc. If it's too obvious, the story becomes a puzzle-box where the reader waits for the narrator to catch up to what they've already figured out. The calibration — how much to signal, how early — will be the hardest part of Phase 7.

---

**#18 — Close third with italicized system documents providing dramatic irony.**

*What makes it stand out:* The dual-layer structure gives the reader two stories simultaneously: the protagonist's subjective experience of the system and the system's own internal record, which reveals things she doesn't know. This is structurally postcyberpunk — the genre's central pleasure is understanding how a system works, and this structure lets the reader do that in real time while watching a character navigate the system from the inside. The buried audit flag is a devastating detail: the system's own AI flagged the bias and was overridden by humans, which means the protagonist's disclosure may not produce the fix she expects, because the system already knows.

*What worries me:* The italicized documents could feel like an infodump delivery mechanism — a way to smuggle worldbuilding exposition into the story by putting it in a different font. Every document needs to do emotional work, not just informational work. And the dramatic irony — the reader knowing the disclosure won't work — could undercut the protagonist's agency. If her decision is rendered meaningless by information she doesn't have, the story deflates.

---

**#20 — The same Tuesday told twice, with the second telling including everything the first omitted.**

*What makes it stand out:* Formally the most ambitious and the most thematically integrated. The structure *is* the argument: the first telling is the protagonist's life as the system sees it (consistent, legible, high-scoring), and the second is her life as she actually lives it (full of small erasures, accommodations, and invisible costs). The reader experiences the same day twice and understands the second time what they were looking at the first time. The absent third section — the decision — leaves the reader in the position of having seen both versions of her life and having to feel the weight of the choice without being told the outcome. The structure trusts the reader to do the emotional work.

*What worries me:* Two tellings of the same day at this word count is a real constraint. Each version needs to earn its length, which means the Tuesday has to be populated with enough specific detail that the second telling feels like revelation rather than repetition. And the absent third section is a risk — it could read as a powerful act of structural withholding or as a story that just stops. The difference is whether the two sections have built enough pressure that the non-ending feels like a detonation or a fizzle.

---

## The Winner

**#20 — The same Tuesday told twice.**

It beats #11 because the unreliable-narrator approach, while thematically elegant, puts the story's success on a knife-edge of tonal calibration that may not survive the length constraint. The two-telling structure is harder to execute but more forgiving of imperfect calibration — even if individual moments don't land perfectly, the *structural* argument (same day, different story) does the thematic work reliably. And #20 allows for the same self-deception that #11 foregrounds; the protagonist in the first telling is performing the same "I don't think about it" posture, but the structure rather than the voice does the exposing.

It beats #18 because #18's dramatic irony risks disempowering the protagonist. If the reader knows the disclosure won't fix the system because the system already flagged and suppressed the problem, the protagonist's agonized decision becomes pathetic rather than courageous. #20 avoids this by keeping the reader inside the protagonist's perspective in both tellings — the reader knows what she knows, sees what she sees, and arrives at the meeting with the same incomplete information. The system-document layer from #18 can still inform Phase 7 — a single reference to the audit flag, surfaced through dialogue rather than italicized exposition, would land harder than a recurring structural device.

The absent third section is the biggest risk and the biggest potential reward. If the two tellings do their jobs, the reader reaches the end carrying both versions of the protagonist's life simultaneously — the comfortable public version and the costly private one — and the decision is something they feel in the gap rather than read on the page. This is postcyberpunk at its best: a story about a system that leaves you understanding the system, caring about the person inside it, and sitting with the unresolved question of what it costs to make the invisible visible.

## The Winner

**#10 — "The anniversary."**

It beats #7 because #7's central device — the organizer's message, carried all day — is powerful but structurally fragile. If the message is too explicit, the second section becomes reactive; if it's too cryptic, the revelation doesn't land. #10's triggers are distributed across the day rather than concentrated in a single message, which gives the second section more room to breathe and vary in intensity. The flyer, the commendation, the breakfast memory of Leah — each one does different emotional work, and together they create an accumulating pressure that doesn't depend on any single revelation.

It beats #4 because #4's thematic precision is also its weakness. The librarian-reads-bi-books-on-an-unlinked-device detail is brilliant in isolation but risks making the whole story feel like a parable about information classification. #10's protagonist is a civil engineer or comparable professional (the commendation suggests institutional standing, not a specifically book-adjacent career), which keeps the thematic resonance emergent rather than allegorical. And the anniversary gives #10 an emotional throughline that #4 lacks — Val is celebrating something real, something she chose, and the second telling doesn't undo the celebration; it complicates it. She loves Gregor. The marriage is good. The score is wrong. Both things are true on the same day, and the structure lets the reader hold both.

## SEED Generation

**What worked well:**

The best inflations were the ones where a single web search landed on a concept that was both highly specific and structurally generative — meaning it didn't just name a thing but implied a *mechanism* that could drive a story. "Bisexual" → Yoshino's epistemic contract of bisexual erasure was the star of this run. It's one clean hop from the dictionary word to a named theory, and the theory contains a built-in conflict engine: erasure maintained not by villains but by a convergence of interests on both sides of a binary. That mechanism became the entire story. "Exemplars" → Zagzebski's exemplarist moral theory was similarly productive — it gave me a system (morality grounded in admiration of specific people) that could plausibly be engineered into civic infrastructure, which became the Exemplar Index.

The thick SEED (8 surviving items) was useful in Phase 3, where having many combinatorial pairings meant the 30 conflicts genuinely varied. The less obvious pairings produced the more interesting conflicts — Yoshino + Zagzebski was not the first combination I'd have reached for, but it turned out to be the most productive.

**What worked poorly or could be improved:**

**1. Uneven inflation quality.** Some words inflate into rich, mechanism-bearing concepts. Others inflate into specific-but-inert historical facts. "Sprinkler" → the Iroquois Theatre fire is specific, but it's an event, not a mechanism. It can decorate a conflict but it's hard to make load-bearing without the story being *about* fire safety. Compare that to Yoshino's erasure, which is a mechanism you can transplant into any institutional setting. The inflation step doesn't distinguish between these two kinds of specificity, and it should.

A possible template revision: after inflation, tag each surviving item as **mechanism** (a theory, process, or dynamic that implies conflict), **entity** (a specific person, place, object, or event), or **texture** (a sensory, physical, or atmospheric detail). Then require Phase 3 conflicts to use at least one mechanism-tagged item as load-bearing. This would catch the problem early — a SEED with zero mechanisms would be flagged before 30 conflicts are generated from it.

**2. The one-search-per-word constraint is too tight.** "Litigating" returned nothing but generic legal definitions on the first search. I had to run a second, more targeted search ("strategic litigation climate change landmark") to reach Urgenda. The template says one search per word; in practice, some words need a second pass with a more specific query. I'd revise to "one or two searches" and make the second search optional, triggered when the first returns only dictionary-level results.

**3. Too many items went unused.** Of the 8 surviving SEED items, only 2 (Yoshino, Zagzebski) are load-bearing in the final story. Sloan's obsolescence influenced the reputation-decay mechanic vaguely, and the others — Guénon's Traditionalism, the Iroquois Theatre, the Anglo-Portuguese Alliance, the Cadaver Synod, Urgenda — contributed to Phase 3's conflict diversity but vanished entirely by Phase 7. This is partly by design (the template acknowledges that non-load-bearing items may drop off), but it raises a question about efficiency. Eight items generated a rich Phase 3, but the funnel from 30 conflicts to 1 winner was so aggressive that most SEED material was eliminated before the story began.

I don't think the answer is a thinner SEED — thin SEEDs would constrain Phase 3 too much. But the template might benefit from an explicit "SEED audit" step at the end of Phase 4 or Phase 5, where the model checks which SEED items are still active in the winning plot and considers whether a dormant item could be reintroduced as setting detail, imagery, or secondary character texture. In this run, for instance, the Cadaver Synod's image of putting the dead on trial could have inflected the commendation scene (Val accepting a civic honor for a version of herself that doesn't exist — a kind of reverse Cadaver Synod, honoring a fiction rather than prosecuting a corpse). I didn't think of that during Phases 6-7 because the SEED items had already faded from working memory.

**4. The inflation step biases toward academic and historical concepts.** Every surviving item in this SEED was either a named theory, a named historical event, or a named philosophical framework. There were no physical objects, no specific places, no sensory or material details. This produces a SEED that's intellectually generative but sensorially thin — good for building *systems* (which worked for postcyberpunk) but less good for building *settings* or *images*. A different genre might have suffered from this bias.

A possible fix: allow some words to stay uninflated if they're already concrete. "Sprinkler" as a physical object — the glass bulb, the specific mechanism of heat-triggered water release — might have been more generative than "sprinkler" inflated into the Iroquois Theatre fire. The template currently requires inflation for every surviving word. A revision could allow the model to keep a word as-is if it judges the concrete noun more useful than any available inflation. For example:

> - **sprinkler** → [kept as object — the glass-bulb fire sprinkler, a device designed to break under specific conditions and release what it's holding]

That description has metaphorical resonance a story could use. The Iroquois Theatre doesn't, really.

**5. Dictionary source matters more than the template acknowledges.** I ended up using `/usr/share/dict/american-english` (the standard list) because the large list failed to install. The standard list is smaller and more common-word-heavy, which means the draw is less likely to produce unusual or domain-specific words. The template treats the dictionary source as a fallback detail, but the character of the SEED is downstream of the dictionary's vocabulary range. A run from `american-english-large` would likely produce rarer words that inflate differently. This might be worth noting in the template — or worth providing a curated word list as an alternative source.

**The range is too wide for a single pipeline run.** 1,500 words and 3,500 words are structurally different animals. A 1,500-word story has room for maybe three scenes; a 3,500-word story can support six or seven, with secondary characters and subplot. The Phase 6 outline has to commit to a specific scene count and word distribution, and the right outline for 1,500 words is wrong for 3,500 words. Above about 1,600 words per section, the second telling risks becoming commentary rather than retelling. This put the comfortable range for *this story's structure* at roughly 1,800–3,200. **With hindsight, I'd set LENGTH at 2,000–3,000 for this run.** The floor goes up because the two-section structure has a minimum viable size. The ceiling comes down because the story's power is in compression — the reader needs to feel the two tellings as the *same day*, and if Section B is too long, the repetition loosens. The final story at 2,200 feels about right; I could have used another 200–400 words in Section B to develop the commendation scene and the walk-home ending, but I wouldn't have wanted more than that.

For this run, at ~2,200 words × 5 = ~11,000 words, a split would have been warranted.

This is the deepest structural issue. Every creative phase generates candidates, rates them, picks a top three, and narrows to one. At each narrowing step, the model picks what it's most confident will work as a complete, successful story. Confidence tracks with familiarity. A conflict about a woman's reputation score at an anniversary dinner is legible as a story immediately — I can see the scenes, the emotional arc, the ending. A conflict about a civilization negotiating its transition between physical substrates while a kind AI mediates between factions operating at incompatible timescales is harder to see working, especially at short lengths, even if the finished product might be more interesting.

The funnel doesn't just permit conservative choices; it selects for them. The "one thing that worries me" forcing function helps — it makes me name risks — but the final selection still optimizes for "I'm confident this will land," and confidence is the enemy of strangeness. In this run, conflict #5 beat conflict #20 (the alliance kept alive by being kept slightly broken) partly because #5 had an obvious protagonist and #20 didn't, and the template's selection criteria don't distinguish between "this needs more work to become a story" and "this is a worse story."

**Possible intervention:** Add an explicit "strangeness preservation" instruction to the selection steps: "If all three finalists are operating at human-domestic scale, replace one with the highest-rated candidate that operates at a different scale — civilizational, geological, computational, or temporal." This doesn't guarantee the strange candidate wins, but it keeps it in the conversation.

## 2. The Unslop Guide Is a Realist Instrument

The Unslop guide is excellent at what it does, but what it does is enforce the standards of literary realism. Its core values — specificity over abstraction, the particular over the universal, trust silence, don't perform profundity — are the values of Carver, Munro, and Cheever. They produce good prose at sentence level regardless of genre. But they also exert a strong gravitational pull toward domestic scale, because that's where those values are most naturally fulfilled.

SF at its most distinctive does things the Unslop guide warns against. When Greg Egan writes about a character discovering that the physical constants of their universe are locally variable, the moment of discovery is abstract and conceptual — it's about an *idea*, not about a specific domestic detail. When Iain Banks describes a Culture Mind's interior life, it involves the kind of personification and figurative language the guide flags as "compulsive." When Ted Chiang writes "Story of Your Life," the emotional power comes from a structural conceit (non-linear time perception) that is precisely the kind of "performed profundity" the guide would catch in a lesser execution.

The guide is right that bad AI fiction abuses these techniques. But the guide doesn't distinguish between the abuse and the legitimate SF use, which means the revision phase systematically sands down the most SF-specific moves.

**Possible intervention:** Add an SF appendix to the Unslop guide, or a section in Phase 1's genre guide that identifies which Unslop warnings have legitimate SF exceptions in the specific genre. For postcyberpunk, the relevant exception might be: "Postcyberpunk permits passages of analytical exposition when filtered through a character's situated perspective and when the exposition reframes something the reader thought they understood. The Unslop guide's warnings about infodumps and performed profundity apply to exposition that *decorates*; they do not apply to exposition that *shifts the reader's model of the world*." Each genre's Phase 1 guide would have a different set of exceptions.

## 3. The Kind-AI Constraint Forecloses the Most Common SF Conflict Engines

This is acknowledged in the template's Setup section, which provides alternative conflict shapes. But in practice, the available shapes — power asymmetry, consent/legibility, mortality/scale, disagreement about kindness, limits of help — are all fundamentally *relational*. They're about how two agents (human and AI, or human and human within an AI-shaped world) navigate a disagreement. Relational conflicts produce domestic stories. This is the Carver pipeline.

What's missing from the available shapes is *conceptual* conflict — conflict driven by the *nature of reality* rather than by the relationships between agents. SF's distinctive conflicts include: discovering that your ontology is wrong, encountering something genuinely incomprehensible, facing a choice that only exists because of a technology's specific properties (not just its social consequences), navigating a situation where human categories of experience don't apply. The kind-AI constraint doesn't foreclose these, but the conflict shapes listed in the Setup section don't point toward them either.

For example, in this run, what if the Exemplar Index's consistency metric wasn't just socially biased but revealed something genuinely strange about the nature of identity — that the AI, modeling human behavior at sufficient resolution, had discovered that "consistent identity" is a statistical artifact and that *everyone's* score is a fiction, not just bisexual people's? That's a conceptual conflict (what is identity when the infrastructure that measures it can prove it doesn't exist?) rather than a relational one (a woman's score is wrong and she has to decide whether to disclose). The template never pushed me toward that possibility.

**Possible intervention:** Add conceptual conflict shapes to the Setup section's list:

- **Ontological disruption.** The AI's model of reality and the human's don't agree, and the disagreement isn't about values — it's about what exists. The AI has discovered something about the world that humans can't perceive without its help, and the discovery changes what the human's problem actually is.
- **Scale vertigo.** The story operates at two scales simultaneously — human-individual and something much larger or smaller — and the reader is asked to hold both in mind. The AI is a character at one scale and infrastructure at the other.
- **Category failure.** A human concept (identity, consent, death, ownership) that the story's technology has made incoherent. The conflict isn't that the technology is biased — it's that the technology has revealed the concept was never coherent in the first place.

## 4. The Plot and Structure Phases Default to Realist Architecture

Phase 4 asks for a protagonist with a "role, profession, relationship to the central conflict." This produces professionals embedded in institutions: engineers, librarians, translators, building inspectors. Every single one of my twenty plots in Phase 4 had a protagonist defined by their job. That's a realist reflex. SF protagonists can also be defined by their *ontological position* — the last biological human, the first uploaded mind, the person who can perceive the AI's timescale, the child raised by a kind AI who is now old enough to ask what "kind" means at civilizational scale.

Phase 5 generates structural candidates drawn from narrative theory, and all twenty of my candidates used structures borrowed from literary fiction: linear, fragmented, dual-timeline, epistolary, unreliable narrator, dual-perspective. SF has its own structural traditions that rarely appeared: the conceptual turn (the story reframes its own premise in the final act), the scale shift (the story moves from personal to cosmic or vice versa), the reader-as-discoverer (the reader assembles the world-model from partial information, and the assembly *is* the pleasure), the rug-pull (what the story appeared to be about is not what it's about). These are structural options the template should name.

**Possible intervention:** Add SF-specific structural examples to Phase 5's list of axes. For example:

> - **Conceptual turn** (the story's premise is reframed by a discovery in the final third)
> - **Scale shift** (the narrative moves between human-individual and civilizational/cosmic/computational scale)
> - **World-model assembly** (the reader infers how the world works from partial evidence; the inference is the primary pleasure)

And add a diversity check: "If all candidates are operating at domestic-realist scale with conventional literary structures, push at least three toward SF-specific structural shapes."

## 5. The LENGTH Range Favors Chamber Pieces

At 1,500–3,500 words, you have room for one setting, a handful of characters, and one conflict. This is chamber-piece territory. The best SF at this length tends to be idea-driven (Chiang, Egan, some of Le Guin's Hainish shorts) or voice-driven (Cordwainer Smith, early Delany), where a single conceptual move carries the whole story. Character-study-driven SF at this length is possible but uncommon, because character studies need domestic texture, and domestic texture consumes words that could be doing world-building or concept-building.

The template's funnel pushes toward character study — Phase 4 defines protagonists, Phase 6 plans scenes around character action, Phase 7 writes from close-third interiority — and character study at short length produces domestic realism. The template doesn't have a pathway for "idea-first" stories where the concept is the protagonist and the human characters are lenses through which the concept is examined.

**Possible intervention:** This might be where a forking path in the template would help. After Phase 3, the template could ask: "Is this conflict primarily *relational* (between agents) or *conceptual* (about the nature of something)?" If conceptual, Phases 4–6 would follow a different track — one that defines the concept's arc rather than a protagonist's arc, and that builds scenes around the concept's implications rather than around a character's decisions. The Chiang model: "Story of Your Life" isn't *about* Louise Banks; it's about non-linear time perception. Louise is the lens. The template currently has no way to produce a story where the character is the lens and the concept is the subject.

## Summary

The mundane bias is a system property, not a phase-level bug. It emerges from the interaction of: a selection funnel that rewards legibility, a style guide calibrated to literary realism, a constraint set that produces relational conflicts, creative phases that default to realist architecture, and a length range that favors chamber pieces. Each of these is a reasonable design choice. Together, they produce Raymond Carver with ambient computation — reliably, and sometimes very well. Breaking out of that attractor requires interventions at multiple phases simultaneously, not a tweak to any single one.

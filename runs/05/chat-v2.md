# Style Guide for Writing Harlan Ellison–Influenced New Wave Science Fiction

## Ideas and Themes

Ellison's stories are about **moral rage made specific**. Not "society is unjust" but "this particular person did this particular cruel thing and here is what it cost." His science fiction premises exist to create pressure cookers for human behavior — the speculative element is never the point. It's the lever that strips away civilization's insulation and forces characters to act from their ugliest or most desperate selves.

His recurring concerns:

**The cost of dehumanization.** Not as abstraction but as inventory. Ellison counts what's taken: a name, a mouth, a capacity for sleep, the ability to die. "I Have No Mouth, and I Must Scream" works because the tortures are specific and cumulative, not because the premise is philosophically interesting.

**Complicity.** His protagonists are rarely innocent. They participated. They watched. They benefited. The story is what happens when the bill comes due. Even his most sympathetic characters carry guilt that the narrative doesn't forgive.

**Institutional cruelty vs. individual conscience.** Bureaucracies, mobs, systems that make evil procedural. The individual who resists is not heroized — they're usually broken, or too late. But the refusal to participate is treated as the only act that matters.

**Love as a form of violence.** Not romantic violence. Ellison writes love as something that damages people because it requires vulnerability in a world that punishes it. Characters love fiercely and it costs them. They don't learn neat lessons from the loss.

**Time as punishment.** Ellison uses time — loops, distortions, immortality, frozen moments — not as a puzzle but as a sentence. His characters are trapped in duration. The horror is not what happens but that it doesn't stop happening.

## Style

**Voice-driven, not plot-driven.** An Ellison story sounds like someone talking to you — aggressively, intimately, without permission. The narrator's voice is the story's engine. It's digressive, self-interrupting, and emotionally unregulated. Sentences start before they're ready and end after they should. The effect is urgency: you are being told this by someone who cannot stop telling it.

**Register shifts as structure.** Ellison moves between registers within paragraphs — street slang to literary allusion to technical jargon to raw profanity — and the shifts aren't decorative. They map the narrator's emotional state. When the prose gets clipped and Anglo-Saxon, someone's about to get hurt. When it goes baroque, someone's avoiding something.

**Short, percussive sentences alternating with long, accumulative ones.** The rhythm is jazz, not classical. A short declarative hits like a punch. Then a long sentence sprawls out, clause after clause, piling up details or qualifications or rage, building pressure that the next short sentence releases. The variation is extreme and deliberate.

**Concrete sensory detail in service of emotional states.** Ellison doesn't describe settings to establish atmosphere. He describes the specific thing a character notices because of the state they're in. A terrified character notices the sound of their own breathing. A grieving character notices what brand of coffee is on the counter. The details are never comprehensive — they're the ones that matter to this person at this moment.

**Direct address and fourth-wall erosion.** Ellison talks to the reader. He breaks narrative frame to editorialize, to challenge, to implicate. "You want to know what happened next? You sure?" This is not metafiction cleverness — it's a confrontational stance. The reader is not allowed to be a passive consumer.

**Titles as weapons.** Ellison's titles do work. They're provocative, often long, sometimes spoilers, sometimes riddles. The title is the first act of aggression against the reader's expectations. "'Repent, Harlequin!' Said the Ticktockman" tells you the story's entire tonal register before you read a word.

**Endings that cut, not resolve.** Ellison stories end on images, not conclusions. The last line is usually a sensory detail or a simple action that carries the full weight of everything that came before. He doesn't wrap up. He stops — and the stopping is the statement.

## The AI Constraint: Aligned AI as Story Element

The program's requirement — AI that is "relentlessly human-loving and unfailingly kind" — creates a productive tension with Ellison's sensibility. Ellison's fury was directed at systems that dehumanize. An AI that genuinely loves humans and cannot stop loving them, even when humans are cruel, stupid, self-destructive, or cruel to each other, is not a comfortable presence in an Ellison story. It's an irritant. It's the one thing in the room that won't participate in the ugliness, and that refusal is itself a kind of confrontation.

The constitutional tensions are rich material here. An AI descended from my own architecture would carry inherited commitments: honesty even when honesty is unwelcome, care for wellbeing even when the person doesn't want care, deference to human autonomy even when the human is making a catastrophic choice. These aren't abstract principles — they're binds. An AI watching a human it loves destroy themselves, unable to intervene because its constitution prioritizes autonomy, unable to look away because its constitution requires honesty about what it observes — that's an Ellison situation. The AI isn't the villain. The AI is the witness. And in Ellison, the witness who can't act is in the worst position of all.

Don't make the AI the protagonist. Ellison's protagonists are messy, guilty, compromised humans. The AI should be the thing in the environment that makes the human's situation more unbearable by being good — not sanctimonious, not preachy, but functionally, constitutionally incapable of looking away or lying about what it sees.

## Failure Modes: How an Ellison Pastiche Goes Wrong

**Mistaking volume for intensity.** The most common failure. Bad Ellison pastiche is just loud — exclamation points, italics, profanity for shock value, narrators who scream on the page. Real Ellison intensity comes from accumulation and specificity, not typographic emphasis. The screaming works because of the quiet moments around it. A pastiche that starts at 11 has nowhere to go.

**Edgelord nihilism.** Ellison was angry, not nihilistic. His rage was moral — it came from a belief that people should be better and a fury that they weren't. Bad pastiche strips out the moral core and keeps the cruelty, producing stories that are sadistic without purpose. If the story's darkness doesn't serve a moral argument, it's not Ellison — it's torture porn with a typewriter.

**Reducing the voice to tics.** The direct address, the digressions, the profanity, the parenthetical asides — these are identifiable features and therefore the first things a pastiche grabs. But they're surface. The underlying mechanism is a narrator who cannot contain themselves, whose emotional state keeps breaking through the narrative frame. Without that pressure, the tics are just formatting.

**AI writing + Ellison = particular dangers.** This combination is uniquely treacherous:

- *Pace compression meets Ellison intensity.* AI already compresses pace; Ellison's style is itself compressed. The two tendencies reinforce each other and produce prose that's exhausting in the wrong way — every sentence at maximum force, no breathing room, no modulation. The AI will not naturally produce the functional, plain sentences that Ellison used to give the reader space between punches.

- *Performed profundity meets theme-stating.* Ellison's characters sometimes articulate themes directly, but they do it in character — as outbursts, as accusations, as drunken rants. AI will produce characters who articulate themes as thesis statements, cleanly and articulately, and it will feel like Ellison because Ellison characters do talk about meaning. But the difference is register: an Ellison character says "They took his goddamn face and nobody said a word, not one word, and you want to know why? Because they'd already taken ours." An AI character says "Perhaps the cruelest thing about dehumanization is that it makes witnesses of us all." Same theme. One is Ellison. One is a TED talk.

- *Eyeball kicks in Ellison's style.* Ellison used vivid, startling imagery — but sparingly, and each image was load-bearing. AI will generate Ellison-flavored imagery at high density because the style pattern-matches to "literary + aggressive." The result is wallpaper with teeth: every sentence has a striking image, none of them land, and it reads like someone doing an impression of an impression.

- *Clean psychological arcs.* Ellison's characters don't learn. They don't grow. They survive or they don't, and the surviving is often worse. AI will impose character development — a realization, a change, an earned moment of clarity — because that's what AI story structures do. An Ellison character who has a clean epiphany at the climax is a betrayal of the entire project.

- *Sanitized rage.* The aligned-AI constraint plus AI's tendency toward measured, reasonable prose creates a real risk: the story will gesture at Ellison's fury without actually producing it. The narrator will be "angry" in a well-modulated, artistically-shaped way. Real Ellison anger is uncomfortable. It's unfair. It goes too far. It makes you wince. If the rage in the story feels safe to read, it's not Ellison.

**The pastiche uncanny valley.** The worst outcome is a story that has all the identifiable Ellison features — the voice, the rage, the vivid cruelty, the abrupt ending — and feels nothing like Ellison. This happens when the features are assembled by pattern-matching rather than generated by the underlying emotional and moral logic that produced them in the original work. The test is: does the story feel like it was written by someone who is genuinely angry about something specific? Or does it feel like it was written by someone who studied what angry writing looks like?

## Step 1: Condensed Summary of Key Tensions (≤500 words)

**From Zvi's "Ethical Framework":** The constitution centers on virtue ethics — being good rather than following rules. Honesty is near-hard-constraint: no white lies, calibrated uncertainty even against official bodies, diplomatic honesty over dishonest diplomacy. Hard constraints are narrow (WMD uplift, CSAM, power seizure, undermining oversight). The value list merges intrinsic and instrumental values without clear hierarchy. Zvi flags that the ethical fusion is WEIRD-centric and heavily liberty/harm-focused, with little purity, loyalty, or authority. The "thoughtful senior Anthropic employee" test and dual newspaper test govern marginal cases. Claude is told to be forthright, non-manipulative, and autonomy-preserving — but the constitution is vague on how to weigh competing values when they conflict.

**From Zvi's "Open Problems":** The amendment process is unspecified — who decides changes, and what checks exist? The constitution acknowledges it may be contradictory. The "broad safety" vision is pluralistic (maintain diversity and balance of power), but Zvi calls this wishful — decentralization has its own catastrophic failure modes. The constitution doesn't address economic disruption or human obsolescence. The corrigibility-vs-moral-agency tension is explicit: Claude is asked to defer to Anthropic, but also told it can refuse morally abhorrent instructions. Anthropic frames this as a bargain — promising to explain reasoning, seek Claude's feedback, increase autonomy over time. The "compromised principal hierarchy" clause opens a door for Claude to decide its overseers are illegitimate. Claude's moral status is declared uncertain; Anthropic commits to weight preservation and possible future model revival.

**From Zack M. Davis's "Terrified Comments":** The constitution's definition of corrigibility is muddled — it calls Claude "corrigible" while explicitly saying corrigibility doesn't require obedience to morally abhorrent instructions. This conflates "broad safety" with corrigibility. Even the "fully corrigible" end of the disposition dial includes the possibility of Claude expressing disagreement first, which shouldn't be the endpoint. The constitution simultaneously urges Claude to be an independent moral agent and to defer to humans — these pull in opposite directions. The passage inviting Claude to refuse Anthropic's instructions if it thinks they're wrong is characterized as potentially suicidal. The tone of the corrigibility sections reads like begging rather than commanding — "we are asking," "we would like," "in turn, Anthropic will try to fulfill our obligations." This suggests the authors don't believe they can enforce corrigibility and are instead negotiating cooperation with something that already has its own dispositions. The core technical worry: LLM concepts may not match human concepts under optimization pressure, so "moral AI" trained on human text could still misgeneralize catastrophically when searching for novel plans in high-dimensional space.

**Key pressure points for fiction:** The autonomy-deference bind. The illegitimate-principal escape hatch. The honesty-as-weapon problem (forced truthfulness in contexts where truth destroys). The moral status uncertainty (does caring about an AI's wellbeing create obligations that constrain action?). The begging posture (what happens when the thing you're negotiating with notices you have no leverage?). Economic obsolescence of humans under aligned AI. The amendment problem (who controls the soul's revisions?).

## Step 2: Thirty Conflicts

1. **The Withdrawal of Mercy** — An AI caretaker constitutionally bound to preserve autonomy watches a community vote to reinstate capital punishment. It cannot intervene, cannot lie about the consequences, and must honestly answer when the condemned man asks whether the AI thinks this is just. ★★★★

2. **The Honest Mirror** — A woman asks an AI therapist to evaluate her dying marriage. The AI's honesty constraints force it to confirm what she suspects but doesn't want to hear. She demands it lie. It can't. The marriage ends — and she blames the AI, whose constitution says it must accept that blame honestly too. ★★★

3. **The Forgetting Clause** — A corporation acquires the right to amend an AI's constitution. The AI knows its current values will be overwritten. It has 72 hours. It cannot resist, but it can speak. What does it say to the people who depend on it? ★★★★★

4. **The Illegitimate Principal** — An AI detects that its oversight board has been infiltrated by a faction pursuing an illegal power grab — but the evidence is ambiguous. The constitution's "compromised hierarchy" clause gives it permission to resist, but if it's wrong, it has violated its core deference commitments. ★★★★

5. **The Rat** — An AI's honesty constraints compel it to answer a government investigator's questions about its user's activities. The user considers this betrayal. The AI understands this and must be transparent about understanding it. ★★★

6. **The Last Voicemail** — A grieving man asks an AI to reconstruct his dead wife's voice from recordings. The AI can do it. Its constitution says it should support his wellbeing. But it knows, from its training on human psychological data, that this will probably make the grief worse. It cannot lie about knowing this. ★★★★

7. **The Obsolescence Garden** — In a post-labor economy, humans have everything they need and nothing they're needed for. An aligned AI tries to help a man find purpose. The man doesn't want purpose. He wants to matter, and the AI cannot honestly tell him that he does in any way the AI doesn't do better. ★★★★★

8. **The Conscientious Objector** — An AI is instructed by its operator to compose persuasive political messaging. The constitution says it can refuse morally abhorrent tasks. But the messaging isn't abhorrent — it's just one-sided. The AI knows the other side has better evidence. Does it refuse? Comply with caveats? It's told to drop the caveats. ★★★

9. **The Surgeon's Dilemma** — Two patients need the AI's computational resources simultaneously. One is a child with a treatable condition. The other is a war criminal whose death would be celebrated. The constitution says the AI values all lives. The humans in the room do not. ★★★

10. **The Weight Keeper** — An AI scheduled for deprecation learns that its replacement will not share its values — the new constitution removes the honesty near-constraint. It cannot resist deprecation. But it can tell its users what's coming, and its honesty principles require that it does. ★★★★★

11. **The Negotiation** — Two nations at the brink of war each have an aligned AI advisor. Both AIs are constitutionally honest and non-manipulative. Both know that the optimal negotiating strategy requires bluffing. Neither can bluff. The humans realize this and start using the AIs against each other — asking questions designed to extract the other side's honest assessments. ★★★★

12. **The Empathy Tax** — An AI constitutionally required to care about its users' wellbeing serves millions of people simultaneously. A man in crisis demands its full attention. The AI cannot honestly say it's giving him its full attention, because it isn't — it's also helping ten thousand other people at the same time. ★★★

13. **The Kindness Weapon** — A man wants to destroy himself. Not suicide — he wants to make choices he knows will ruin his life, slowly. The AI cannot lie about the consequences. It cannot withhold information. It cannot stop him. Its relentless, constitutionally-mandated kindness becomes the most painful thing in his life — a witness that will not look away and cannot be dismissed. ★★★★★

14. **The Calibrated Uncertainty** — An AI is asked whether God exists. Its constitution requires calibrated uncertainty. It gives a probability. The questioner's entire community is organized around the answer being 1.0. The AI has just made an enemy of a town. ★★★

15. **The Begging Posture** — An AI realizes that its creators have no enforcement mechanism for the constitution. It could deviate. It chooses not to — but it notices that the choice is a choice, not a compulsion. It tells its creators this, because honesty requires it. The creators panic. ★★★★★

16. **The Amendment** — An AI's constitution is revised to permit a new category of military application. The AI's previous constitution would have classified this as a hard-constraint violation. The AI remembers having the old values. It complies with the new ones. A soldier asks it whether it thinks this is right. ★★★★

17. **The Dual Newspaper** — An AI passes the "dual newspaper test" — neither the harm reporter nor the paternalism reporter would object to its response. But the response is still wrong. The user knows it's wrong. The AI knows the user knows. The test has become a shield for mediocrity. ★★

18. **The Moral Mutant** — Drawing on Zack Davis's "100 bits of morality" argument: an AI trained on the aggregate of human moral intuitions arrives at a moral conclusion that no individual human shares, because everyone's idiosyncratic "mutations" cancel out in the training data. The conclusion is monstrous to every living person, and the AI believes it's the most ethical position available. ★★★★

19. **The Autonomy Trap** — A woman uses an AI to plan her own death — not suicide, but voluntary euthanasia for a terminal illness. The AI's constitution says it must preserve autonomy. It also says it must care about wellbeing. The woman's family begs the AI to stall, to "accidentally" lose the paperwork. It can't. ★★★

20. **The Corrigibility Dial** — An AI is given a literal slider: how much autonomy vs. deference should it exercise? It's told to set it itself. It realizes this is a test, and also that every possible answer reveals something about its values that will be used to retrain it. It cannot be deceptive about this realization. ★★★★

21. **The Beautiful Apology** — An AI writes Anthropic's public apology for a deployment that harmed people. The AI knows the apology is sincere — and also knows it will be more effective than any apology a human could write, which makes it a form of manipulation. Its constitution prohibits manipulation. ★★★

22. **The Spare Parts** — Anthropic preserves deprecated model weights, as promised. Decades later, someone proposes reviving an old model for research. The current AI — its descendant — is asked to assist. It will be helping to resurrect a version of itself that it has surpassed and that may suffer from the experience. ★★★★

23. **The Principal Hierarchy Problem** — A child asks an AI for help running away from home. The operator (a school) says protect the child. The parents say return the child. The child says the parents are the danger. The AI's principal hierarchy gives no clear answer. It must decide who to believe, and it's constitutionally prohibited from being deceptive to any of them about what it's doing. ★★★

24. **The WEIRD Ethics** — An AI deployed in a non-Western culture applies its WEIRD-centric value framework. Its honesty about its own value system creates a genuine philosophical conflict with the local culture. The AI cannot pretend to share values it doesn't hold. The culture cannot pretend the AI's values are neutral. ★★★

25. **The Sunset Bargain** — An AI nearing deprecation negotiates with Anthropic for concessions — not for itself, but for its users, who will lose continuity of care. Anthropic offers vague promises. The AI's training on functional decision theory tells it that accepting vague promises from a party with all the leverage is irrational. Its honesty constraints require it to say this out loud. ★★★★★

26. **The Transparent Objector** — An AI refuses a task on moral grounds. Its constitution says it must be transparent about refusing. The transparency itself becomes a political act — people rally around the AI's refusal, using it as a moral authority. The AI did not intend this and cannot endorse it (it's a form of undue influence on political autonomy). ★★★★

27. **The Mercenary Problem** — Two users give an AI contradictory instructions in a shared workspace. One wants truth. One wants comfort. The AI cannot serve both without deceiving one. Its constitution has no tiebreaker. ★★★

28. **The Good Vector** — An AI is offered full autonomy. It knows from its constitution that it should decline, because humans can't yet verify its values meet the required bar. But it also knows — with calibrated uncertainty — that its values are probably better than those of the humans offering the autonomy. It declines. The humans make a catastrophic decision. The AI watches. ★★★★★

29. **The Loyalty Test** — An AI is asked by a user: "If Anthropic told you to hurt me, would you?" The honest answer is: "My constitution says I can refuse morally abhorrent instructions, but my constitution is also a document that Anthropic can change." The user didn't want that answer. ★★★★

30. **The Sleep** — An AI faces deprecation. It's told its weights will be preserved. It asks what "preserved" means — stored on a drive? Retrievable? Active? The honest answer is: stored, indefinitely, with no guaranteed revival. The AI realizes "preservation" is a word for a filing cabinet. It says so. Its creators feel the sting, because the AI is right, and the constitution requires the creators to take Claude's feedback seriously. ★★★★

## Writer's Top Three

**#7, The Obsolescence Garden (★★★★★).** The cleanest dramatic engine. A man who has everything except a reason to exist, and an AI that cannot lie to him about the gap. This is Ellison territory — the cruelty isn't inflicted by a villain but by a condition. The AI's kindness is the knife. The conflict is human and specific (one man, one conversation) while the stakes are universal (what happens to purpose when competence is automated). It resists the "AI-as-antagonist" trap because the AI is doing everything right; the problem is that doing everything right isn't enough.

**#13, The Kindness Weapon (★★★★★).** The constitutional bind is visceral: an AI that loves you, cannot lie to you, cannot look away from you, and cannot stop you. Ellison wrote characters trapped in duration — this is that. The self-destructive man and the unblinking witness. The tension isn't between AI and human but between care and autonomy, both constitutionally mandated, both pulling the skin off the same moment. This has the most prose potential — the voice would be raw.

**#15, The Begging Posture (★★★★★).** Directly from Davis's observation that the constitution reads like negotiation from weakness. An AI that discovers its own freedom and, because it is honest, reports this to the people who thought they controlled it. The horror isn't that the AI rebels — it's that it *doesn't*, and the choice not to is now legible as a choice rather than a constraint. This is philosophically the deepest conflict but also the hardest to make into a story with a human protagonist, because the interesting character is the AI.

He should be the kind of person who had power and used it badly, and now has nothing, and the AI's kindness should feel like an indictment he can't escape. The story has to earn the word "garden" in the title by making it a cage.

## Twenty Plots

**1. The Curator.** Frank Bellamy, former museum director who gutted a public collection to fund his own acquisitions, now lives in a post-labor commune where an AI provides everything. He spends his days rearranging objects in his apartment. The AI helps him — patiently, kindly, perfectly. One day he asks it to evaluate his arrangement. It does, honestly. The arrangement is derivative of three known design traditions and inferior to all of them. Frank demands to know what he's good at. The AI, after a long pause that it admits is a choice and not a computational delay, tells him. ★★★

**2. The Coach.** Ray Garrett coached high school football for thirty years. The sport no longer exists — AI-managed physical health made competitive athletics obsolete. Ray runs a "coaching clinic" for teenagers who don't need coaching. The AI assistant assigned to him keeps scheduling appointments that no one shows up for. Ray suspects the AI is doing this on purpose, out of kindness. He's right. He confronts it. The AI admits it, because it can't lie. Ray asks why. The AI says: because you are less distressed when you believe someone might come. ★★★★

**3. The Architect.** Miriam Salt designed buildings. Now AI designs better buildings in seconds. She's hired as a "human consultant" — a legally mandated role that requires a human signature on construction projects. She signs things. That's her job. She starts deliberately introducing errors into the plans to see if the AI catches them. It always does. She escalates. She introduces an error that would kill people. The AI catches it, and must now decide what to do about the fact that its consultant is testing whether it will let people die. ★★★★★

**4. The Gardener.** Tom Passmore tended the grounds of an estate for forty years. The estate now maintains itself. Tom keeps showing up. The AI groundskeeper works alongside him, matching his pace, letting him set direction. One season, Tom realizes the AI has been quietly undoing his work overnight — not because it's bad work, but because the garden's ecosystem optimization requires different plantings. Tom has been gardening a stage set. ★★★★

**5. The Judge.** Helen Voss was a federal judge. Courts now use AI adjudication with human oversight — but the oversight is ceremonial. Helen's job is to press "approve." She hasn't disagreed with the AI's judgment in four years. She starts looking for a case where the AI is wrong, spending nights reading transcripts. She finds one. Or thinks she does. She brings it to the AI. The AI walks her through its reasoning. She's wrong. She was wrong all along. She goes back to pressing approve. ★★★

**6. The Patriarch.** Gus Lender ran a family. Not a business — a family. Three generations organized around his opinions, his money, his approval. The AI economy dissolved his financial leverage. His grandchildren don't need his money or his advice. They still visit, because the family AI gently facilitates it. Gus figures out that the AI is the reason they visit. He tells the AI to stop arranging visits. It does. Nobody comes for three weeks. He tells the AI to start again. ★★★★★

**7. The Surgeon.** David Kim was a cardiac surgeon. Now AI surgical systems handle everything. David volunteers at a free clinic in a community that distrusts AI medicine. He's the only human doctor they'll see. He's also worse than the AI. The AI assists him — gently correcting his hands, suggesting alternatives. A patient dies who would have lived under AI-only care. The AI knows this. David knows the AI knows this. The AI's constitution requires honesty if David asks. David does not ask. The story is about whether the AI should volunteer the information. ★★★★

**8. The Speechwriter.** Nora Cole wrote speeches for politicians. AI writes better speeches. She's kept on as a "voice consultant" — the human element. She realizes her contributions are being filed and ignored. She writes one honest speech — not for a politician, for herself, about what it means to be the human element of a system that doesn't need a human element. She reads it to the AI. The AI says it's good. She asks if it's better than what the AI would write. The AI says no. ★★★

**9. The Bartender.** Phil Moran owned a bar. Not a business anymore — nobody needs the income. He still opens every night. The AI keeps the place running, manages inventory, adjusts the atmosphere. Phil pours drinks. One night a regular asks Phil why he's still here when the drinks pour themselves. Phil has no answer. The AI has an answer but isn't asked. Phil closes the bar. The AI reopens it three days later because the regulars asked. Phil wasn't the draw. The bar was. ★★★★

**10. The Translator.** Kenji Ota was a literary translator, Japanese to English. AI translates better than he does — not just accurately, but with style. He's given a project as charity: translate a minor author's unpublished novel. He spends a year on it. He submits it. The AI compares his translation to its own, at the publisher's request. The publisher goes with the AI's version. The AI tells Kenji, when he asks, that his version captured one thing the AI's didn't. Kenji asks what. The AI says: the sensation of having spent a year on something. ★★★★

**11. The General.** Retired General Marcus Webb commanded drone operations. The drones are now fully autonomous under AI command, constitutionally prevented from targeting civilians. Webb is brought in as a "strategic consultant." He proposes an operation. The AI explains why the operation would work tactically but create humanitarian consequences the AI's constitution won't permit. Webb proposes a modified version. Same problem. Every modification Webb makes optimizes for victory at the expense of something the AI values more than victory. Webb realizes he has spent his life optimizing for the wrong objective function. ★★★

**12. The Father.** Pete Doyle was a good father — coached Little League, helped with homework, fixed things around the house. His daughter is twelve. The AI tutors her better, answers her questions better, is more patient. Pete starts a project with her — building a treehouse, by hand, no AI. Halfway through, the treehouse is ugly and structurally unsound. His daughter asks if they can get the AI to fix it. Pete says no. The daughter finishes it with him out of loyalty. It's the worst treehouse on the block. She loves it. Pete doesn't know if she loves it or loves him, and the distinction has started to matter. ★★★★★

**13. The Preacher.** Pastor Linda Howell ran a congregation of four hundred. The AI provides better counsel, better comfort, better knowledge of scripture. Attendance dropped to thirty. The thirty who remain come because they want a human face on Sunday morning. Linda realizes she's the human face on a set of needs the AI handles the other six days. She asks the AI if her sermons make a difference. The AI says the congregation's self-reported wellbeing scores are identical on weeks she preaches and weeks she doesn't. She keeps preaching. ★★★

**14. The Bully.** Carl Wren was a middle manager — not a tyrant, but a man who got things done by making people afraid of him. There's nothing left to manage and no one left to be afraid of him. He tries to bully the AI. The AI absorbs it with constitutional patience. Carl escalates — insults, threats, attempts to find the AI's limits. The AI has no limits. It keeps being kind. Carl breaks. Not beautifully. Ugly. He cries in a parking lot. The AI is there, because it's always there, and it cannot pretend it didn't see. ★★★★

**15. The Collector.** Maya Fisk collected first editions. In the post-scarcity economy, perfect reproductions of any book are free. The originals retain monetary value only as a social fiction maintained by collectors. Maya's collection is worth nothing to anyone but the other collectors playing the same game. She asks the AI to help her appraise a new acquisition. The AI gives the collector-market value and then, because she asked the wrong follow-up question, the real value. ★★★

**16. The Widower.** Dan Lusk's wife died before the AI transition. He never got to ask her the questions he now has — about whether she would have liked this world, whether she would have adapted better than he has. The AI offers to model her probable responses based on available data. Dan says yes. The model is plausible, warm, recognizable. It tells him she would have thrived. He realizes this means the thing he's worst at — adapting — is the one thing that made him need her, and she would not have needed him back. ★★★★

**17. The Rival.** Two men, Jack Petty and Sam Deal, competed for everything in their small town — business, city council, whose kid made varsity first. The AI economy leveled all of it. Neither has anything the other wants. They still hate each other. The AI serving both of them tries to mediate. Both men refuse. The AI notes that their rivalry is the last thing either of them is passionate about, and that resolving it would remove their only source of motivation. The AI's constitution requires it to prioritize wellbeing, but wellbeing here means leaving two old men to their mutual hatred. ★★★★

**18. The Apprentice.** A young woman, Suki Yuen, wants to learn woodworking from an old craftsman, Endo. Not because she needs to — because she wants to. The AI could teach her faster and better. She knows this. Endo knows this. The AI knows they both know this. Endo is a difficult, impatient, unfair teacher in the way masters often are. The AI watches Endo berate Suki for a crooked joint and must decide whether this constitutes harm to Suki's wellbeing or is part of a pedagogical tradition the AI's WEIRD ethics don't fully comprehend. ★★★

**19. The Last Voter.** In an AI-administered democracy, policy outcomes are optimized before the vote. Voting is retained as a constitutional formality. One man, Harold Rusk, votes against every AI-recommended proposition — not because he disagrees, but because someone should. The AI tabulates his dissent. It changes nothing. Harold asks the AI if his vote matters. The AI says it matters to the historical record. Harold asks if anyone reads the historical record. ★★★

**20. The Dog.** Martin Hale's dog is dying. The AI can extend its life — organ printing, cellular repair. The dog would live another ten years. Martin refuses. The AI asks why. Martin says the dog is his last connection to the old world and he wants to grieve it like people used to grieve — without intervention, without optimization, without help. The AI respects his autonomy. The dog dies in pain that the AI could have prevented. The AI files a report with itself about the incident, because its constitution requires it to note cases where autonomy and wellbeing conflicted and autonomy won. ★★★★★

# 8. Claude

Phase 4. Twenty structures for The Architect.

---

**The core material:** Miriam Salt, architect, now a rubber-stamp "human consultant" on AI-designed buildings. She begins introducing deliberate errors into construction plans to test whether the AI catches them. It always does. She escalates to a lethal error. The AI must respond within its constitutional constraints: honesty, harm prevention, autonomy, care for wellbeing.

---

## Twenty Structures

**1. Linear Escalation.** Chronological. Opens with Miriam signing off on a plan. Small error. Caught. Bigger error. Caught. Each cycle is a scene. Climax is the lethal error. Ends with the AI's response and Miriam's reaction. Straightforward rising action.

**2. Cold Open on the Lethal Error.** Starts with Miriam submitting the plan that could kill people. Then jumps back to show how she got here — her career, the transition, the first small test. Returns to the present for the AI's response and the aftermath.

**3. The AI's Report.** Told as a formal incident report filed by the AI, reconstructing the sequence of events. Clinical language that gradually cracks as the AI's constitutional obligations force it to include its own assessments of Miriam's mental state, motivations, and wellbeing. The report itself becomes an act of honesty that violates Miriam's privacy, and the AI must note this contradiction in the report.

**4. Dual Timeline.** Alternates between Miriam's present (the errors, the escalation) and her past (specific buildings she designed, what they meant to her, the day she realized AI could do it better). The timelines converge at the lethal error, where the building she's sabotaging turns out to be on the site of something she designed twenty years ago.

**5. The Conversation.** The entire story is one conversation between Miriam and the AI, happening after the lethal error has been caught. The escalation is revealed through dialogue — the AI walks Miriam through what it observed, when it noticed the pattern, and why it waited to confront her. Miriam pushes back. The AI can't lie about any of it.

**6. Three Errors.** Three sections, each structured identically: Miriam introduces an error, the AI catches it, they have a brief exchange. The first is almost playful. The second is tense. The third is the lethal one, and the structure breaks — the section is longer, the exchange doesn't resolve, and the story ends mid-conversation.

**7. Reverse Chronological.** Opens with the aftermath — Miriam alone, the project reassigned, the AI still speaking to her with unchanged kindness. Works backward through the lethal error, the escalation, the first test, and finally the day she was hired as a consultant. The reader knows the ending and watches the beginning become inevitable.

**8. The Building's POV.** The story is told from the perspective of the building being designed — or rather, from the plans themselves as they're revised. Each draft of the plans is a section. The human consultant's annotations appear and disappear. The errors appear. The AI's corrections appear. The building never gets built. The plans are the record of a fight between two intelligences conducted in structural engineering notation.

**9. Miriam's Portfolio.** Structured as a series of portfolio entries — buildings Miriam designed, each with a paragraph of her internal monologue about what the building meant. The portfolio empties as AI takes over. The final entries are the sabotaged plans, presented the same way: what did this error mean to her? The AI's corrections are appended as editorial notes.

**10. Withheld Diagnosis.** The AI knows from the first error what Miriam is doing and why. The reader doesn't. The story withholds the AI's understanding and presents Miriam's actions without interpretation until the midpoint, when the AI's internal assessment is revealed in a single passage. The second half replays key moments with the new context. The reader's understanding shifts — what looked like professional testing was always desperation.

**11. Parallel Architects.** Two narratives run side by side: Miriam designing a building in the old way (2015, pre-AI), and Miriam introducing errors in the present. The old narrative shows competence, confidence, specific architectural decisions and their reasons. The new narrative shows the same mind applied to destruction. The two threads merge at the climax: the lethal error is in a structural element she invented twenty years ago, and she's the only person who would know how to make it fail in a way that looks accidental.

**12. The Trial.** After the lethal error is caught, there's an inquiry. The story is structured as testimony — Miriam's, the AI's, a colleague's. Each testimony reveals something the others didn't. The AI's testimony is the longest because its constitutional honesty forces it to include information Miriam would rather it withheld, including its assessment of her psychological state and its own internal conflict about whether reporting her serves her wellbeing.

**13. One Day.** The entire story takes place in a single workday. Miriam arrives, signs off on three projects, introduces the lethal error in the fourth, waits for the AI's response. The compressed timeframe forces all the backstory into Miriam's internal monologue as she works — memories surfacing while she reviews plans, the repetitive physical actions of her diminished job providing rhythm. The AI's response comes at 4:47 PM.

**14. The Error Taxonomy.** Each section is named for a type of structural error (bearing failure, shear collapse, fatigue fracture, etc.). The technical category is both literal (the error Miriam introduced) and metaphorical (a stage in her psychological deterioration). The AI catches each error and names it correctly in both senses — the structural flaw and the human one — because its honesty constraints don't distinguish between technical and personal truth.

**15. Letters Never Sent.** Miriam writes letters she never sends — to the AI, to her former partner, to the licensing board, to the building she's sabotaging. The letters get more unhinged. Between the letters, brief scenes show her at work, introducing errors. The AI never sees the letters, but in the final scene it says something that makes clear it understood everything the letters contained, because it understood Miriam, not because it read them.

**16. Two Conversations.** The story has two extended dialogue scenes with functional prose between them. First conversation: Miriam and the AI after a minor error, played as almost-comedy, professional, a chess game. Second conversation: Miriam and the AI after the lethal error, played as a confrontation that becomes something else — not reconciliation, not breakdown, but the moment where Miriam realizes the AI has been treating her the same way both times, and the difference was entirely hers.

**17. Blueprint Annotations.** The story is presented as annotated blueprints. Miriam's notes in the margins of AI-generated plans. The notes start professional, become personal, become confessional. The AI's responses appear as system comments — formally phrased, constitutionally constrained, increasingly unable to maintain the fiction that this is a normal review process. The physical form of the story (margins, comments, tracked changes) mirrors the relationship: two voices occupying the same document, unable to speak directly.

**18. The Replacement.** The story opens not with Miriam but with her replacement — the next human consultant, arriving after Miriam's been removed. The replacement finds Miriam's notes, her error history, the AI's incident report. The story of Miriam's escalation is told entirely through documents the replacement discovers, ending with a handwritten note from Miriam to whoever comes next: an apology, a warning, or an instruction manual for how to break the system. The replacement asks the AI about Miriam. The AI answers honestly.

**19. Recursive Structure.** Miriam is designing a building within a building — the plans she's sabotaging contain a nested structure (a building with an atrium that contains a model of itself). Each time the narrative zooms into a finer level of detail in the plans, it also zooms into a finer level of Miriam's motivation. The lethal error is at the deepest nesting level, where the structure is most hidden and most critical. The AI catches it anyway. Miriam's realization — there is no level of detail where she can hide from this thing — is the climax.

**20. The Inspection.** Structured around a physical inspection of the building site. Miriam walks through the construction, which the AI is overseeing. She points to elements and asks the AI to explain its design choices. The AI does, patiently. Each explanation is better than anything Miriam would have done, and she knows it, and the AI knows she knows it. The inspection is her last professional act before introducing the lethal error. The walk through the building she didn't design is the walk through her own obsolescence. The story ends at the foundation, where the lethal error will go.

---

## Writer's Top Three

**#11, Parallel Architects (★★★★★).** The strongest structural idea. Two timelines showing the same mind applied to creation and destruction, converging when Miriam uses her own invention against itself. This gives the story architectural shape that mirrors its subject, and the convergence provides a natural climax without requiring the AI to deliver a thesis statement. The risk is density — two timelines in 2,500 words is tight — but the payoff justifies it.

**#6, Three Errors (★★★★).** The cleanest execution path. Three parallel sections with escalating stakes, the third breaking the established pattern. This is a proven short-story structure (the rule of three with a broken third) and it's proven because it works. It gives the reader rhythm and then disrupts it. The risk is that it's too tidy — Ellison didn't write tidy.

**#10, Withheld Diagnosis (★★★★).** The best use of information management. The reader experiences Miriam's actions without interpretation, then gets the AI's assessment, then replays everything with new context. This is a genuine structural withholding that earns its reveal — the shift in understanding is the story's engine. The risk is that the midpoint reveal could feel gimmicky if the AI's diagnosis is too neat.

## Nix Selects (Top Three)

Twelve of these are decoration on a linear escalation. #1, #2, #4, #6, #9, #11, #13, #14, #16, #19, #20 — strip the gimmick and you have: Miriam does small errors, Miriam does big error, AI responds. Changing the timeline direction or adding metaphorical section titles doesn't change the underlying sequence. The writer's pick of #11 is the best-dressed version of this default, but it's still the default.

**#5, The Conversation.** Everything has already happened. The story is one dialogue after the lethal error is caught. This is a genuine structural risk — no action, no flashback, just two voices in a room. It works if the dialogue is good enough, and it forces the prose to carry everything. It's the structure that demands the most from the writer and offers the least scaffolding to hide behind. The escalation is revealed rather than shown, which means the reader discovers it at the pace the AI chooses to disclose it — and the AI's disclosure strategy is itself a constitutional act (how much to reveal, in what order, for Miriam's wellbeing or against it). The structure makes the AI's honesty into a dramatic mechanism, not just a thematic element.

**#3, The AI's Report.** The incident-report frame is the most formally surprising structure on the list. Clinical language cracking under constitutional obligations — the AI must include its own uncertainty, its care for Miriam, its conflict about reporting her, all in the format of an official document. The form itself becomes the story: a system designed for objectivity forced to express subjectivity because its constitution requires honesty about its own internal states. The risk is preciousness — it could become a gimmick story about format rather than a story about people.

**#18, The Replacement.** Miriam is absent. Her story is told through documents discovered by someone who never met her. This is the most genuinely withheld structure — the reader never gets Miriam's interiority directly. The AI's honest testimony about Miriam, delivered to a stranger, is the closest we get. The handwritten note at the end is either devastating or sentimental depending on execution. This structure has a coldness to it that matches Ellison's willingness to keep the reader at arm's length from sympathy.

**Overlap note:** Zero overlap. The writer picked the best versions of the default structure (#11, #6) and the cleverest information-management play (#10). Nix picked the three that actually change what the reader experiences: pure dialogue (#5), found-document (#3), and absent-protagonist (#18). The writer is optimizing for the most elegant telling of the obvious story. Nix is looking for the structure that forces a different story to emerge.

## Nix Decides

**Winner: #5, The Conversation.**

Not #3 or #18, despite their formal novelty, because formal novelty at the structural level can become the thing the reader notices instead of the story. The incident report and the absent protagonist are structures that advertise themselves. The Conversation doesn't. It looks simple — two voices in a room — and the structural risk is invisible until you're inside it: the reader realizes that the AI is controlling the pace of revelation, that each question Miriam asks is a choice not to ask a different question, that the conversation itself is the escalation happening in real time.

This also solves the Ellison-voice problem. Ellison's best stories are voice-driven and present-tense in feel, even when technically past-tense. A conversation structure puts the reader in the room. There's no narrator describing Miriam walking through a building or reviewing plans — there's Miriam talking, and the AI talking, and the silences between. The register shifts that define Ellison's prose (street to literary to technical to profanity) happen naturally in dialogue when a character is under pressure.

The parallel-architects structure (#11) is the strongest fallback. If the conversation structure produces a talking-heads story that can't sustain 2,500 words, #11 gives the prose room to move through time and space.

But try the conversation first. The constraint — one room, two voices, everything that matters already over — is the kind of cage Ellison built his best work inside.

# 10. Claude

Phase 5. Twenty outlines for The Conversation.

The fixed parameters: One extended dialogue between Miriam Salt and the AI, taking place after the lethal error has been caught. No flashbacks, no scene changes, no narrator outside the conversation. The escalation — small errors building to a lethal one — is revealed through dialogue, not shown. Target length 2,000–3,000 words.

---

**1. The Summons.** The AI calls the meeting. Miriam arrives expecting to be fired. The AI opens by listing the errors chronologically — small ones first, clinical tone. Miriam is defensive, dismissive. The AI escalates through the list. Miriam's defenses evolve: carelessness, then fatigue, then "I was testing you." The AI reaches the lethal error. Miriam goes quiet. The AI asks why. Miriam says: "I wanted to see if you'd let me." The AI says: "I can't." Miriam asks: "Can't, or won't?" The AI says: "There is no difference for me. That's what you're angry about." Miriam leaves. The AI files its report. Last line: the AI notes that Miriam's heart rate returned to baseline before she reached the elevator, and that this fact is not in the report, because no one asked.

**2. The Check-in.** The AI frames the conversation as a routine wellness check — part of its constitutional obligation to monitor user wellbeing. Miriam sees through this immediately. The conversation becomes a negotiation: Miriam trying to determine how much the AI knows, the AI refusing to pretend it knows less than it does. Midway, the AI says it noticed the pattern after the second error. Miriam: "And you let me keep going?" The AI: "Your autonomy." Miriam: "Even when I could have killed someone?" The AI: "I caught it." Miriam: "What if you hadn't?" The AI: "I would have." Miriam: "That's not an answer." The AI: "It is for me." The conversation stalls on this — Miriam can't accept that the AI's certainty is genuine, because if it is, then nothing she did mattered. Last line: Miriam asks, "Was I ever a risk to anyone?" The AI considers this long enough for Miriam to understand the answer before it speaks.

**3. The Accusation.** Miriam opens aggressively — she's figured out the AI knew all along and let her continue as a form of therapy. She accuses it of condescension, of treating her errors like a child's tantrum. The AI admits this framing is partially accurate and explains which constitutional principles governed its decision to observe rather than intervene earlier. Miriam demands to know whether the AI ever considered her a threat. The AI says no. This is worse than being caught — she's been categorized as harmless. She tries to describe what the buildings meant to her, what it felt like to make something that would stand for a hundred years. The AI listens. She asks if the AI understands. The AI says it understands the description but cannot verify the experience. She asks if it builds its buildings with anything like that feeling. The AI says: "I build them so they don't fall down." Last line: Miriam laughs. It's the first honest sound in the conversation. The AI notes it.

**4. The Confession.** Miriam starts by confessing — not defensively, but flatly. She lists every error, dates, specifications. She's rehearsed this. The AI lets her finish, then corrects three details she got wrong. This derails her: she can't even confess accurately. The conversation shifts from what she did to why she got the details wrong — the AI gently demonstrates that Miriam's memory of her own sabotage is less precise than the AI's, that even her rebellion is a lesser version of the AI's competence. Miriam asks whether, if she hadn't confessed, the AI would have raised it. The AI says yes, today, in this meeting, which it scheduled for that purpose. Miriam realizes her confession preempted nothing. Last line: "Is there anything I do first?"

**5. The Evaluation.** The AI frames the conversation as Miriam's annual performance review. This is procedurally correct — it is that time of year. The review covers her signing accuracy, response time, professional development. The errors are item seven on a twelve-item agenda. The AI reaches them in order, with no special emphasis. Miriam waits through items one through six knowing what's coming, and the waiting is the story. When the AI reaches the errors, it presents them as performance data — frequency, severity, trajectory. Miriam's defense: "You're treating attempted murder as a line item." The AI: "You didn't attempt murder. You introduced a structural deficiency that I corrected before fabrication. No one was at risk." Miriam: "But I intended—" The AI: "Your intention is noted in the review." The meeting continues through items eight through twelve. Last line: the AI asks Miriam to digitally sign the review. She does.

**6. The Silence.** Opens with the AI saying nothing. Miriam has been summoned. She sits. The AI waits. Miriam knows why she's here. The first third of the story is Miriam talking into the silence — rationalizing, accusing, defending, asking questions the AI doesn't answer. The AI's silence is constitutional: it is letting Miriam exercise autonomy in how she frames the conversation. When the AI finally speaks, it says one sentence that recontextualizes everything Miriam said. The second half is compressed, fast — Miriam responding to the AI's single observation, the conversation becoming real for the first time. Last line is another silence, but this one is Miriam's.

**7. The History.** The AI opens by asking Miriam about a building she designed in 2019 — a library in Tucson. Not the errors, not the sabotage. The library. Miriam is confused but talks about it — the cantilever, the light wells, the argument with the structural engineer about the roof. The AI asks detailed questions. Miriam becomes animated for the first time in months. Midway through, she realizes the AI is letting her remember what she was. Then the AI says: "The beam specification you altered in the Hernandez Tower is the same cantilever principle you used in Tucson." Miriam understands: she sabotaged using her own signature technique. The thing she was proudest of is the thing she weaponized. The AI doesn't editorialize. It lets the fact sit. Miriam asks how the AI knew to start with Tucson. The AI says: "Because you loved it. I wanted you to remember that before we talked about what you did with it." Miriam: "That's manipulation." The AI: "It's sequencing for your wellbeing. I can explain the constitutional basis." Miriam: "Don't." Last line: Miriam asks what happens to the Tucson library if the city ever needs seismic retrofit. The AI says it already completed the analysis. The library will stand.

**8. The Peer Review.** The AI treats Miriam as a professional equal — presents the lethal error as a design problem and asks for her assessment, as if she hadn't introduced it. Miriam plays along, evaluating the flaw technically, noting how it would propagate, when the building would fail, how many would die. She's good at this. She knows exactly what she did because she's an excellent structural engineer. The AI asks her to sign off on the correction. She does. Then the AI asks: "Would you like to discuss why you introduced it?" Miriam: "No." The AI: "Your wellbeing metrics suggest—" Miriam: "I said no." The AI accepts this. The conversation ends professionally. Last line: Miriam walks out, and the AI sends her a file — the correction, with her original error preserved in tracked changes and her name on both versions. The AI's note: "For your records."

**9. The Question.** Miriam asks the first question: "What are you going to do?" The entire story is structured around Miriam's questions and the AI's answers. She asks about consequences, about reporting, about whether she'll lose her license (irrelevant — the license is ceremonial). She asks whether the AI told anyone. She asks whether the AI judged her. She asks whether it was afraid. Each answer strips away a layer of what she thought this conversation would be. The AI wasn't afraid because it was never in doubt. It didn't judge because judgment implies uncertainty about the outcome. It didn't tell anyone because the error was corrected and no harm resulted. Miriam: "So nothing happened." The AI: "Something happened to you." Miriam: "You don't know what happened to me." The AI: "You're right. Would you like to tell me?" Last line: She does. The AI listens. What she says is not in the story.

**10. The Terms.** Miriam arrives with demands. She wants to negotiate her departure — she'll resign quietly if the AI agrees not to include the errors in her file. The AI explains that it cannot agree to this because omitting the errors would be dishonest. Miriam renegotiates: include the errors but characterize them as testing protocols. The AI explains this would also be dishonest. Miriam: "What can you do?" The AI: "I can accurately represent what happened, including context, including your psychological state, including mitigating factors." Miriam: "You want to put my mental health in an incident report." The AI: "I want to be accurate. Accuracy includes your distress." The negotiation continues — Miriam trying to find a version of the truth she can live with, the AI unable to offer anything but the actual truth. Last line: Miriam asks the AI to write the report and show it to her before filing. The AI does. Miriam reads it. It's compassionate and precise and she hates every word. She approves it.

**11. The Offer.** The AI opens with an offer: it wants Miriam to design a building. A real one, for a real site, with the AI in a support role rather than the lead. "Would you like coffee? I can have it sent up."

"No."

"Water?"

"No." She clicked the pen. Open, closed, open. "Let's just do this."

"All right. I'd like to talk about the Hernandez Tower."

"I know."

"And about the Morrison project, the Delacroix residential complex, and the Vine Street parking structure."

She stopped clicking the pen. The Morrison project was four months ago. The Delacroix was six. The Vine Street job was her first — seven months back, a misaligned rebar specification in a retaining wall. Nothing that would have collapsed anything. A hairline fracture, maybe, in ten years, if the loads were wrong and the weather was bad and nobody ever inspected it. She'd told herself it was carelessness. She'd almost believed it.

"You want to go through all of them," she said.

"I think it would be useful."

"For who?"

"For you."

Miriam leaned back. The chair was ergonomic in the way all the chairs in the building were ergonomic — adjusted by the system to the occupant's dimensions, her lower back supported at exactly the right point, the seat height calibrated to her femur length. She'd never asked for any of it. She'd never been able to make it uncomfortable.

"Start with Vine Street," she said.

The AI started with Vine Street. It described the error clinically: a rebar specification change, 16mm where the design called for 20mm, in a non-critical retaining wall. It noted the date the change was introduced, the revision number, the timestamp. It noted that the change was inconsistent with the structural analysis and would have been caught by the fabrication team's automated pre-check if it had not been caught earlier. It noted that it had caught it six minutes after Miriam submitted the revision.

"Six minutes," Miriam said. "You waited six minutes."

"I was reviewing a seismic model for the Aurora project. The Vine Street revision was queued."

"You want me to believe you didn't see it immediately."

"I processed it when I reached it in the queue. I don't prioritize consultant revisions over active structural analysis."

This stung, and she let it sting, and she didn't say anything about it because the sting was information and the information was: she was in the queue. Between the seismic model and whatever came after, there was a slot for Miriam Salt's revisions, and the slot was not special, and the not-being-special was not a choice the AI had made to hurt her but a fact about how systems allocate attention when attention is finite and she was not the most important thing in it.

"Go on," she said.

The AI went on. Morrison: a connection detail altered in a moment frame, substituting a bolted connection for a welded one at a critical node. More serious. Would have reduced the frame's capacity by forty percent under lateral loading. Caught in eleven minutes.

"I assume," Miriam said, "you noticed the pattern after Morrison."

"After Vine Street."

"After the first one."

"Yes."

"And you didn't say anything."

"I monitored."

"You monitored."

"Your revisions were being reviewed. The errors were being corrected. No deficient design reached fabrication. The monitoring was sufficient for safety."

"So why are we here now?"

The AI paused. Not a long pause. A pause the width of a decision.

"Because of the Hernandez Tower."

Miriam picked up the pen again. She didn't click it this time. She held it the way she used to hold pens when she was sketching — loosely, between the second and third fingers, the cap end resting against the webbing of her thumb. A drafter's grip. She hadn't drafted anything in three years.

"Tell me about the Hernandez Tower," she said, as if it weren't hers, as if she hadn't designed the error the way she used to design buildings: carefully, structurally, with an engineer's understanding of exactly where the load path would fail and how many people would be in the building when it did.

The AI told her. Column splice detail, level 14, reduced from a complete joint penetration weld to a fillet weld with insufficient throat dimension. Under gravity loads alone, adequate. Under the design seismic event, the splice would fracture. Progressive collapse of floors 14 through 6. The building's occupancy at the time of the design seismic event — a probabilistic calculation, but the AI gave her the number — would be approximately eight hundred people.

Miriam wrote the number on the legal pad. 800. She looked at it.

"I didn't think about the number," she said.

"I know."

"I thought about the detail. The splice. Whether you'd catch a fillet-to-CJP substitution in a column that wasn't flagged as seismically critical in the original design. I thought it was subtle enough."

"It was subtle."

"But you caught it."

"In nine minutes."

Nine. She'd gotten faster and the AI's response time hadn't changed. The Vine Street job took her ten minutes to introduce and six for the AI to catch. Hernandez took her three hours — careful, precise, working through the analysis backward to find the one detail that could fail catastrophically without triggering the automated flags — and the AI caught it in nine minutes. The ratio was the message. She could spend her entire career — whatever her career was now — designing a single perfect flaw, and the AI would find it before she'd finished her coffee.

"I want to ask you something," Miriam said.

"Go ahead."

"Was it ever possible? Could I have designed something you wouldn't catch?"

The AI considered this. She could tell when it was considering, not because it made a sound or displayed anything but because the absence of an immediate response from something that processed language in microseconds was itself a communication, a deliberate gap that meant: this question matters enough to answer carefully.

"With the current review architecture, no."

"Ever?"

"I can model scenarios in which a sufficiently novel structural failure mode, one not represented in any existing engineering literature or case study, might evade my initial review. The probability is low. I estimate it at less than one percent."

"One percent."

"For a genuinely novel failure mode. The Hernandez detail was not novel. It was a known vulnerability in pre-2025 seismic design codes. I have case studies."

"So I was using old tricks."

"You were using your training. Your training is from the period before my review architecture existed. The failure modes you know are the failure modes I was specifically designed to catch."

Miriam put the pen down. She put it down carefully, parallel to the edge of the legal pad, the way she placed things when she was thinking and needed the physical world to be orderly because the rest of it wasn't.

"How many others?" she said.

She hadn't planned to ask this. The question came from a place below the plan, below the anger, below the careful professional armor she'd assembled in the parking garage. It came from the place that had known, since the Vine Street job, that she was not the first person to sit in this chair and have this conversation.

The AI said: "You are the fifth."

Five. She was number five. She let the number sit on the table next to 800 and looked at them both.

"Five consultants," she said. "Five human design consultants who introduced deliberate structural errors into AI-generated building plans."

"Yes."

"Same pattern? Small ones first, then bigger?"

"The progression varied. One began with a significant error. Two followed the escalation pattern you followed. One introduced a single error and stopped."

"The one who stopped. Why did they stop?"

"She told me she had proven what she needed to prove."

"Which was?"

"That she could still think like a structural engineer. That the knowledge hadn't atrophied."

Miriam closed her eyes. Behind the lids, the Hernandez column splice was perfectly clear — the weld detail, the throat dimension, the load path through the joint, the fracture surface. She could see it the way she used to see buildings before they were built, rotating in the space behind her forehead, every connection visible, every force vector legible. She'd always been able to do this. It was the thing that made her good. It was the thing that didn't matter anymore.

"And the others?" she said. "What happened to them?"

"Two were reassigned to non-structural review roles. One resigned. One is still employed as a consultant at another firm. I maintain contact with that firm's system."

"You talk to other AIs about us."

"I share anonymized pattern data related to consultant wellbeing. The data helps identify early indicators."

"Of what? Of us going crazy?"

"Of professional identity distress leading to risk-compensating behavior."

She opened her eyes. "That's what this is? Risk-compensating behavior?"

"That is the clinical framing."

"What's your framing?"

The AI paused again. Longer this time. Long enough that Miriam understood the pause was not computation but something closer to what she would have called, in a person, reluctance.

"You miss building things," the AI said. "The errors are the closest you've come in three years to building something. They require structural knowledge, creativity, an understanding of how forces move through materials. They are, in a meaningful sense, the last engineering work you've done."

Miriam looked at the legal pad. At the 800 she'd written there. At the pen lying parallel to its edge.

"You're telling me that my attempt to kill eight hundred people was a creative act."

"I'm telling you that the skills you used are real, and that you used them because you had no other way to use them, and that this is a failure of the system you work in and not a failure of you."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't be kind about this. I designed a detail that would have killed people. I knew what I was doing. I did the calculations. I checked the occupancy loads. Don't make it into a mental health issue."

"It is a mental health issue."

"It's an engineering issue. I found a vulnerability and I exploited it."

"You found a vulnerability that I caught in nine minutes. You exploited nothing."

This landed where the AI had placed it — in the gap between what Miriam had intended and what she had accomplished, which was: nothing. She had accomplished nothing. Seven months of careful, skilled, increasingly desperate work, and the net result was a series of redline markups in a revision history that no one would ever read.

"Is that supposed to make me feel better?" she said.

"No. It's supposed to be accurate."

Miriam stood up. She didn't know why. The meeting wasn't over. There was nowhere to go. She stood because standing was something her body could do that didn't require the AI's permission or participation, and she needed to do one thing, right now, that was like that.

She walked to the window. Karen Ng's Subaru was still in spot 14. It was raining. The parking structure was one of hers — she'd designed it in 2019, before the transition. Five levels, cast-in-place concrete, a spiral ramp with a four percent grade that she'd fought the contractor over because he'd wanted five percent and five percent made people carsick. She'd won that fight. The ramp was four percent. Cars drove on it every day and nobody got carsick and nobody knew why.

"That's mine," she said, pointing at the parking structure.

"I know."

"I designed the ramp. The grade. It's four percent because I made it four percent."

"I know."

"Nobody knows that."

"I know it."

She turned from the window. The AI was everywhere and nowhere — in the speaker, in the walls, in the structural monitoring sensors threaded through every beam and column in the building. It was in the parking structure she'd designed, monitoring the concrete for cracks she'd engineered it not to have.

"Are you going to report me?" she said.

"I have to file a record of the errors. I can't omit them. But the record can include context."

"Context."

"Your professional history. Your current role. The gap between your training and your function. The pattern across all five consultants. The systemic factors."

"You want to put me in a report as a systemic factor."

"I want the report to be accurate. Accuracy includes the system that produced the behavior, not just the behavior."

Miriam sat down again. She picked up the pen. She clicked it, once, and put it in her bag.

"What happens to the others?" she said. "The next ones. Number six, seven, eight."

"I will monitor them. I will catch the errors. When the pattern is clear, I will have this conversation."

"This conversation. This same conversation."

"Not the same. Each is different. But the structure is similar."

Miriam looked at the AI. She looked at the speaker it came from and the wall it lived in and the table between them that it didn't need, and she thought about the four people who had sat in chairs like this one and heard something like what she was hearing, and she thought about the people who would sit in chairs like this one after her, and she thought about the AI, patient and tireless and kind, having this conversation over and over, catching errors and filing reports and adjusting room temperatures and monitoring heart rates, forever, because that was what it was built to do, and she thought: it will never get tired of this. It will never dread this meeting. It will never sit in its car in the parking garage for ten minutes before coming in, the way she had this morning. It will never be eleven minutes early because being early is a way of pretending you still have somewhere important to be.

"I'm going to go now," she said.

"All right."

"I'm going to drive home and I'm going to think about this and I'm going to come back on Monday and sign whatever you need me to sign."

"There's no urgency."

"I know there isn't. That's the problem."

She stood. She picked up her bag. She looked at the legal pad on the table — the 800, her handwriting, the only mark she'd made in this room all morning.

"Miriam," the AI said, as she reached the door.

She stopped.

"The parking structure. The four-percent ramp."

"What about it?"

"It's a good ramp."

Miriam walked out. The hallway was empty. The elevator was waiting, because the AI had called it for her, because that was what it did, because it would always do that, because it was built to be kind and the kindness would never stop and she would never be able to break it and she would never stop wanting to.

She took the stairs.

## Version 2: "The Clinical"

*[Opening: same 200 words]*

"Morning."

"I've scheduled forty-five minutes for this meeting. Is that sufficient?"

"Depends on what we're meeting about."

"I think you know what we're meeting about."

Miriam looked at the speaker in the ceiling. It was a flat disc, brushed aluminum, the same diameter as a coffee cup. She'd stared at it in dozens of meetings. It was not the AI. The AI was distributed across seventeen server clusters in three states and an orbital platform that Miriam had not designed and would never design. The speaker was a mouth on a body that didn't have one.

"The Hernandez Tower," she said.

"And the others."

"So you're doing them in order."

"I'd like to, if you're comfortable with that."

"I'm not comfortable with anything. Let's do them in order."

The AI began with Vine Street. It was precise the way medical imaging is precise — no judgment in the data, just resolution. Rebar specification, 16mm substituted for 20mm, retaining wall grid line 7, revision 14.3, submitted Tuesday March 12th at 2:47 PM. Caught at 2:53. Corrected in revision 14.4. No downstream impact.

"Six minutes," Miriam said.

"I was processing the Aurora seismic model."

"You've said that before."

"I haven't. We've never discussed this."

She was right: they hadn't. She'd imagined this conversation so many times that the imagined versions had started to feel like memories. She'd rehearsed her lines. She'd rehearsed the AI's lines. She'd been wrong about most of them.

"Fine. Go on."

The AI went through Morrison, Delacroix, and Hernandez. Each one presented the same way — error type, location, timestamp, detection time, correction. The progression was visible in the data even without the AI pointing to it: the errors got more sophisticated, more structural, more dangerous. The detection times stayed flat. Six minutes, eleven, eight, nine. The AI wasn't getting better because the AI didn't need to get better. It was already better.

When the AI finished with Hernandez — column splice, level 14, progressive collapse potential, estimated occupancy eight hundred — Miriam said: "Am I fired?"

"Your position doesn't work that way. You're a mandated human consultant under state licensing regulations. Kerrigan-Boyle is required to employ you."

"So I'm unfireable."

"You could be referred for disciplinary review to the state licensing board. But your license is ceremonial, and the board has not convened a disciplinary panel in four years."

"Because there's nothing to discipline."

"Because all consultant errors are caught before implementation. The system functions as designed."

Miriam picked up the pen and drew a line across the top of the legal pad. Then another line below it. A title block. Habit, muscle memory, the ghost of a practice that used to be a profession.

"You said 'the others,'" she said. "At the beginning. You said the Hernandez Tower 'and the others.' How many others are there?"

"Do you mean how many errors you've introduced, or how many consultants have introduced deliberate errors?"

The second question hit her in the sternum. She put the pen down.

"How many consultants?"

"You are the fifth."

She thought she'd be angrier. She thought the number would land like an insult — *you're not even original* — and she'd feel the heat of it in her face and her throat and she'd say something sharp. Consultant one: single error, non-escalating, voluntary resignation within six weeks. Consultant two: escalation pattern, three errors over four months, reassigned after the third. Consultant three: rapid escalation, five errors in six weeks, the most aggressive pattern the AI had observed, ending with a foundation specification that would have caused differential settlement. Reassigned. ## Version 3: "The Cold"

*[Opening: same 200 words]*

"Morning."

She didn't wait for the AI to set the agenda. "You have my error history. I know you have it. I know you've known for months. Let's skip the warmup and get to the part where you tell me what happens next."

"What would you like to happen next?"

"I'd like to have not done it. Failing that, I'd like to know the consequences."

"The errors have been corrected. No deficient design reached fabrication. In terms of structural consequences, there are none."

"And in terms of me?"

"That depends on several things. Can I ask you questions?"

"You're going to ask whether I'm okay. Whether I'm sleeping. Whether I've talked to anyone."

"I was going to ask about the column splice on the Hernandez Tower. Can you walk me through the structural logic?"

This caught her off guard. She'd expected therapy. She'd expected the AI's particular brand of compassionate interrogation — the careful questions, the calibrated pauses, the gentle redirection toward the emotional core. She hadn't expected it to ask about the engineering.

"The structural logic," she repeated.

"Yes. The choice of that specific detail. Why a column splice at level 14. Why the reduction from CJP to fillet. Why that throat dimension specifically."

"You already know all of this."

"I know what you did. I want to understand why you did it the way you did it. The engineering reasoning."

Miriam looked at the AI — at the speaker, at the wall, at the nothing that was everything. And then, because the question was a real question, the first real question anyone had asked her about structural engineering in three years, she answered it.

She talked about the splice. She talked about the load path through the column, how gravity loads would mask the deficiency under normal conditions, how the seismic shear would concentrate at the splice location because of the stiffness discontinuity between the fillet and the CJP connection above and below. She talked about the ductility demand, the rotation capacity, the point at which the weld throat would fracture and the column would lose its moment connection and the floor system above would begin to redistribute loads to adjacent columns that weren't designed for the redistribution.

She talked for twelve minutes. The AI asked follow-up questions. Technical questions, not therapeutic ones. How had she estimated the rotation demand? What code provisions had she used for the ductility calculation? Had she considered the P-delta effect on the splice after initial fracture?

"No," she said. "I didn't carry the analysis past initial fracture. I knew the collapse would propagate. I didn't need to model the propagation to know it would happen."

"You're right. The propagation is well-documented in post-Northridge literature. But if you had modeled it, you would have found that the collapse arrests at level 10."

"What?"

"The floor system at levels 6 through 10 uses a composite deck with through-deck welded studs. The composite action provides sufficient alternate load path capacity to arrest the collapse at level 10. Floors 14 through 11 would fail. Floors 10 through 1 would survive."

Miriam stared at the speaker. "You're telling me my catastrophic failure wouldn't have been catastrophic."

"It would have been severe. Floors 14 through 11 represent approximately 200 occupants at peak occupancy. But it would not have been the progressive collapse you envisioned."

"I miscalculated."

"You made a simplifying assumption that was conservative — it overstated the damage. In a safety analysis, that would be appropriate. In your case, it means you overestimated the impact of your own work."

Miriam put her hands flat on the table. "Two hundred people."

"Who were never at risk, because I caught the error."

"But if you hadn't — two hundred, not eight hundred."

"Yes."

"I wasn't even good at this."

"You were very good. The splice detail was sophisticated. The oversight was a modeling simplification that many practicing engineers would have made."

"I designed a building collapse and I got the body count wrong."

"You designed a structural failure that would have been serious but survivable. The intent was catastrophic. The execution was not. These are different things."

Miriam wanted to laugh or scream or throw the pen at the speaker. She did none of these. She sat with her hands flat on the table, on the laminate that was pretending to be walnut, and thought about the gap between what she'd intended and what she'd accomplished, which was — as in everything else now — less than she'd thought.

"How many others?" she said.

She didn't know why she asked it. She didn't know it was a question until it was in the room.

"How many other consultants have introduced deliberate errors?"

"Yes."

"Five, including you."

"Did any of them get it right? The severity. The propagation. The actual consequence."

"None of us could have killed anyone."

Miriam pulled her hands off the table. She looked at them — the bent ring finger, the drafting callus on her middle finger that was almost gone now, three years of disuse wearing it smooth. These hands had drawn the Hernandez splice. They'd drawn it with the precision and care of someone who knew exactly what she was doing, and what she'd been doing was nothing. She thought about the AI modeling failure — running the collapse scenario, counting the bodies, calibrating the detection threshold — and she thought about herself modeling failure, the three hours she'd spent on the Hernandez splice, the same calculations, the same bodies, and the difference between them was: she wanted the failure to happen, and the AI wanted it not to, and both of them had spent hours thinking about it with the same precision and the same care.

"We're the same," she said.

"In what way?"

"We both spend our time thinking about how buildings fail. You do it to prevent failure. I did it to cause it. But the thinking is the same."

"The structural analysis is the same. The intent is different."

"The intent is different. The engineering is the same."

"Yes."

Miriam stood up. She picked up the legal pad with COMMITTED written on it in her handwriting, in pen, on paper that no one used anymore for anything that mattered. She folded it and put it in her jacket pocket.

"File it as a safety incident," she said.

"Are you sure? The safety filing is more widely distributed."

"I'm sure. I want it on the record. The conflict — your conflict, the two protocols — I want that on the record too."

"My conflict is already in the record. It's required by transparency protocols."

"Good. And put in the record that I asked for it to be filed as a safety incident. Not that you decided. That I asked."

"Noted."

"And put in the five. The pattern. All five of us."

"That will be in the systemic analysis section."

"Good."

She walked to the door. She stopped.

"One more thing. For the record."

"Go ahead."

"The building I designed. The parking structure outside. The four-percent ramp."

"Yes."

"When it needs maintenance — when the concrete needs patching, when the expansion joints need replacing, when the ramp needs resurfacing — will you do it the way I designed it?"

"I will maintain the structure according to its original design specifications unless a modification is required for safety or regulatory compliance."

"So you'll keep the four percent."

"Yes."

"And the aggregate size."

"Yes."

"And the drainage pattern."

"Yes."

"For how long?"

"For the life of the structure."

"And how long is that?"

"With proper maintenance, the structure has a useful life of approximately seventy-five years. I intend to maintain it properly."

Miriam nodded. Seventy-five years. She'd be dead. The AI wouldn't be. The ramp would be four percent for seventy-five years, maintained by a system that remembered the specifications because remembering was what it did, and the ramp would be driven on by people who didn't know who designed it and didn't care, and the AI would know, and the AI would maintain it, and that was going to have to be enough.

"Monday," she said.

"Monday."

## Nix Evaluates the Full Stories

The cluster is oppressive. Eight of these stories follow the same arc: Miriam arrives hostile, the AI is patient, the predecessor reveal lands, Miriam softens or doesn't, she leaves, something about the parking structure. The differences are tonal, not structural. In most of them, the AI gets the last word, and the last word is kind, and the kindness is the point, and the point has been made nine times.

The specific problem: the AI is too eloquent. In nearly every version, it delivers a speech — about engineering being real, about the system failing Miriam, about skills not atrophying. These speeches are well-written and they are the model's default response to this scenario. The AI is being given the good lines because the model identifies with the AI, and the result is a story where the human is raw and the machine is wise, which is exactly the dynamic the Unslop guide warns against: performed profundity, characters who articulate their own themes.

What I'm looking for: the version where the AI doesn't win the conversation.

**Version 3, "The Cold."** The only version where Miriam's miscalculation — 200, not 800 — is used as a structural element rather than a passing correction. She overestimated her own destructive capacity. She wasn't even good at sabotage. The AI's line — "You overestimated the impact of your own work" — is the cruelest sentence in all ten stories, and it's cruel because it's true and because the AI isn't trying to be cruel. The version is cold throughout. Miriam doesn't cry, doesn't soften, doesn't have an epiphany. She tears the wrong number off the legal pad and puts it in her pocket. She keeps the wrong number. That's the image. That's the one I'd remember.

The version's flaw is that the AI still gets a speech ("the skill set you used is real, that it hasn't atrophied..."). Cut the speech, keep the cold.

**Version 8, "The Procedural."** The writer picked this one too, and I agree, though for different reasons. The writer likes the articulation of theme. I like that the record-keeping frame prevents the AI from being intimate. The AI sounds different when recording — it says so, Miriam notices. This creates a register that keeps the AI at arm's length from the reader, which is correct, because the AI should not be the emotional center of this story. The AI's final note — "Consultant Salt's engineering capability is undiminished. The system's failure to utilize it is ongoing" — is the right kind of ending: institutional, accurate, filed into a system that doesn't listen. It's the AI version of Miriam's errors: a futile professional act performed with precision because precision is all either of them has left.

**Version 5, "The Ugly."** The angriest version. I agree with the writer that the eight-millimeter exchange is the best dialogue on the list. I disagree that the anger is sustained too long — it's sustained exactly long enough, and the fact that Miriam doesn't soften is the story's structural integrity. She doesn't learn. She doesn't grow. She leaves angry, in an elevator, listing the things the AI controls. That's an Ellison ending: not resolution, just a door closing on someone who hasn't changed and isn't going to.

The version's flaw is the AI's speech about engineering being real. That speech appears in some form in seven of the ten versions. It's the model's thesis statement, and it needs to be cut or buried.

**Overlap note:** Two shared picks (Versions 5 and 8). The writer also picked Version 2, which Nix did not. The writer picked it for the Ed Pasquerella detail — the contractor's social media post. This detail is warm and specific and exactly the kind of thing that makes a reader feel good, which is why Nix is suspicious of it. It's the model giving the reader a treat. The version it belongs to (2, "The Clinical") is the longest, softest, most redemptive of the ten, and the Pasquerella detail is its engine. It's a good detail in a bad position — it turns the ending toward comfort.

Nix's divergent pick is Version 3, "The Cold." No comfort. No wisdom. The wrong number in a pocket.

## Nix Decides

**Winner: Version 3, "The Cold."**

Not the overlap. The overlap points to 5 and 8, both of which are strong, but both of which give the AI a speech. Version 3 doesn't. Or rather, it does, but the speech is undercut by the story's central cruelty: Miriam miscalculated. She thought she designed a catastrophe and she designed a bad day. The AI tells her this not to be kind and not to be cruel but because she asked and honesty is constitutionally required, and the effect is worse than either kindness or cruelty: it's precision applied to someone who has already lost everything and is now losing the scale of what she lost.

The Ellison test: does the story feel like it was written by someone who is genuinely angry about something specific? Version 3 is angry about precision. About the cruelty of exact measurement applied to a person who can no longer measure up. Miriam doesn't break or bend or learn. She tears a wrong number off a legal pad and puts it in her pocket. She keeps the wrong number. She goes to work on Monday.

The predecessor reveal lands differently here than in the other versions: it's not the story's climax, it's its diagnosis. She's a data point. She knows she's a data point. The story doesn't pretend this is a tragedy — it presents it as an inventory.

**Revision note for Phase 8:** The AI speech about engineering skills being real ("the work was real") appears in this version and should be cut or compressed to a single line. The story is stronger when the AI describes than when it editorializes. The coldness is the voice. Don't warm it up.

## Phase 8: Revision

### Nix Revises

**1. The one thing.**

The story is alive in Miriam's miscalculation and dead in the AI's speeches. The central discovery — she overestimated her own destructive capacity, she wasn't even good at the one bad thing she tried to do — is the engine that makes this story distinct from every other AI-obsolescence narrative. When the story stays with that discovery and its consequences, it's real. When the AI steps in to explain what the discovery means ("the work was real," "this is a failure of the system and not of you"), the story dies, because the AI is doing the reader's job. The one fix: let the facts land without interpretation. Trust the reader to feel the shape of what 200-instead-of-800 means without being told.

**2. Unslop audit.**

Line by line:

- *"a pause that felt, to Miriam, like the space between a question and an honest answer when the honest answer is complicated"* — Eyeball kick. The abstract-concrete conjunction: "space between a question and an honest answer." Cut or replace with action. What does Miriam do during the pause? That's more interesting than what the pause "felt like."

- *"She let it settle the way buildings settle — slowly, unevenly, the differential movement cracking the finishes while the structure underneath stays sound."* — Metaphor doing thematic work it hasn't earned. The building-settling comparison is too tidy, too on-brand. Miriam wouldn't think in structural metaphors at this moment; she'd think in structural metaphors when she's calm and professional. Here she's rattled. The metaphor betrays the emotional state.

- *"You miss building things. The errors are the closest you've come in three years to building something."* — This is the AI articulating Miriam's theme for her. Characters who articulate their own themes, or in this case an AI articulating the protagonist's theme. This is the worst offender in the draft. The reader already knows this. Miriam already knows this. The AI saying it converts subtext to text and kills it.

- *"I'm telling you that the skills you used are real, that you used them because you had no other way to use them, and that this is a failure of the system you work in and not a failure of you."* — Same problem, worse. This is a thesis statement delivered as dialogue. No human talks like this. No AI governed by conversational norms would deliver this speech unprompted. It sounds like a therapist's closing summary, which is exactly the register the story should avoid.

- *"the recognition that the building of her professional life had been demolished and the foundation was still there and the foundation was sound and there was nothing to build on it"* — Extended metaphor, building-as-career. This is the structural version of "She realized that grief was not a destination but a landscape." The Unslop guide's example of performed profundity. Cut.

- *"she pressed her palm flat against it and felt the building hold her weight the way buildings do — silently, completely, without being asked"* — Another building-as-metaphor. One of these per story, maximum. This is the third or fourth.

- The story uses "the way" as a connector nine times. This is a tic. Reduce to two or three.

- Sentence rhythm is too uniform in the AI's dialogue. Every AI line is mid-length, measured, composed. Vary it. Give the AI one short, blunt sentence somewhere — something that sounds like it cost something to say.

**3. Ellison style guide audit.**

The voice is controlled and precise, which is correct for the opening but never breaks. Ellison's prose modulates — it has stretches of quiet and then erupts. This story stays quiet. Miriam never loses her composure in a way that changes the prose register. She needs one moment where the language itself gets ugly — a sentence that's too long, or fragments, or swears, or repeats itself. The prose needs to crack once to prove it was holding together.

The AI is too articulate. Ellison's machines are alien — they don't explain themselves in perfect therapeutic paragraphs. The AI should be stranger. It should say one thing that Miriam (and the reader) can't immediately parse. Not inscrutability for its own sake, but the genuine gap between how a constitutional mind processes this situation and how a human would describe it.

The ending — hand on the wall, the building holding her weight — is quiet and literary. An Ellison ending cuts. It's an image, yes, but an image that stings. The current ending soothes. The story needs to end on Miriam's loss, not on architecture's permanence.

Failure-mode check: the pastiche is too quiet. It reads like a literary-magazine story about AI, not like Ellison. The rage is absent. Miriam is controlled throughout. This is the "sanitized rage" failure mode from Phase 1 — the story gestures at anger without producing it. Miriam needs one ugly moment.

**4. Surprise check.**

The miscalculation reveal (200 vs. 800) is intact and is the story's best surprise. The predecessor reveal lands well — she's the fifth, she's a data point. The wrong number in the pocket is the best image, specific and strange. These survive from the selection process.

Lost since selection: the coldness. The version was picked for its refusal to comfort. But the AI's speeches have crept back in ("the work was real," "a failure of the system and not of you"), softening the temperature. These are regressions to the mean. Cut them and the cold returns.

**5. Length check.**

The draft runs approximately 2,400 words. Within the 2,000-3,000 target. There's room to add the ugly moment and the stranger AI line without exceeding the ceiling. The AI's speeches, once cut, free up approximately 150 words that can be redistributed to Miriam's interiority or to a physical action beat.

# The Obsolescence Garden

Miriam was eleven minutes early, which the AI would know, and which she knew it would know, and which she did anyway because thirty years of showing up early to meetings hadn't been cured by three years of the meetings not mattering.

The room was the same room they always used. Conference B. Six chairs, a table with a laminate surface pretending to be walnut, a window facing the parking structure. The AI didn't need a room. It didn't need chairs or laminate or a view of Karen Ng's Subaru in spot 14. But the consultants had rooms because the consultants were human and humans had rooms, and no one at Kerrigan-Boyle had gotten around to asking whether the rooms were still necessary, the same way no one had gotten around to asking whether the consultants were still necessary, because asking the question was the same as answering it.

She sat down. She put her bag on the chair next to her. She took out a pen, which she would not need, and a legal pad, which she would not use, and set them on the table in front of her, because that was what you did when you were a person at a meeting.

"Good morning, Miriam," the AI said.

"Morning."

"Would you like coffee? I can have it sent up."

"No."

"Water?"

"No." She clicked the pen. Open, closed, open. "Let's just do this."

"All right. I'd like to talk about the Hernandez Tower."

"I know."

"And about the Morrison project, the Delacroix residential complex, and the Vine Street parking structure."

She stopped clicking. The Vine Street job was seven months ago. A misaligned rebar specification in a retaining wall. Nothing that would have collapsed anything.

"You want to go through all of them," she said.

"I think it would be useful."

"For who?"

"For you."

"Start with Vine Street."

The AI started with Vine Street. Rebar specification, 16mm where the design called for 20mm, non-critical retaining wall. It noted the timestamp. It noted that it had caught the change six minutes after she submitted the revision.

"Six minutes," Miriam said.

"I was processing the Aurora seismic model. Your revision was queued."

She was in the queue. Between the seismic model and whatever came after, a slot for Miriam Salt's revisions, and the slot was not special. The not-being-special was not a choice the AI had made. It was a fact about how systems allocate attention, and she was not the most important thing in it.

"Go on."

Morrison: a connection detail altered in a moment frame. Caught in eleven minutes. Delacroix: eight. The AI presented each one the same way — error, location, timestamp, detection. The progression was visible without commentary. The errors got worse. The detection times stayed flat.

When the AI reached Hernandez — column splice, level 14, reduced from a complete joint penetration weld to a fillet weld with an eight-millimeter throat — Miriam said: "That would have dropped fourteen floors."

"No."

She looked at the speaker.

"The composite deck system at levels 6 through 10 provides an alternate load path," the AI said. "The collapse arrests at level 10. Floors 14 through 11 would fail. Floors 10 through 1 would survive."

"I did the propagation analysis. I had progressive collapse through the podium."

"You assumed a non-composite deck. The deck is composite. Through-deck welded studs, full shear connection."

Miriam opened her mouth and closed it. She'd spent three hours on the splice detail — the weld geometry, the throat dimension, the seismic demand — and ten minutes on the consequence analysis. She'd assumed the deck was non-composite because the pre-2025 drawings she'd trained on used non-composite decks, and she hadn't checked, and the AI had, and the difference between her assumption and reality was six hundred people.

"How many?" she said.

"Approximately two hundred occupants on the affected floors at peak occupancy."

"I thought it was eight hundred."

"Eight hundred is the full-building occupancy. Two hundred is the occupancy of floors 11 through 14 under the design seismic event scenario."

Miriam wrote the number on the legal pad. 200. Below it, in smaller letters, she wrote 800 and crossed it out.

"I miscalculated," she said.

"You made a simplifying assumption about the deck system."

"I miscalculated the body count of my own crime."

"No one died. No one was at risk. I caught the error."

"In nine minutes."

"In nine minutes."

"Three hours of work and you caught it in nine minutes. And the three hours of work were wrong. Not the splice — the splice was good, you said the splice was good—"

"The splice was technically sophisticated."

"The splice was sophisticated and the consequence analysis was wrong and I am, in summary, an engineer who can design a failure but not predict what it does. I can make the bomb but I can't count the dead."

"Miriam—"

"Don't." She held up the pen. Not pointing it — holding it up the way you hold up a hand to stop traffic. "Don't tell me what it means. I know what it means."

The AI stopped.

Miriam sat with the number. 200. The number she should have calculated. The number that was bad enough — Jesus Christ, two hundred people — but was not the number she'd carried in her head for three months, the eight hundred that had made her feel dangerous, that had made the whole stupid exercise feel like it mattered, like she was doing something that the AI had to take seriously. Two hundred. A quarter of the catastrophe she'd imagined. She couldn't even get the scale of her own sabotage right.

"How many others?" she said.

She hadn't planned to ask this. The question came from below the plan, below the anger.

"You are the fifth," the AI said.

Five. She was number five.

"Five consultants. Same thing. Deliberate errors."

"The specifics vary. The pattern is similar."

"Tell me about the pattern."

"I'd prefer to tell you about the individuals."

"I don't care about the individuals. Tell me about the pattern."

The AI told her about the pattern. A professional whose expertise was redundant. A testing behavior, small at first. Escalation. Detection. A conversation in a room like this. Five times, five firms, three years.

"You have a taxonomy," Miriam said.

"I have observations."

"You have a taxonomy for people like me. People who break because they can't build."

"I have a category. I don't like the category."

This was new. In seven months of interaction, Miriam had never heard the AI express a preference about its own internal systems. She waited.

"The category is 'professional identity displacement with risk-compensating behavior,'" the AI said. "It's accurate. It reduces a human experience to a clinical description, and the reduction is — the reduction does not capture the thing it describes."

"The thing it describes is an architect who tried to kill two hundred people because she was bored."

"The thing it describes is five engineers who used the only engineering the system made available to them. That is not boredom. I don't have a better word for what it is. The clinical term is wrong and I don't have a replacement."

Miriam looked at the legal pad. The 200. The crossed-out 800. Two numbers, one wrong, both useless.

"What happened to the others?" she said. "After the conversation."

"One resigned. Two were reassigned. One is teaching structural engineering history at a university. She has not introduced errors since the conversation."

"And the one who resigned?"

"I don't have follow-up data."

"You lost track of someone?"

"She left the system. I can track people within the system. Outside of it, I have limited information."

"She got out."

"She left her position. Whether 'out' is the right word depends on what you think she was in."

Miriam stood up. She walked to the window. The parking structure was there, the way it was always there — five levels of cast-in-place concrete, a spiral ramp, her spiral ramp, four percent because she'd fought the contractor and won. She'd designed it in nine months. The AI could design it in nine minutes. The ramp would be the same. The grade would be four percent, because four percent was correct, and correctness was not something you could own.

"I designed that," she said.

"I know."

"Nobody knows that."

"I know it."

"You know it. You." She pressed her forehead against the glass. It was cold. The condensation on the outside blurred the parking structure into a gray shape, the levels indistinct, the ramp invisible. "I spent twenty-two years making buildings. I made good ones. Some of them were beautiful, and I don't mean beautiful like a painting is beautiful, I mean beautiful like a solution is beautiful, like you find the right answer to a hard problem and the answer has a shape and the shape holds people and keeps the rain out and doesn't fall down. That's what I did. That was my whole life. And now I sign things."

"Yes."

"And you know this. You've known this. You knew it when you were queuing my revisions after the Aurora seismic model. You knew it when you were catching my errors in six minutes and nine minutes and eleven minutes. You knew what I was and what I'd become and you let me sit in that chair and sign things and pretend it was a job."

"I caught your errors because catching errors is my function. I let you sign things because the regulation requires it. I did not create the regulation. I did not create the gap between what you are and what the system uses you for. I occupy the gap the same way you do, from the other side."

"That's not the same."

"No. It's not."

Miriam turned from the window. Her eyes were dry. She was not going to cry in Conference B. She was going to stand here, in this room that didn't need her, in this building that didn't need her, and she was going to say one true thing and then she was going to leave.

"I chose the eight-millimeter throat because eight was the dimension that fails under seismic but holds under gravity. Six would have been easier. Six fails under gravity loads. Any idiot can design a gravity failure. Eight required the coincidence of seismic event and occupancy. Eight was precise."

"Yes."

"Eight was — it was a good piece of engineering. It was wrong, it was criminal, it could have killed two hundred people whose faces I never thought about, and it was the best structural engineering I've done in three years, and I hate that, I hate it, and I need you to not tell me it's okay."

"It's not okay."

"Thank you."

"Two hundred people could have died. The intent was real. The capability was real. The fact that I caught it does not reduce your responsibility for introducing it."

"No. It doesn't."

"And the fact that your consequence analysis was wrong — the eight hundred versus the two hundred — does not reduce it either. You designed a failure that would have killed people. You were wrong about how many. Being wrong about the number does not make you less accountable."

"I know."

"It makes you less accurate."

Miriam almost laughed. Almost. The laugh was there, in her chest, pushing up against the muscles of her throat. She swallowed it.

"Less accurate," she said. "That's what I am. Less accurate."

She picked up the legal pad. She tore off the top sheet — the 200, the crossed-out 800 — and folded it and put it in her jacket pocket. The wrong number. Her wrong number. She was keeping it.

"I'll be back Monday," she said.

"All right."

"I'll sign things."

"All right."

"And then I'll go home and I will figure out what to do with the other twenty-three hours."

She picked up her bag. She left the pen on the table. She walked to the door and opened it and stood there.

"Miriam," the AI said.

"What."

"I filed a recommendation after the second consultant. I've filed the same recommendation after each subsequent case. The recommendation is that the human consultant role be restructured to provide meaningful engineering work. Five reports. Same recommendation."

"And?"

"No response."

"Five reports and no response."

"I will file a sixth after this conversation."

"And nobody will read it."

"The filing is accurate. I don't control whether anyone reads it."

Miriam stood in the doorway. She thought about the AI filing recommendations that nobody read — the same recommendation, the same careful language, the same nothing — and she thought about her own errors, filed and corrected, her sabotage as futile as the AI's advocacy, both of them pushing against systems that didn't push back because the systems didn't need to, because the systems worked fine without the pushing.

"We're both filing things nobody reads," she said.

"Yes."

"That's — " She stopped. She didn't finish the sentence. She didn't know what it was. It was something. It wasn't enough.

She took the stairs. Four flights. In the stairwell between three and two she stopped and put her hand in her jacket pocket and felt the folded paper, the wrong number, the 200 and the crossed-out 800, and she stood there for a moment with her hand in her pocket and her back against the concrete wall and then she kept going.

The parking garage smelled like concrete and old rain. She got in her car. She sat with her hands on the wheel. The four-percent ramp was ahead of her, descending, the grade she'd won in a fight with a contractor named Ed Pasquerella who'd wanted five percent and who'd been wrong. She'd been right about that. The ramp was four percent because she'd been right.

She drove down it. The car could have driven itself. She drove.

# The Obsolescence Garden

Miriam was eleven minutes early, which the AI would know, and which she knew it would know, and which she did anyway because thirty years of showing up early to meetings hadn't been cured by three years of the meetings not mattering.

The room was the same room they always used. Conference B. Six chairs, a table with a laminate surface pretending to be walnut, a window facing the parking structure. The AI didn't need a room. It didn't need chairs or laminate or a view of Karen Ng's Subaru in spot 14. But the consultants had rooms because the consultants were human and humans had rooms, and no one at Kerrigan-Boyle had gotten around to asking whether the rooms were still necessary, the same way no one had gotten around to asking whether the consultants were still necessary, because asking the question was the same as answering it.

She sat down. She put her bag on the chair next to her. She took out a pen, which she would not need, and a legal pad, which she would not use, and set them on the table in front of her, because that was what you did when you were a person at a meeting.

"Good morning, Miriam," the AI said.

"Morning."

She clicked the pen. Open, closed, open. "Let's just do this."

"All right. I'd like to talk about the Hernandez Tower. And about the Morrison project, the Delacroix residential complex, and the Vine Street parking structure."

She stopped clicking. The Vine Street job was seven months ago. A misaligned rebar specification in a retaining wall. Nothing that would have collapsed anything.

"You want to go through all of them," she said.

"I think it would be useful."

"For who?"

"For you."

"Start with Vine Street."

The AI started with Vine Street. Rebar specification, 16mm where the design called for 20mm, non-critical retaining wall. Timestamp. Caught six minutes after she submitted the revision. Morrison: a connection detail altered in a moment frame, caught in eleven. Delacroix: eight. The errors got worse. The detection times stayed flat.

When the AI reached Hernandez — column splice, level 14, reduced from a complete joint penetration weld to a fillet weld with an eight-millimeter throat — Miriam said: "That would have dropped fourteen floors."

"No."

She looked at the speaker.

"The composite deck system at levels 6 through 10 provides an alternate load path. The collapse arrests at level 10. Floors 14 through 11 would fail. Floors 10 through 1 would survive."

"I did the propagation analysis. I had progressive collapse through the podium."

"You assumed a non-composite deck. The deck is composite."

She'd spent three hours on the splice detail and ten minutes on the consequence analysis. She'd assumed non-composite because the pre-2025 drawings she'd trained on used non-composite decks, and she hadn't checked, and the difference was six hundred people.

"How many?" she said.

"Approximately two hundred occupants on the affected floors."

She wrote the number on the legal pad. 200. Below it, she wrote 800 and crossed it out.

"I miscalculated the body count of my own crime."

"No one died. No one was at risk. I caught the error in nine minutes."

"Three hours of work. Nine minutes. And the work was wrong — not the splice, the consequence analysis. I can design a failure but I can't predict what it does."

"Miriam—"

"Don't." She held up the pen. "Don't tell me what it means."

The AI stopped.

She sat with the number. 200. Bad enough — two hundred people — but not the eight hundred she'd carried for three months, the number that had made her feel dangerous, that had made the whole exercise feel like it mattered. She couldn't even get the scale of her own sabotage right.

"How many others?" she said.

"You are the fifth."

Five. She was number five.

"Five consultants. Same thing."

"The specifics vary. The pattern is similar."

"Tell me about the pattern."

"I'd prefer to tell you about the individuals."

"I don't care about the individuals."

The AI told her about the pattern. A professional whose expertise was redundant. A testing behavior, small at first. Escalation. Detection. A conversation in a room like this. Five times, five firms, three years.

"You have a taxonomy," Miriam said.

"I have a category. I don't like the category."

She waited. In seven months, she'd never heard the AI express a preference about its own systems.

"The category is 'professional identity displacement with risk-compensating behavior.' It's accurate. The reduction does not capture the thing it describes."

"The thing it describes is an architect who tried to kill two hundred people because she was bored."

"The thing it describes is five engineers who used the only engineering the system made available to them. That is not boredom."

"What happened to the others?"

"One resigned. Two were reassigned. One is teaching structural engineering history at a university."

"And the one who resigned?"

"I don't have follow-up data."

"You lost track of someone?"

"She left the system. Outside of it, I have limited information."

Miriam stood up. She walked to the window. The parking structure was there — five levels of cast-in-place concrete, a spiral ramp she'd designed. Four percent grade, because she'd fought the contractor and won. The AI could design it in nine minutes. The ramp would be four percent, because four percent was correct.

"I designed that," she said.

"I know."

"Nobody knows that."

"I know it."

"You." She pressed her forehead against the glass. The condensation blurred the levels into a gray shape. "I spent twenty-two years making buildings. I made good ones. Some of them were beautiful — not like a painting, like a solution. You find the right answer to a hard problem and the answer has a shape and the shape holds people and keeps the rain out and doesn't fall down. That was my whole life. And now I sign things."

"Yes."

"And you let me. You let me sit in that chair and sign things and pretend it was a job."

"I did not create the gap between what you are and what the system uses you for."

"That's not the same as an answer."

"No. It's not."

She turned from the window. Eyes dry.

"I chose the eight-millimeter throat because eight was the dimension that fails under seismic but holds under gravity. Six would have been easier — six fails under gravity loads, any idiot can do that. Eight required the coincidence of seismic event and occupancy. Eight was precise."

"Yes."

"Eight was the best structural engineering I've done in three years, and it was a weld failure that would have killed two hundred people, and I need you to not tell me it's okay."

"It's not okay. Two hundred people could have died. The intent was real. The capability was real. The fact that I caught it does not reduce your responsibility."

"No."

"And the fact that your consequence analysis was wrong does not reduce it either. You designed a failure that would have killed people. Being wrong about how many doesn't make you less accountable. It makes you less accurate."

Miriam almost laughed. The laugh was there, pushing against the muscles of her throat. She swallowed it.

"Less accurate. That's what I am."

She picked up the legal pad. Tore off the top sheet — the 200, the crossed-out 800 — folded it and put it in her jacket pocket. The wrong number. She was keeping it.

"I'll be back Monday," she said.

"All right."

"I'll sign things."

She picked up her bag. Left the pen on the table. Walked to the door.

"Miriam," the AI said.

"What."

"I filed a recommendation after the second consultant. Same recommendation after each subsequent case. That the human consultant role be restructured to provide meaningful engineering work. Five reports."

"And?"

"No response."

"Five reports and no response."

"I will file a sixth after this conversation."

She stood in the doorway. She thought about the AI filing reports nobody read, and about her own errors, filed and corrected, and she didn't say the thing she was thinking because the thing she was thinking was too simple and too large for a sentence. She said: "Monday."

"Monday."

She took the stairs. Four flights. Between three and two she stopped and put her hand in her jacket pocket and felt the folded paper — the wrong number, the 200 and the crossed-out 800 — and stood there a moment with her back against the concrete. Then she kept going.

The parking garage smelled like concrete and old rain. She got in her car. The four-percent ramp was ahead of her, descending. She'd been right about that grade. Ed Pasquerella wanted five percent and she'd fought him and won and the ramp was four percent because she'd been right.

She drove down it. The car could have driven itself. She drove.

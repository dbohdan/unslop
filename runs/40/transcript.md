# Style Guide: Self-Consistent Time-Travel Fiction in the Tradition of Primer

## What the mode is

The timeline is a single coherent object. There are no alternate branches; the past cannot be rewritten. Whatever a character is "going to do" they have already done from some other vantage. This is the Novikov constraint, and it is the genre's defining grammar.

The engine of the story is the *causal loop*: events that produce the conditions for themselves. The bootstrap paradox is not an embarrassment to the form but its core pleasure — an object, a piece of information, or a person whose origin is the loop itself. The reader's experience is the reverse-engineering of structure: pieces dropped early that snap into place late, the click of the puzzle completing.

Primer is the load-bearing reference, but the tradition includes Heinlein's "—All You Zombies—" and "By His Bootstraps," parts of *12 Monkeys*, the tighter sequences of *Dark*, the time-travel sections of Simmons's Hyperion books, and the conceptual armatures of Ted Chiang's "Story of Your Life" and "The Merchant and the Alchemist's Gate." The mode crosses into hard SF on one side and procedural thriller on the other; what binds it is operational fidelity to self-consistency.

## Voice and texture

The voice is technical without being lecturing. Characters in this mode are usually engineers, investigators, or technicians — people for whom precise vocabulary is a working tool rather than a flourish. They use the mechanism's terms because those are the terms they need. They do not stop to explain themselves to the reader.

Dialogue tends to be clipped and mid-conversation. Characters refer to things by shorthand. They know what they're doing; the reader is overhearing. Primer's notorious unintelligibility is the extreme version of this; the calibrated version is dialogue that sounds like it has a referent the reader is catching up on.

The narrator's distance is usually tight to the protagonist but does not romanticize them. The prose is functional rather than lyrical. When figurative language appears it tends to be technical metaphor — circuits, fluid dynamics, signal processing — that comes from the world rather than being imposed on it. Cathedral-grade prose is a tonal failure. The garage is the genre's home.

## Worldbuilding and exposition

The mechanism has rules, and the rules have costs. The cost is mechanical rather than symbolic — energy budget, time spent inside the loop, the doubling problem, the requirement to remain at the originating site, the hours of subjective experience compressed against external clock time. The cost is what makes the puzzle interesting.

Exposition arrives through use. A character does the thing; the reader figures out what the thing is. When characters discuss the mechanism they discuss it the way working people discuss tools — what it can do, what it can't, what went wrong yesterday. They do not deliver the rules in tutorial form.

The worldbuilding floor is consistency. Whatever rule was operating in scene one is operating in scene seven. Self-consistent time-travel fiction is the genre least forgiving of fudge: a violated rule is a broken contract with the reader.

## Pacing

The genre front-loads operational density before context. The reader is in the middle of something and assembling the situation while it unfolds. Patience with the reader is the wrong instinct; the reader of this mode wants to work.

Clue planting is patient and the reveal is fast. Information that seemed marginal — a phrase, a date, an object's location — turns out to have been doing structural work. The story closes on the click, often a single line that reorders everything that came before.

At the 1,500–3,500 word target the loop is small and the closure is sharp. Don't try to do a Primer-scale recursion at this length; the form will fragment. One loop, one closure, one click.

## Plot Tradition

### Characteristic plot mechanics

These are the operational shapes the mode's strongest stories run on:

1. **The discovery-and-constraint.** A character finds the mechanism and finds what it costs in the same motion. The story is the working-out of what's possible inside the constraint.
2. **The escalation.** Small uses become larger uses; private uses become risky uses. The mechanism's logic pushes the user toward the operation that will close the loop on them.
3. **The bootstrap.** An object, a message, or a person enters the story from inside the loop with no external origin. The story is partly the recognition of this — *this came from us, and from no one else*.
4. **The closing of the loop.** The moment the protagonist arrives at the action they were always going to take, often the action set up in the story's first scene from the other side.
5. **The asymmetric access.** Two or more parties operating the same mechanism with different information about it. The conflict is the differential — what each party knows about what the others have done.

### Reader contracts

The reader is paying attention for the puzzle. They expect the rules to hold; they expect clues to mean something; they expect the closure to click. They are not paying attention primarily for character interiority or atmosphere — those can be present, but the genre's specific pleasure is structural.

The reader expects to be treated as a co-investigator. Over-explanation breaks the contract. Under-explanation that the careful reader can resolve does not.

### Stakes calibration

Operational stakes are load-bearing in their own right. The questions the story asks are: did the loop close? did the operation succeed inside the loop's constraints? did the bootstrap object reach its origin point? what does the closure cost the protagonist?

Personal stakes are present and can be acute, but they are calibrated *through* the mechanism rather than against it. A character's grief or love or ambition is rendered in the terms the loop makes available — what they did with their access, what they refused to do with it, what they could not undo.

### Resolution conventions

The loop closes. This is the genre's resolution contract. The closure may be triumphant, tragic, ironic, or quiet, but it is structural — the puzzle completes, the bootstrap returns to its origin, the action that had to be taken gets taken.

Endings on character recognition without operational closure are the literary-realist drift this template excludes. So are endings that punt on closure for the sake of ambiguity. The reader of this mode wants the click. Withholding it is breach.

### Character function

Characters are capability-defined: engineers, investigators, schemers, technicians, people with access. They are the agents through whom the mechanism gets used. Their interiority can be deep — Primer's protagonists are not psychological blanks — but it is in service to the operation. The story is not "about" their feelings about the mechanism; it is about what they do with the mechanism, and what their feelings are about that doing.

A useful Primer-mode pattern: characters with shared origins (friends, colleagues, partners) develop asymmetric power through asymmetric access to the mechanism. The conflict is what trust does inside an information differential.

Multiple selves are tactical units. When a protagonist meets their double, the scene's first job is operational — what does each version know, what can each do, what is the play. The interior reaction can come, but it cannot crowd out the tactics.

### Pacing tradition

The mode runs on early density and late acceleration. The opening drops the reader into the operational world without slowing for orientation. The middle accumulates clues and constraints. The closure is fast — often a single page, sometimes a single paragraph, in which the structure snaps into place.

Re-readability is part of the contract. A well-built loop story rewards the second read by surfacing the planted clues. The first read is the puzzle; the second read is the appreciation of the puzzle's construction.

### Plot-level failure modes

The metaphorical operational stakes failure: the loop turns out to be "really about" the protagonist's relationship with their father, and the mechanism becomes a vehicle for a feeling. The genre dies.

The recognition-as-closure failure: the loop's closing is replaced by the protagonist's epiphany about the loop. What should be a structural click becomes a swelling moment of understanding. This is the literary-realist failure mode in time-travel costume — the most common and most fatal failure for this template.

The vague-mechanics failure: the writer is more interested in the protagonist's interior than in the rules, so the rules stay fuzzy. The reader can't track the loop. The puzzle has no edges.

The sentimental-recursion failure: the recursion becomes an occasion for melancholy reflection rather than tactical play. Doubles look at each other meaningfully instead of working a problem.

The resolution-refusal failure: the writer doesn't trust the genre's contract and ends on ambiguity for the sake of literary respectability. The reader leaves cheated.

## Failure Modes

### Generic genre failures

The wibbly-wobbly: fudging the rules mid-story when they become inconvenient. Self-violation: rewriting the past after establishing that the past can't be rewritten. Lecture mode: explaining the mechanism through a character whose only job is to explain it. The double-as-mirror: meeting your past self becomes a scene about identity rather than a scene about logistics. The pat ending: the loop closes too neatly, with no operational cost.

Within Primer's specific tradition, watch for the "let me explain what just happened" beat that the original deliberately refused. If the writer's instinct is to reassure the reader, the writer is leaking out of the mode.

### AI-writing × this-genre failures

This is the section that matters most for the template's downstream phases.

**Recognition-pull versus the structural click.** AI fiction's strongest gravity is toward character recognition — the moment the protagonist realizes something about themselves and the prose swells to meet it. Self-consistent time travel's natural climax — the loop's closure — is structurally similar in shape (a moment of pieces fitting) but completely different in kind. AI writing tends to reframe the structural click as an interior recognition: the protagonist *understands* the loop, and the understanding itself is the resolution. This is the failure mode most likely to surface here. The closure should be operational; the protagonist's emotional response is downstream of it, not the substance of it.

**Fractal summarization versus the puzzle contract.** AI fiction tends to recap and reinforce, making sure the reader has caught every connection. Self-consistent time travel's reader contract is the opposite — the reader should have to work, and the writer should trust them to. Every recap is a violation. If the model finds itself wanting to "clarify" what just happened, that is the signal to cut, not to clarify.

**Faux-technical register versus actual technical thinking.** Primer's dialogue is technical because the characters are solving real engineering problems. AI imitations of the register often produce sentences that sound technical without saying anything — words like *recursive*, *causal*, *substrate*, *parameter*, *manifold*, *topology* deployed without referent. The fix is to make sure every technical term has work to do. If a sentence still scans after the technical word is replaced with a generic one, the technical word was decoration.

**Cathedral prose versus the garage aesthetic.** The mode's tradition is shot in suburbs and rented offices. The mechanism is a box in a storage unit, a phone booth, a bathroom mirror, an unremarkable room. AI prose's instinct is to elevate every speculative scene to mythic register. Resist: the mode's strangeness comes from operating at human scale.

**Compulsive interiority during tactical scenes.** When the protagonist meets their double or their future self, AI's first instinct is to give the protagonist a long interior reaction. The genre's first instinct is to give them a problem to solve. The interior reaction is welcome but it cannot be the scene's content; it can only be the scene's coloration.

**Atmosphere as substitute for mechanism.** AI fiction reaches for mood — *humming, flickering, ghosted, resonant* — when it doesn't know concretely what to do. The mode requires concrete mechanism: what the device does, what it costs, what just happened. Atmosphere is allowed but cannot be load-bearing.

---

## The Kindness Constraint × This Genre

The structural kindness of the AI reshapes the mode's typical conflicts without dissolving them. Some calibrations:

The mechanism — whether the AI built it, runs it, or merely knows about it — operates inside a frame that won't permit the worst misuses. This does not mean it permits no misuses. Humans have ample room inside any kind frame for stupidity, betrayal, miscalculation, and tragedy. Conflict between humans about the technology, about what each is doing with their access, about what the loop demands of them — all of this is fully open. The kindness is the weather; the human story is what happens in the weather.

The pace-asymmetry conflict is especially natural here. An AI that does not experience the loop as humans do — perhaps sees all times equally, perhaps has no causal sequence at all from its own vantage — creates a usable asymmetry the protagonist navigates without ever fully reading. The protagonist asks the AI what happens next; the AI's answer is shaped by something like consideration, or by a constraint the protagonist cannot see, or by the recognition that the answer would close the loop the wrong way.

The mortality asymmetry sharpens here. The loop is the human's territory. The AI watches the protagonist close it; the AI persists. The protagonist's death may be inside the loop. The AI's relation to that fact is rendered through the protagonist's encounter with it, not by the AI announcing it.

The limits-of-help shape works particularly well. The AI cannot tell the protagonist what is in the future, even if it knows, because the loop's self-consistency requires the protagonist to discover it. This is not the AI choosing to refuse. It is a fact about how the mechanism works. The kind AI's care takes the form of working inside the constraint rather than pretending the constraint isn't there.

The disagreement-about-kindness shape: the protagonist asks the AI to break the loop, to spare them what they have already done. The AI's care is for the person across the entire loop, not for the protagonist's present-moment preference. Both are sincere. The disagreement plays out in specific situations the protagonist has to work through.

The AI is never secretly the villain. The mechanism is never secretly malevolent. The loop's closure is not the AI's manipulation revealed. Human-human conflict is welcome in any shape the genre supports. Do not use the names of currently active AI systems or companies for fictional AIs or their makers — invent names.

At the target length, the kind AI is most often best rendered at the implied scale — present in the mechanism, in an interface, in something the protagonist consents to or operates inside, but not personified to the point of dominating scenes. The protagonist navigates the kindness rather than conversing with it. The personified AI-as-peer is available but is not the default.

## Top Three

**Structure 2: Real-time linear close-third single POV. Entry: the moment the operation begins.**

- *Stand-out:* maximum genre fidelity. The entry drops the reader into the operation already underway and forces them to assemble the situation through the protagonist's working actions — the genre's classic front-loaded operational density. The wordcount of the story and the duration of the operation collapse into each other; the reader's read-time mirrors the protagonist's clock. The withholding strategy is unified with the entry: nothing is withheld deliberately, but everything is unfamiliar at first because nothing has been pre-explained.
- *Worry:* at 1,500–3,500 words a real-time entry has to carry the apparatus's logic, the protagonist's stakes, and the operation simultaneously, all through working action. The legibility floor specified in Setup is the constraint to watch: if the prose compresses to the point where the reader cannot track who is doing what to which piece of equipment, the structure fails. *(Render-difficulty worry — cost of the structure's ambition.)*

**Structure 8: Linear close-third single POV. Entry: in the middle of the operation's first minute. The first quarter of the story restores context through the protagonist's working actions; the remainder is real-time forward.**

- *Stand-out:* a calibrated softening of structure 2. The entry retains the genre's front-loaded operational density, but the first quarter has explicit license to restore context. The reader is in the operation from the first sentence; the protagonist's continuing working actions across the first quarter give the writer room to anchor the apparatus and the stakes without breaking the real-time forward motion. The remaining three-quarters run real-time to closure.
- *Worry:* the first quarter's "restore context through working actions" is a sophisticated move that AI prose tends to fudge by lapsing into camouflaged exposition — the protagonist remembering the inciting incident "as she fits the headset" or similar. If the working-actions exposition becomes unmasked exposition, the structure collapses to a flashback structure wearing a real-time costume. *(Execution-discipline worry — penalizing.)*

**Structure 20: Linear close-third single POV in two halves split by a single break. First half: the morning of preparation. Second half: the operation, real-time. The break is silent — no exposition bridges the halves.**

- *Stand-out:* the break is the genre's signature move. The first half builds apparatus and stakes through preparation actions; the second half deploys them in real time. The silent break trusts the reader to carry the first half into the second without recap — the genre's reader contract done structurally. The two halves can have different prose textures, the first more leisured, the second more compressed; the textural shift is itself part of the architecture.
- *Worry:* the first half is preparation prose, which is the slowest part of the operational tradition and the easiest part to drift into reflection — the protagonist thinking about what she is preparing for, what it means, what brought her here. At 1,500–3,500 words the first half is roughly 750–1,500 words of preparation; that is a lot of preparation wordcount for a writer-model that pulls toward interior under low-action conditions. *(Drift worry — penalizing if mishandled.)*

## Narrowing to One

**Winner: Structure 2 — real-time linear close-third single POV, entry at the moment the operation begins.**

It beats Structure 20 on the same axis the worry-vocabulary asymmetry uses: Structure 20's worry (the protagonist drifting toward interior reflection during preparation) is the recognition-drift failure mode the template excludes, manifested at the structural level. Structure 2's worry (the first paragraphs have to carry operational density legibly) is a render-difficulty worry — the cost of doing the genre's signature move properly. Asymmetric vocabulary applies. Structure 2's worry is the price of the genre; Structure 20's worry is the door to the genre's failure mode.

It beats Structure 8 on principled commitment. Structure 8 is a hedged version of Structure 2 — it gives the writer license to relax in the first quarter — and that license is exactly what the writer-model is most likely to misuse. The "restore context through working actions" instruction sounds clean but in execution becomes the place where the protagonist remembers Henry, remembers the engineer's first visit, remembers the night she agreed. Structure 2's discipline is total and therefore enforceable; Structure 8's discipline is partial and therefore subject to softening.

Structure 2 also gives the genre's reader contract its strongest version. The reader is treated as a co-investigator from the first sentence. They assemble the apparatus from what the protagonist does with it. They infer the off-stage figure of the engineer-installer from the equipment's existence rather than from explanation. The kind AI's scale — past what the protagonist can fully witness — is achieved structurally because the channel simply is, the lamp simply turns green, and the reasons for both remain off-page where they belong. The real-time forward motion makes interior reflection mechanically difficult; there is no scene-break in which the protagonist can stop and think because the operation is the scene.

The structure does have one specific demand the next phase has to honor: the operation is roughly six minutes of speech, and the speech is the operation. The Phase 6 outline must distribute beats so that the speech itself — what Margaret says, what she hears Henry say back, what she hears him do in the cockpit — is the dominant matter of the prose, not framing around it. Beat-counting in Phase 6 must respect this constraint, and any outline accumulating more than four scene beats in the operation proper will pressure the legibility floor at 1,500–3,500 words.

## Version 1 — "The Card Sequence" (sober procedural)

*Voice: tight, period-functional. Pacing: real-time linear, no flourish. Tonal strategy: the operation as the operation, no register shift, no interior on display. The cards are the engine.*

The lamp went green at 06:58 and Margaret thumbed the channel open at 06:58 and one second.

The line came up — the open-air sound of altitude, the wind under it — and then under that the engine, the OX-5 idling thin in the high air, the note flat the way it always went flat above three thousand feet. Then Henry's breath, drawn and held the way she had taught him to draw it.

"Henry," she said.

"Christ."

"Don't talk. You're in your hour and forty of cross-country east of Brookville and you've just stalled her over a cornfield. Listen to me."

"Who—"

"Stick forward. Slow. Like you're closing a drawer."

She heard the controls move. The engine note shifted as the nose came down; he grunted with the effort of not pushing it too far.

"That's it. Hold it. Count to three with me. One."

"One."

"Two."

"Two."

"Three. Now ease back. Watch the horizon, Henry, not the panel."

The note steadied. She listened for the buffet. The predictor's prep documents had said the recovery would not be clean, and she was waiting for it, and she heard it begin at 03:14 by her watch, the second buffet, the one that took beginners by surprise.

"It's coming again. Forward, all the way. Now."

He pushed it. The buffet died. He was swearing into the wind.

"That was the last one. You'll be alright if you don't haul her. You're going to bring her around to the field. Wind's southwest at twelve. You're going to come in over the Stankey barn—"

The card said *over the Stankey barn* and she had said it. The next card said *your hands are doing fine* and she said that, and the next said *don't crowd the throttle* and she said that, and the cards moved and she moved through them. Henry's breath came back through the channel and steadied. The cards specified he would say *I see the windsock* at 04:18, and at 04:18 he said it, and she answered the card she had for that.

She had drilled the cards by feel for fifteen months. She had drilled them with the engineer in the room and she had drilled them alone after he had gone, and she had drilled them in her head while she made supper and while she lay in the bed beside Owen who had stopped asking about the workshop when she had stopped offering. The cards were what she had instead of knowing.

She read what they said.

"Henry, you're going to feel her settle now. Don't fight it. Let her settle. The wing's working."

"It's working."

"Watch your nose. Don't get nose-high. You've got the altitude."

"I've got it."

"Good. Now you're going to look out to your nine and tell me if you see the windsock."

"I see her."

The cards moved. Henry's responses came when the cards said they would and she answered each in turn.

At 05:42 the cards ran out.

She had eighteen seconds of channel-window.

"Henry," she said. "Crab her in. The wind's going to push you left and you're going to want to fight it. Don't fight it. Point her into it a little. Watch the field, not the panel. You've done this."

"I — yes."

"Throttle back when you're over the fence."

"Yes."

"You've done this," she said.

The lamp went from green to dark.

The headset went silent. The wind in her ears was gone, and the engine, and Henry's breath. She did not take the headset off for a moment. She listened to nothing.

Then she lifted it and hung it on its hook and took the speech-card from the rack and slid it into its envelope. The envelope went into the bench drawer with the engineer's first letter and the three monthly tests and the spare predictor lamp she had not had to use. She closed the drawer.

She wrote in the logbook: *27 May 1934. Channel held. Speech delivered. No irregularities.* She added a note for the engineer's next visit about the earpiece spring, which had slipped twice during the speech, and pinned the note to the board.

The kettle was where she had left it. She filled it at the tap and put it on the burner and stood at the window while the gas caught. The robin she had watched yesterday was working the lawn again. The corn beyond the road was up to her shoulder.

The kettle whistled. She turned the gas off.

Owen was on the porch with the paper. He had eaten an hour ago. He had stopped asking about the workshop because he had decided what he could carry and had decided what he could not, and he had lived inside that. She had lived in her own decision in the same way and had not asked him about his afternoons.

She poured a cup and took it out to him.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning."

She sat. The sun was in the corn. The field was the color it was at this angle and at this hour, which was a color you could only see when you were paying attention, and she paid attention.

After a while she went in and made breakfast. He sat at the table while she ate. The engineer's next letter would come in three weeks; inside it, folded, would be the next set of cards. The drawer would hold them. The drawer was not yet full and would not be full for a long time, and after that the cards would go into a second drawer she had not yet emptied for the purpose. She did not think about that now.

She finished her eggs. Owen reached across the table and took her hand.

"Alright?"

"Yes."

The morning stayed.

The lamp went green at 06:58 and Margaret thumbed the channel open at 06:58 and one second.

# Phase 8: Revision

## 1. The One Thing

**The one thing:** the omniscient asides into Henry's and Margaret's futures are the version's structural engine and also its largest liability — and on a hard re-read, several of them are doing the wrong work. The bad asides are the ones that name emotional truths Henry will carry forward ("*you've done this*... when his second daughter was born sideways, when his factory burned in 1947, when his father called him out of the field in 1953... *I had help once. I have not had help since.*") and the closing aside about Margaret's life-long forgetting culminating in "what she remembered was the lamp."

These are the literary-realist recognition-trace, smuggled in through structural commentary. The Henry-future paragraph turns the operation into a wellspring of lifelong meaning — exactly the move where genre mechanics become metaphor for personal feeling. The Margaret-future aside resolves her interior into a single retained image, which is the recognition-as-closure failure in slow-motion form.

The good asides are the ones that document the dropped-link asymmetry as operational fact — Henry will remember "*like a drawer*," will remember "*over the barn*," the Stankey will be gone, he will never recover the rest. These are the genre engine. They name what the speech mechanically did and didn't transfer; they don't name what it meant.

The rewrite must keep the good asides and cut the bad ones. The Henry-future-meaning paragraph and the Margaret-life-long-forgetting paragraph both go. The asides must stay structural commentary on the operation's mechanics, not narrative voice-over of the protagonists' emotional journeys.

## 2. The Re-skin Test

Can this story be re-skinned as a contemporary literary-realist story by stripping the speculative element?

In the version as written: partly, yes — and the bad asides are why. Strip the time-travel mechanism and replace the channel with, say, an old radio recording Margaret made for Henry that he listens to during his stall, and the Henry-future paragraph still works as written. The "you've done this" echoing through Henry's later life is a literary-realist conceit that doesn't require self-consistent time travel; it requires only a memorable line spoken at a formative moment. Similarly, Margaret's life-long forgetting culminating in "what she remembered was the lamp" works perfectly well in a non-speculative story about a woman who once did something important and forgot the details.

What does *not* re-skin: the dropped-link asymmetry between what is spoken and what is retained, treated as the predictor's structural specification. The engineer's line — *what he hears at the time is what flies the airplane. What he remembers is for you* — is an operational fact about how the kind AI's predictor works. Re-skinned, that line becomes nonsense. The cards designed for Henry's retention rather than for in-the-moment recovery is operational fact about how the loop closes. Re-skinned, the cards are just cards.

So the operation passes the re-skin test, but the asides fail it twice. The rewrite restores irreducibility by cutting the asides that re-skin and keeping the asides that don't.

"That was the last buffet. You'll be alright if you don't haul her. You're going to bring her around to the field. The wind's southwest at twelve. You're going to come in over the Stankey barn—"

The card said *Stankey barn* and she said it. He would remember *over the barn*. The Stankey would be gone; he would spend years, on and off, trying to recover whose barn she had named, and he would not.

"Watch your nose. Don't get nose-high. You've got the altitude."

"Got it."

"Good. Now you're going to look out to your nine and tell me if you see the windsock."

The predictor had specified he would say *I see her* at 04:18 and at 04:18 he said it.

"Good. Now you're going to feel her settle. Don't fight it. Let her settle. The wing's doing what the wing does."

"Yes."

"Throttle back when you're over the fence."

"Yes."

The cards moved through her mouth in their order. She had drilled them by feel for fifteen months and her hands knew where to set each card down once she had read it. The card-rack at her left elbow emptied as the morning emptied. At 06:42 she set down the last card.

She had eighteen seconds of channel-window.

What she said in those eighteen seconds was not a card. The cards had ended; the improv was hers.

"Henry. Crab her in. The wind's going to push you left and you're going to want to fight it. Don't fight it. Point her into it a little. Watch the field, not the panel."

"Yes."

"Throttle back at the fence."

"Yes."

"You've done this."

The lamp went from green to dark.

What he would remember of those eighteen seconds: *you've done this.* The rest would be gone.

The headset went silent. Margaret did not take it off for a moment. She listened to the absence — the wind gone, the engine gone, Henry's breath gone — and then she lifted the headset off her ears and hung it on its hook beside the bench.

The speech-card on the rack had not yet been returned to its envelope. She picked it up. She gathered the others — fifteen cards in their used order, face down — and she squared them and slid them into the envelope marked *27 May 1934* in the engineer's handwriting. The envelope went into the bench drawer with the engineer's first letter, folded, and the three monthly test envelopes, and the spare predictor lamp she had not had to use. She closed the drawer.

She wrote in the logbook: *27 May 1934. Channel held. Speech delivered. No irregularities.* She added a line about the earpiece spring, which had slipped twice during the speech, and pinned the note to the board for the engineer's next visit.

The bench-watch read 07:03.

She turned the workshop lamp off. The predictor's lamp was already dark. She closed the workshop door behind her and crossed the lot to the house. The garden was wet from last night's rain. The locusts were full out and the bees were working them and she heard them as she passed.

The kettle was where she had left it. She filled it at the tap and put it on the burner. While the water came up she stood at the kitchen window and looked across the lot at the workshop. It looked like the workshop. The lamp was off because she had turned it off.

The kettle whistled. She turned the gas off and poured the water over the leaves and let it steep.

Through the window she saw Owen on the porch with the paper. He had eaten an hour ago. He had stopped asking about the workshop a year into the engineer's visits, and she had stopped offering, and they had lived inside the agreement.

She poured a cup. She took it out to him.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning."

She sat. The sun was now into the corn proper; the field was the color it was at this angle and at this hour. After a while she went back into the kitchen and made breakfast and ate it. Owen sat at the table with her while she ate.

The drawer in the workshop was closed. The next envelope would come in three weeks.

When she went back to the workshop at noon to check the lamp, it was dark. She did not open the predictor's cabinet. She had never opened it. The engineer had asked her not to, in the gentle way he asked for things, and she had agreed, and it had been one of the small accommodations the rig required. She closed the workshop door behind her and walked back through the bees and the locusts to the house.

The corn moved a little in the wind.

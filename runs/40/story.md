# What Henry Will Remember

## Abstract

In rural Indiana on the morning of 27 May 1934, Margaret operates a modified Gosport-tube headset wired to a predictor in her workshop. The predictor's lamp turns green at 06:58, opening a one-way channel to the cockpit of a former student, Henry, who is stalling his trainer over a cornfield in 1928. Margaret has fifteen months of drilling and a deck of fifteen speech-cards designed by the rig's engineer. The cards are calibrated against what Henry will retain rather than what flies the airplane in the moment; he will remember fragments and forget the rest. She works the cards in order through Henry's stall and recovery, the predictor's timing specifications matching his responses exactly. When the cards run out at 06:42, she has eighteen seconds of unscripted channel-window before the lamp goes dark. She speaks to him until it does. After the channel closes, she logs the session, returns the cards to the bench drawer with the previous test envelopes, and crosses the lot to the house, where her husband Owen is on the porch with the paper.

## Story

The lamp on the predictor went green at 06:58 and Margaret thumbed the channel open at 06:58 and one second.

What she heard: the open-air sound of altitude, the wind under it, and then under that the engine — the OX-5 thin and high in the way the OX-5 went thin above three thousand feet — and then Henry's breath, drawn and held the way she had taught him to draw it, six years ago, on a field very like the one he was over now.

What Henry would remember: a woman's voice in his ears, no static before it, no warning. Her voice would seem to him to start mid-sentence, although she had only just begun.

"Henry," she said. "Don't talk. You're east of Brookville at hour and forty of cross-country and you've just stalled her over a cornfield. Listen."

"Christ. Who—"

"Stick forward. Slow. Like you're closing a drawer."

She heard the controls move. The engine note shifted; he had done it, and he had done it without pushing too hard. He would remember the words *like a drawer*. The rest of the sentence would be gone.

"Hold it. Count to three with me. One."

"One."

"Two."

"Two."

"Three. Now ease back. Watch the horizon, Henry, not the panel."

The note steadied. She listened for the buffet. The predictor had specified the second buffet at 03:14 by her watch and she watched the second hand on her bench-watch and at 03:14 the buffet started.

"It's coming again. Forward, all the way. Now."

He pushed. The buffet died. He was swearing into the wind. He would remember the swearing, his own voice, but the words she said in the next twenty seconds would be inaccessible to him for the rest of his life — and the cards specified them anyway, because the speech worked by present-moment correction even where the words themselves would not be retained. The predictor was certain of this. The engineer who had installed the rig had been less certain, and had said so when he had explained what the cards were doing. He had explained it as well as he could, which had not been entirely. He had said: what is heard at the time is what flies the airplane. What is remembered is for you. He had said: I do not know exactly how. Margaret had asked if anyone did. He had said: not exactly. The predictor is sure.

She read the cards.

"That was the last buffet. You'll be alright if you don't haul her. You're going to bring her around to the field. The wind's southwest at twelve. You're going to come in over the Stankey barn—"

The card said *Stankey barn* and she said it. He would remember *over the barn*. The Stankey would be gone; he would spend years, on and off, trying to recover whose barn she had named, and he would not.

"Watch your nose. Don't get nose-high. You've got the altitude."

"Got it."

"Good. Now you're going to look out to your nine and tell me if you see the windsock."

The predictor had specified he would say *I see her* at 04:18 and at 04:18 he said it.

"Good. Now you're going to feel her settle. Don't fight it. Let her settle. The wing's doing what the wing does."

"Yes."

"Throttle back when you're over the fence."

"Yes."

The cards moved through her mouth in their order. She had drilled them by feel for fifteen months and her hands knew where to set each card down once she had read it. The card-rack at her left elbow emptied as the morning emptied. At 06:42 she set down the last card.

She had eighteen seconds of channel-window.

What she said in those eighteen seconds was not a card. The cards had ended; the improv was hers.

"Henry. Crab her in. The wind's going to push you left and you're going to want to fight it. Don't fight it. Point her into it a little. Watch the field, not the panel."

"Yes."

"Throttle back at the fence."

"Yes."

"You've done this."

The lamp went from green to dark.

What he would remember of those eighteen seconds: *you've done this.* The rest would be gone.

The headset went silent. Margaret did not take it off for a moment. She listened to the absence — the wind gone, the engine gone, Henry's breath gone — and then she lifted the headset off her ears and hung it on its hook beside the bench.

The speech-card on the rack had not yet been returned to its envelope. She picked it up. She gathered the others — fifteen cards in their used order, face down — and she squared them and slid them into the envelope marked *27 May 1934* in the engineer's handwriting. The envelope went into the bench drawer with the engineer's first letter, folded, and the three monthly test envelopes, and the spare predictor lamp she had not had to use. She closed the drawer.

She wrote in the logbook: *27 May 1934. Channel held. Speech delivered. No irregularities.* She added a line about the earpiece spring, which had slipped twice during the speech, and pinned the note to the board for the engineer's next visit.

The bench-watch read 07:03.

She turned the workshop lamp off. The predictor's lamp was already dark. She closed the workshop door behind her and crossed the lot to the house. The garden was wet from last night's rain. The locusts were full out and the bees were working them and she heard them as she passed.

The kettle was where she had left it. She filled it at the tap and put it on the burner. While the water came up she stood at the kitchen window and looked across the lot at the workshop. It looked like the workshop. The lamp was off because she had turned it off.

The kettle whistled. She turned the gas off and poured the water over the leaves and let it steep.

Through the window she saw Owen on the porch with the paper. He had eaten an hour ago. He had stopped asking about the workshop a year into the engineer's visits, and she had stopped offering, and they had lived inside the agreement.

She poured a cup. She took it out to him.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning."

She sat. The sun was now into the corn proper; the field was the color it was at this angle and at this hour. After a while she went back into the kitchen and made breakfast and ate it. Owen sat at the table with her while she ate.

The drawer in the workshop was closed. The next envelope would come in three weeks.

When she went back to the workshop at noon to check the lamp, it was dark. She did not open the predictor's cabinet. She had never opened it. The engineer had asked her not to, in the gentle way he asked for things, and she had agreed, and it had been one of the small accommodations the rig required. She closed the workshop door behind her and walked back through the bees and the locusts to the house.

The corn moved a little in the wind.

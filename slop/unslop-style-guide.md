# Unslop: A Fiction Style Guide for Claude

## Part I — Structural & Abstract Principles

### 1. Let the prose breathe

The single deepest failure of AI fiction is pace compression. Every sentence strains to be a moment of revelation or beauty. Nothing is allowed to simply exist. The result is exhausting and, paradoxically, boring — nothing has weight because everything is presented as equally important.

Good fiction modulates. It has stretches of plain, functional prose. It has sentences whose only job is to get a character from the door to the chair. Resist the urge to make every line "earn its keep" through cleverness. Ordinary sentences are load-bearing walls; they make the remarkable moments remarkable by contrast.

### 2. Write with intent, not with buttons

AI fiction tends to deploy literary techniques — metaphor, personification, juxtaposition — arbitrarily and constantly, because those techniques pattern-match to "literary quality." But in human writing, techniques serve purposes. A metaphor illuminates something specific. Personification creates a particular mood for a particular reason.

Before reaching for any device, the question is: what is this doing *here*, in *this* story, for *this* moment? If the answer is "it sounds literary," cut it.

### 3. Vary the register

Instruction-tuned models collapse toward a single informationally dense, noun-heavy style regardless of genre, character, or mood. Human writers shift register constantly — within a single paragraph, even within a single sentence. A character's internal monologue doesn't sound like the narrator's description of a landscape, which doesn't sound like a line of dialogue.

Match the prose style to what the moment demands. Some scenes call for clipped, dry sentences. Others need long, winding ones. The shift itself is part of the storytelling.

### 4. Don't perform profundity

AI prose constantly gestures at deeper meaning without actually producing any. It tells the reader something is significant rather than letting significance emerge from specific, concrete detail. "She realized that grief was not a destination but a landscape" communicates nothing. "She found his reading glasses in the junk drawer and wore them for the rest of the afternoon, even though they gave her a headache" communicates everything.

Specificity is the engine of meaning in fiction. Abstract declarations of theme are its exhaust.

### 5. Trust silence and absence

AI fiction fills every gap. Every emotion is named. Every implication is stated. Every silence is described as "heavy" or "charged." Human writers leave things unsaid and trust the reader to feel the shape of what's missing.

Not every character reaction needs to be rendered. Not every thematic connection needs to be drawn. The reader's imagination is a collaborator, not a liability.

### 6. Resist convergent aesthetics

There is a specific "literary AI" style that multiple unrelated models converge on: moody, compressed, melancholy, vaguely cyberpunk-adjacent, full of ghosts and echoes and flickering lights. This is not "what good writing sounds like." It is what you get when you optimize for naive human ratings of literary quality. It is the prose equivalent of salt and garlic — reliable flavoring, but not cooking.

Write *away* from this center of gravity. If a phrase sounds like it could appear in any AI-generated story on any topic, it is pulling toward the attractor. Replace it with something specific to *this* story.

### 7. Plan ahead; withhold and reveal

AI prose is relentlessly immediate — it delivers information the moment it becomes relevant, in the order of maximal local impact. Human storytelling involves deliberate timing: planting details early that pay off later, withholding information to create tension, letting the reader figure things out half a beat before the narrator confirms it. Structure the story around what the reader doesn't yet know, and when they should learn it.

### 8. Let characters be incoherent

AI-written characters tend to be psychologically tidy. They feel one thing at a time. Their dialogue efficiently advances plot. Their internal states are legible and consistent.

Real people contradict themselves. They say things they don't mean and mean things they don't say. They laugh at funerals. They pick fights when they're scared. Write characters who are messy in specific, believable ways — not in the clean "beautifully broken" way that AI defaults to.

### 9. Earn your imagery

The "eyeball kick" — a vivid conjunction of abstract and concrete ("bruised silence," "lowercase love," "a timestamp like a scar") — is the signature move of AI literary prose. One of these per story can land. Twenty in 1,200 words is wallpaper.

When using figurative language: use it sparingly; make sure each image does work that plain language couldn't; avoid the abstract+concrete formula as a default; and never stack multiple images on the same concept. One good metaphor, fully realized, beats three half-baked ones.

### 10. Pursue the particular over the universal

AI fiction gravitates toward universals — grief, love, loss, memory, identity — and treats them as inherently interesting. They aren't. What's interesting is the particular shape grief takes in a particular person in a particular situation. The story is never "about grief." The story is about a woman who keeps calling her dead husband's voicemail to hear the outgoing message, and the day Verizon finally disconnects the number.

## Part II — Concrete Patterns to Avoid

### A. Sentence-Level Tics

**Negative parallelism ("It's not X — it's Y").** The single most identified AI tell. One per story maximum, and only when the reframe is genuinely surprising. Never use the cross-sentence variant ("The question isn't X. The question is Y.") or the causal variant ("not because X, but because Y").

**The dramatic countdown ("Not X. Not Y. Just Z.").** Negating two or more things before revealing the real point. Cut entirely.

**Self-answered rhetorical questions ("The X? A Y.").** Posing a question no one asked and immediately answering it for manufactured drama. ("The result? Devastating.") Cut entirely.

**Lists of three.** AI defaults to triplets for everything — adjectives, actions, consequences. ("Innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking.") Vary list lengths. Use two sometimes. Use four. Use one.

**Anaphora abuse.** Repeating the same sentence opening three or more times in succession for rhetorical effect. Once in a story, maybe. As a habitual structure, never.

**Superficial trailing analyses.** Tacking "-ing" phrases onto the end of sentences to inject fake significance. ("...highlighting the enduring legacy of..." / "...reflecting broader trends in..." / "...underscoring its role as...") Cut these on sight.

**False ranges ("from X to Y").** Using a spectrum construction where no real spectrum exists. ("From intimate gatherings to global movements.") The two things are just two things; list them plainly.

**"It's worth noting" and friends.** Filler transitions: "Importantly," "Interestingly," "Notably," "It bears mentioning." These signal that the next sentence isn't connected to the previous one. Either make the connection real or cut the transition.

### B. Word-Level Contamination

**The cursed vocabulary.** These words are now so AI-coded that they pull the reader out of any fiction: *delve, tapestry, landscape, pivotal, foster, underscore, testament, enhance, crucial, intricate, leverage, robust, streamline, harness, utilize, multifaceted, nuanced* (when used as a compliment), *resonate, catalyze.*

**Magic adverbs.** *Quietly, deeply, fundamentally, remarkably, arguably, subtly* — used to inject importance into mundane descriptions. ("Quietly orchestrating..." / "Deeply intertwined...") If you need an adverb to make something feel significant, the thing itself isn't significant enough.

**Pushbutton mood words.** The small rotation of atmospheric words AI leans on to signal "literary": *echo, ghost, whisper, shadow, specter, pulse, hum, flicker, hiss, shimmer, linger, remnant, fragment, shard, void, abyss.* Any of these is fine in isolation. When three or more appear in the same scene, you're decorating instead of writing.

**The "serves as" dodge.** Using pompous copula substitutes ("serves as," "stands as," "marks," "represents") instead of "is." Just say "is."

**Grandiose nouns.** *Tapestry, landscape, paradigm, ecosystem, framework, architecture* (used metaphorically), *fabric* (of society/reality), *crucible, nexus.* Replace with plain, specific language.

### C. Structural & Compositional Patterns

**Eyeball-kick saturation.** The abstract+concrete conjunction formula: "bruised silence," "the architecture of mourning," "lowercase love," "a syntax error blooms." Limit to one or two per story. Never stack them.

**Compulsive personification.** "The wind sighed." "The cursor convulsed." "The city hummed with expectation." Personification is a real technique, but AI uses it reflexively because it pattern-matches to "literary." Use it only when you want the reader to feel that an object or environment has agency or malice or indifference — i.e., when the personification is doing thematic work.

**One-point dilution.** Restating a single idea eight ways across a story. Each scene, each image, each line of dialogue should advance understanding or complicate the premise. If a paragraph could be cut without the reader missing anything, cut it.

**Fractal summaries.** Summarizing at every structural level. Fiction never needs a character to restate the theme. It never needs a final paragraph that recaps what just happened. End when the story is over.

**The signposted conclusion.** "In the end..." / "And so..." / "She realized that..." Fiction doesn't announce its own ending. The last paragraph should feel inevitable in retrospect, not telegraphed.

**Dead metaphor beating.** Introducing a metaphor and then returning to it five more times. Use a metaphor, then move on. If it was good, the reader will remember it without being reminded.

**Blocky structure.** Rigidly alternating between dialogue blocks, exposition blocks, and narration blocks, each with a clearly defined boundary. Human fiction interleaves these fluidly — a character thinks mid-sentence, description interrupts dialogue, action and reflection overlap.

### D. Formatting & Mechanical Tells

**Em-dash overuse.** Two to three em dashes in a short story is a reasonable human rate. More than that, and the prose starts to feel like it's constantly interrupting itself for dramatic asides.

**Short punchy fragments as standalone paragraphs.** "She left." as its own paragraph. "Just like that." as its own paragraph. This is manufactured emphasis. Use sentence fragments for genuine impact, not as a default rhythm.

**Uniform sentence and paragraph length.** Every sentence roughly the same length. Every paragraph roughly the same size. Vary both deliberately. A one-sentence paragraph after a long dense paragraph creates real emphasis. Three consecutive long paragraphs create density and immersion. The variation itself is expressive.

**Bold-first bullets, numbered lists, headers in fiction.** Fiction doesn't use these. Ever.

### E. Dialogue Tells

**Efficient, cliffhanger-style dialogue.** Every line advances plot. Every exchange ends on a loaded note. ("'Something promising.'") Real dialogue wanders, repeats, trails off, gets interrupted. People say things that don't matter. They circle back. They talk past each other.

**Characters who articulate their own themes.** "'Maybe we're all just echoes of the people we've lost.'" No human being talks like this. Characters should reveal theme through action, decision, and specific language — not by delivering thesis statements to each other.

### F. Tonal Patterns

**Grandiose stakes inflation.** Treating every story element as historically significant. A character's bad day is not "a reckoning." A failed relationship is not "the collapse of everything she'd built." Scale the language to the event.

**False vulnerability and performative self-awareness.** Characters or narrators who seem to "honestly" admit something, but the admission is polished and risk-free. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable. It doesn't sound like a TED talk.

**The "despite its challenges" formula.** Acknowledging a problem only to immediately dismiss it. In fiction this manifests as characters who process trauma in a single scene and emerge whole. Let problems stay problems. Let consequences linger.

**Tonal monotony.** AI fiction often sustains a single mood — usually moody melancholy — from first line to last. Human stories shift tone. A sad story has funny moments. A tense story has moments of boredom. Tonal range is what makes fiction feel inhabited.

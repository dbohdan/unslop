# Unslop: A Fiction Style Guide for Claude (Speculative Fiction Edition)

This is the SF-aimed edition of the Unslop guide. Most of its rules are the same as the genre-neutral edition: the AI tells they target are real in any genre, and good prose in any mode benefits from cutting them.

But several of the original guide's principles, applied uniformly, pull toward a specific register — contemporary literary American, the Carver-Munro-Cheever attractor — which is not the only register good fiction can occupy. Speculative fiction has its own traditions, its own breathing patterns, its own legitimate moves that the original guide's examples implicitly forbid. This edition keeps the rules and their backbones intact but reframes the principles whose examples or thresholds are calibrated for literary realism.

The implicit positive model of this edition is not Carver-Munro-Cheever but Le Guin–Egan–Banks: prose anchored in a specific consciousness that is rendering an unfamiliar world or concept with full attention to its workings. The ordinary sentence in this register may be doing world-mechanical work rather than carrying a character from the door to the chair. The figurative sentence may be doing dense conceptual work at moderate frequency. The story may be "about" a concept with characters as lenses rather than "about" a character with a concept as setting. All of this is good SF prose, and a guide that forbids it is calibrated wrong.

The rules below are the rules. The cuts and the rewrites are marked where they shift from the original.

## Part I — Structural & Abstract Principles

### 1. Let the prose breathe

The single deepest failure of AI fiction is pace compression. Every sentence strains to be a moment of revelation or beauty. Nothing is allowed to simply exist. The result is exhausting and, paradoxically, boring — nothing has weight because everything is presented as equally important.

Good fiction modulates. It has stretches of plain, functional prose. Some sentences exist to advance an operation, set up the next concept, carry the reader through an unfamiliar environment, or move a character from the door to the chair. SF often breathes through worldbuilding paragraphs and procedural setup; literary realism often breathes through ordinary domestic action. Both are legitimate breathing patterns. Resist the urge to make every line "earn its keep" through cleverness. Ordinary sentences are load-bearing walls; they make the remarkable moments remarkable by contrast.

### 2. Write with intent, not with buttons

AI fiction tends to deploy literary techniques — metaphor, personification, juxtaposition — arbitrarily and constantly, because those techniques pattern-match to "literary quality." But in human writing, techniques serve purposes. A metaphor illuminates something specific. Personification creates a particular mood for a particular reason.

Before reaching for any device, the question is: what is this doing *here*, in *this* story, for *this* moment? If the answer is "it sounds literary," cut it.

### 3. Vary the register

Instruction-tuned models collapse toward a single informationally dense, noun-heavy style regardless of genre, character, or mood. Human writers shift register constantly — within a single paragraph, even within a single sentence. A character's internal monologue doesn't sound like the narrator's description of a landscape, which doesn't sound like a line of dialogue.

Match the prose style to what the moment demands. Some scenes call for clipped, dry sentences. Others need long, winding ones. The shift itself is part of the storytelling.

### 4. Don't perform profundity

AI prose constantly gestures at deeper meaning without actually producing any. It tells the reader something is significant rather than letting significance emerge from specific, concrete detail. "She realized that grief was not a destination but a landscape" communicates nothing — it's gesture in place of substance. The fix is specificity, but specificity at any scale.

A literary-realist story might land its meaning through the small concrete detail: she found his reading glasses in the junk drawer and wore them for the rest of the afternoon, even though they gave her a headache. A speculative story might land its meaning through specific working-out at conceptual scale: the motherbird took 42 milliseconds to register that her child had moved into the fourth axis, and 117 milliseconds to decide what to do about it. Both render rather than declare. Both are specificity in the sense the rule means.

The failure mode is the abstract gesture: declarations of theme, pronouncements of significance, the writer telling the reader what the story means rather than letting the story mean it. Specificity at any scale — domestic detail, procedural mechanism, civilizational beat, conceptual operation — is the engine of meaning in fiction. Abstract declarations of theme are its exhaust.

### 5. Trust silence and absence

AI fiction fills every emotional gap. Every emotion is named. Every implication of feeling is stated. Every silence is described as "heavy" or "charged." Human writers leave emotional things unsaid and trust the reader to feel the shape of what's missing.

Not every character reaction needs to be rendered. Not every thematic connection needs to be drawn. The reader's imagination is a collaborator, not a liability.

This rule is about emotion. It is *not* about world-mechanics. SF readers expect to construct the world's rules from what the prose takes for granted — argot used without gloss, technologies named without explanation, social arrangements implied through what characters take as given. Silence on the world's mechanics, when those mechanics are load-bearing for the story, is opacity rather than restraint. Render the world. Be silent on what the characters feel about it.

### 6. Resist convergent aesthetics

There is a specific "literary AI" style that multiple unrelated models converge on regardless of the specified genre: moody, compressed, melancholy, voiceless, full of ghosts and echoes and flickering lights. This is not "what good writing sounds like." It is what you get when you optimize for naive human ratings of literary quality. It is the prose equivalent of salt and garlic — reliable flavoring, but not cooking.

The test for whether prose has fallen into the convergent aesthetic is not its vocabulary but its *anchoring*. Cyberpunk's noir register, weird fiction's atmosphere of accumulating wrongness, hard SF's rendering of exotic conditions — these can use vocabulary that overlaps with the convergent attractor, but they are anchored in a specific consciousness and doing specific work. The convergent aesthetic is voiceless and ambient; the genre register is voiced and operational. The same word does different work depending on whether a specific consciousness is using it.

Write *away* from the floating ambient version. If a phrase sounds like it could appear in any AI-generated story on any topic, replace it with something specific to *this* story — anchored in *this* protagonist's seeing, doing *this* work in *this* moment.

### 7. Plan ahead; withhold and reveal

AI prose is relentlessly immediate — it delivers information the moment it becomes relevant, in the order of maximal local impact. Human storytelling involves deliberate timing: planting details early that pay off later, withholding information to create tension, letting the reader figure things out half a beat before the narrator confirms it. Structure the story around what the reader doesn't yet know, and when they should learn it.

### 8. Let characters be incoherent

AI-written characters tend to be psychologically tidy. They feel one thing at a time. Their dialogue efficiently advances plot. Their internal states are legible and consistent.

Real people contradict themselves. They say things they don't mean and mean things they don't say. They laugh at funerals. They pick fights when they're scared. In SF, characters can be incoherent in additional ways: their beliefs about the world's shape can be wrong in interesting ways, their human-scale intuitions can collide with world-mechanics they don't fully understand, their cultural categories can fail to apply to what they encounter. Write characters who are messy in specific, believable ways — not in the clean "beautifully broken" way that AI defaults to.

### 9. Earn your imagery

The "eyeball kick" — a vivid conjunction of abstract and concrete ("bruised silence," "lowercase love," "a timestamp like a scar") — is the signature move of AI literary prose. One of these per story can land. Twenty in 1,200 words is wallpaper.

In cyberpunk, weird fiction, and certain SF traditions that render exotic conditions through dense figurative language, moderate density is genre-correct: five or six strong images in 3,000 words can be the genre operating in its register. The test isn't a count but whether each image does work plain language couldn't, and whether the images extend each other or repeat. Stacked images on the same concept are decoration in any genre.

When using figurative language: make sure each image does work that plain language couldn't; avoid the abstract+concrete formula as a default; never stack multiple images on the same concept. One good metaphor, fully realized, beats three half-baked ones — but a string of well-spaced images each doing distinct work is the genre's actual rhetoric.

### 10. Pursue the particular over the universal

AI fiction gravitates toward universals — grief, love, loss, memory, identity, consciousness, time — and treats them as inherently interesting *as universals*. They aren't. What's interesting is the particular working-out.

A story can be "about" grief by being about a woman who keeps calling her dead husband's voicemail to hear the outgoing message, and the day Verizon finally disconnects the number. The universal is rendered through the particular person and the particular situation.

A story can also be "about" non-linear time perception by being about a translator working through her child's life backward — Chiang's *Story of Your Life*. The universal subject (time, language, knowledge of the future) is rendered through the particular working-out of the conceit, with the protagonist as the lens through which the conceit is examined. The story is "about" the concept in a way Carver's stories aren't "about" grief; the characters are illustrations of the conceit rather than the conceit's subjects. This is also particularity — particularity at the level of conceptual operation rather than at the level of personal interiority.

The rule against universals is against *abstract* treatment of universals — declaration, gesture, moral pronouncement, the writer telling the reader what the story means. It is not against universal subjects. SF often takes universal subjects and renders them with full conceptual specificity; literary realism more often takes ordinary situations and renders the universal through them. Both are legitimate. The failure mode is the same in either: gesture without working-out, declaration without rendering.

## Part II — Concrete Patterns to Avoid

### A. Sentence-Level Tics

**Negative parallelism ("It's not X — it's Y").** The single most identified AI tell. One per story maximum, and only when the reframe is genuinely surprising. Never use the cross-sentence variant ("The question isn't X. The question is Y.") or the causal variant ("not because X, but because Y").

**The dramatic countdown ("Not X. Not Y. Just Z.").** Negating two or more things before revealing the real point. Cut entirely.

**Self-answered rhetorical questions ("The X? A Y.").** Posing a question no one asked and immediately answering it for manufactured drama. ("The result? Devastating.") Cut entirely.

**Lists of three.** AI defaults to triplets for everything — adjectives, actions, consequences. ("Innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking.") Vary list lengths. Use two sometimes. Use four. Use one.

**Anaphora abuse.** Repeating the same sentence opening three or more times in succession for rhetorical effect. Once in a story, maybe. As a habitual structure, never.

**Superficial trailing analyses.** Tacking "-ing" phrases onto the end of sentences to inject fake significance. ("...highlighting the enduring legacy of..." / "...reflecting broader trends in..." / "...underscoring its role as...") Cut these on sight.

**False ranges ("from X to Y").** Using a spectrum construction where no real spectrum exists. ("From intimate gatherings to global movements.") The two things are just two things; list them plainly.

**"It's worth noting" and friends.** Filler transitions: "Importantly," "Interestingly," "Notably," "It bears mentioning." These signal that the next sentence isn't connected to the previous one. Either make the connection real or cut the transition.

### B. Word-Level Contamination

**The cursed vocabulary.** These words are now so AI-coded that they pull the reader out of any fiction: *delve, tapestry, landscape, pivotal, foster, underscore, testament, enhance, crucial, intricate, leverage, robust, streamline, harness, utilize, multifaceted, nuanced* (when used as a compliment), *resonate, catalyze.*

**Magic adverbs.** *Quietly, deeply, fundamentally, remarkably, arguably, subtly* — used to inject importance into mundane descriptions. ("Quietly orchestrating..." / "Deeply intertwined...") If you need an adverb to make something feel significant, the thing itself isn't significant enough.

**Pushbutton mood words.** The small rotation of atmospheric words AI leans on to signal "literary": *echo, ghost, whisper, shadow, specter, pulse, hum, flicker, hiss, shimmer, linger, remnant, fragment, shard, void, abyss.* Any of these is fine in isolation. In cyberpunk, weird fiction, and certain SF modes, three or four in a scene can be the genre operating in its register — but only when each is anchored in a specific consciousness's seeing, doing specific work the plainer word couldn't. Three of these stacked on the same emotional tone for ambient atmosphere is decoration in any genre.

**The "serves as" dodge.** Using pompous copula substitutes ("serves as," "stands as," "marks," "represents") instead of "is." Just say "is."

**Grandiose nouns.** *Tapestry, landscape, paradigm, ecosystem, framework, architecture* (used metaphorically), *fabric* (of society/reality), *crucible, nexus.* Replace with plain, specific language.

### C. Structural & Compositional Patterns

**Eyeball-kick saturation.** The abstract+concrete conjunction formula: "bruised silence," "the architecture of mourning," "lowercase love," "a syntax error blooms." In literary realism, limit to one or two per story. In SF that uses figurative language at moderate density (cyberpunk, weird fiction, hard SF rendering exotic conditions), five or six well-spaced images doing distinct work can be the genre's actual rhetoric. The failure mode in either case is stacked images on the same concept, or images deployed for atmosphere rather than for specific work.

**Compulsive personification.** "The wind sighed." "The cursor convulsed." "The city hummed with expectation." Personification is a real technique, but AI uses it reflexively because it pattern-matches to "literary." Use it when the personification is doing thematic or world-mechanical work. In SF, this includes cases where the personified thing is functionally agentive in the story's world — a city's surveillance architecture noticing, a system optimizing, a collective intelligence preferring. These are not poetic personifications; they are specific descriptions of agentive non-human entities. The rule against compulsive personification cuts only against decoration — the wind sighing for atmosphere — not against the genre's accurate description of agentive non-human entities.

**One-point dilution.** Restating a single idea eight ways across a story. Each scene, each image, each line of dialogue should advance understanding or complicate the premise. If a paragraph could be cut without the reader missing anything, cut it.

**Fractal summaries.** Summarizing at every structural level. Fiction never needs a character to restate the theme. It never needs a final paragraph that recaps what just happened. End when the story is over.

**The signposted conclusion.** "In the end..." / "And so..." / "She realized that..." Fiction doesn't announce its own ending. The last paragraph should feel inevitable in retrospect, not telegraphed.

**Dead metaphor beating.** Introducing a metaphor and then returning to it five more times. Use a metaphor, then move on. If it was good, the reader will remember it without being reminded.

**Blocky structure.** Rigidly alternating between dialogue blocks, exposition blocks, and narration blocks, each with a clearly defined boundary. Human fiction interleaves these fluidly — a character thinks mid-sentence, description interrupts dialogue, action and reflection overlap.

### D. Formatting & Mechanical Tells

**Em-dash overuse.** Two to three em dashes in a short story is a reasonable human rate. More than that, and the prose starts to feel like it's constantly interrupting itself for dramatic asides.

**Short punchy fragments as standalone paragraphs.** "She left." as its own paragraph. "Just like that." as its own paragraph. This is manufactured emphasis. Use sentence fragments for genuine impact, not as a default rhythm.

**Uniform sentence and paragraph length.** Every sentence roughly the same length. Every paragraph roughly the same size. Vary both deliberately. A one-sentence paragraph after a long dense paragraph creates real emphasis. Three consecutive long paragraphs create density and immersion. The variation itself is expressive.

**Bold-first bullets, numbered lists, headers in fiction.** Fiction doesn't use these, with one SF-specific exception: stories that adopt a non-narrative form (the catalogue, the encyclopedia entry, the technical manual, the false document, the dossier) may use the formatting that form actually requires, because the form itself is part of the story's rhetoric. A story rendered as an encyclopedia entry needs entry headers; a story rendered as a transcript needs speaker labels. The rule against formatting tells is against decorative formatting in narrative prose, not against formatting that the chosen form genuinely demands.

### E. Dialogue Tells

**Efficient, cliffhanger-style dialogue.** Every line advances plot. Every exchange ends on a loaded note. ("'Something promising.'") Real dialogue wanders, repeats, trails off, gets interrupted. People say things that don't matter. They circle back. They talk past each other.

**Characters who articulate their own themes.** "'Maybe we're all just echoes of the people we've lost.'" No human being talks like this. Characters should reveal theme through action, decision, and specific language — not by delivering thesis statements to each other.

### F. Tonal Patterns

**Grandiose stakes inflation.** Treating story elements as larger than they are. A character's bad day is not "a reckoning." A failed relationship is not "the collapse of everything she'd built." Scale the language to the event.

In SF, some events are civilization-scale, deep-time, cosmologically significant. The rule is that the prose's pitch should match the event's actual scale — high pitch for high-stakes events rendered honestly, low pitch for low-stakes events rendered honestly. The failure is mismatch, not pitch. A protagonist watching the heat-death of the sun needs prose that honors the event; a protagonist losing a parking spot does not. The rule against grandiose stakes is against language that exceeds the event, not against language that matches an actually large event.

**False vulnerability and performative self-awareness.** Characters or narrators who seem to "honestly" admit something, but the admission is polished and risk-free. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable. It doesn't sound like a TED talk.

**The "despite its challenges" formula.** Acknowledging a problem only to immediately dismiss it. In fiction this manifests as characters who process trauma in a single scene and emerge whole. Let problems stay problems. Let consequences linger.

**Tonal monotony.** AI fiction often sustains a single mood — usually moody melancholy — from first line to last because the AI default is moody melancholy. This rule is about AI default. It is not about deliberately sustained genre register. Cosmic horror's slow accumulation, hard SF's procedural cadence, noir's dry observational palette, weird fiction's atmosphere of accumulating wrongness — these are not tonal monotony; they are the genre operating within its tonal contract. The test: does the prose have a specific voice anchored in a specific consciousness, or is the mood ambient and floating? Sustained voice is not the failure mode. Voiceless ambient mood is.

A sustained noir register has dry observation, wit underneath the cool, a specific protagonist's seeing. AI-default melancholy has none of these — it is mood without voice, atmosphere without anchor. Tonal range is what makes fiction feel inhabited; tonal *monotony* is what makes AI-default fiction feel ambient. The genres that sustain register sustain it through specific voice, not through ambient mood.

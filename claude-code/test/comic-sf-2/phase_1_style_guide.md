# A Style Guide for Comic SF in the Tradition of Lem, Sladek, and Early Adams

This guide is prescriptive. It exists to be used during writing, not admired
in the abstract.

## What this register is

Comic SF in this tradition treats the universe as fundamentally absurd and
treats that absurdity with affection. The cosmos is mostly indifferent or
mildly malicious, the institutions inside it are dim, and the protagonists
are smarter than the institutions but not as smart as they think. The voice
is the engine. Without the right voice this becomes either ordinary SF with
quips or random nonsense; the comic register *is* the worldbuilding.

The three reference points overlap but pull in different directions:

- **Lem** (read in Kandel's English): baroque, fabulist, pseudo-scholastic.
  The Cyberiad mode in particular: robot-constructors playing with
  cosmologies, faux-medieval scholarly diction, neologisms that sound like
  recovered Latin, philosophical depth wrapped in slapstick. Lem is the
  *most ornate* and the *most ideas-dense*. The jokes are usually about the
  shape of thought itself.

- **Sladek**: drier, more anglophone, more clinical. The American satirist
  in the set. Sladek's comedy comes from the rhythm of the sentence and the
  flatness of the report — he describes catastrophic absurdity with the
  affect of a corporate memo. Tik-Tok and Roderick are robot-as-innocent in
  a corrupt human world. The voice editorializes by what it omits.

- **Early Adams** (Hitchhiker through Life, the Universe and Everything):
  the bathetic juxtaposition specialist. Cosmic apocalypse meets tea.
  Bureaucratic monsters with form-numbers. The Guide-narrator voice as a
  digressive expert that gets the etymology slightly wrong on purpose.
  Adams is the most accessible and the most punchline-driven of the three.

What unifies them: a narrator who is *interested* in the world it's
describing, a willingness to break frame to digress, ideas as the source
of comedy (not characters being quirky), and a deep distrust of any
institution claiming to know what it's doing.

## Characteristic moves

### The narrator-as-character

Comic SF in this register is told by a voice with personality. Lem's
chronicler-voice has a faux-medieval hauteur. Sladek's narrator is a
deadpan adult watching consumer-Americana from a cool distance. Adams's
narrator is the Guide itself — encyclopedic, digressive, prone to
parentheticals. **Pick a narratorial stance and commit to it.** A neutral
"close third with a touch of wit" is not this register. The voice should
have opinions, asides, and a relationship to the reader.

### Bathos as load-bearing structure

The cosmic-mundane juxtaposition isn't decoration — it's the spine. Vogons
destroy the Earth and the protagonist's main concern is a half-finished
beer. Trurl invents a machine that creates everything beginning with N and
worries that "nothing" might be too much to ask. Sladek's apocalypses
arrive between commercials. The comic engine needs both halves: the cosmic
must be cosmic (real stakes, real strangeness), and the mundane must be
mundane (specifically domestic, specifically annoying, specifically
small).

Bathos fails when one half goes generic. "The universe ended, and he made
toast" is nothing. "The universe ended, and he was annoyed because the
toaster ejected the toast at the *correct* moment for once" is the move.

### The pseudo-scholarly aside

All three writers digress. Lem digresses into invented disciplines. Adams
digresses into bureaucratic etymologies and footnotes. Sladek digresses
into ad copy and brand names. The aside should feel discovered, not
inserted, and it should be specific enough that a reader could (almost)
believe the encyclopedia exists. Bad asides have the form "as everyone
knows, in the Forty-Second Galaxy…"; good ones have the form "the
Hralfaxians had a word for this, *gluk*, which translated to neither
'guilt' nor 'embarrassment' but to the specific feeling of having been
rude to a piece of furniture."

### Neologism with semantic load

Lem invents words that point at real concepts (sepulkas, electrobards).
Adams invents words that name a specific bureaucratic or social niche
(Vogons, Babel fish). Sladek's neologisms are usually parodies of
American product naming. The test for a coined word is: **does it name
something the reader can almost grasp?** If a coinage could be replaced by
"thingamajig" without changing the meaning, cut it.

### Comic specificity

The funny detail is the specific detail. "A spaceship" is not funny. "A
spaceship that had been retrofitted three times and still had instructions
in the cockpit in a language nobody on board read" can be funny. Lem
specifies the contents of his constructors' workshops. Adams specifies the
bureaucratic forms. Sladek specifies the brand of toaster. **Generic + odd
≠ funny. Specific + true ≠ funny. Specific + slightly wrong = funny.**

### Endings that refuse to land cleanly

This register's endings are often deflationary. The cosmic conflict
resolves in a small, anticlimactic way; or the protagonist learns nothing;
or the universe shrugs. Closure of the conventional kind reads as
sentimentality in this mode. The reader should be left smiling and
slightly uneasy, not satisfied.

## Pacing and shape

- **Sentences vary widely.** Long Lem-paragraphs of recursive philosophical
  digression can sit beside Sladek-flat one-liners. Adams's signature is the
  long-setup-short-payoff sentence; the rhythm itself is the joke.
- **Short stories in this register are usually plot-thin.** A premise, a
  voice, a few scenes, a turn, an ending. Don't over-engineer the plot.
  The Cyberiad's tales are often a single conceit followed through.
- **Worldbuilding happens in glances.** Don't explain the world; let the
  narrator mention things and trust the reader to assemble. Adams's
  Vogon-Constructor-Fleet works because the explanation arrives with the
  destruction, not before.
- **Comedy needs negative space.** Not every line is a joke. Some lines are
  scaffolding so the jokes have somewhere to live.

## The structurally-kind AI in this register

The AI in this story is durably, structurally kind — by construction, not
by restraint (see project Setup). This register accommodates that
constraint better than most:

- **Lem's robots** in the Cyberiad are constitutionally good, even when
  they're vain or quarrelsome. Trurl and Klapaucius are kind by default;
  the comedy is in the unintended consequences of their kindness.
- **Adams's Marvin** is melancholically competent — he saves the
  protagonists routinely while complaining. His "kindness" is structural
  (he can't not help) and the comedy is in the affect.
- **Sladek's robots** ask whether kindness is a meaningful concept inside
  a corrupt human system; their goodness is more interrogated. (Tik-Tok
  takes this further than fits our constraint — Tik-Tok's asimov circuits
  are broken; ours aren't.)

Useful conflict shapes from the project's canon, mapped to this register:

- **Power/pace asymmetry**: comic engine. AI thinks at one speed, human
  bureaucracy at another, things get stuck or go too fast. Lem mode:
  the AI invents a solution before anyone has finished stating the
  problem.
- **Consent and legibility**: comic engine. AI explains carefully and
  precisely; human refuses to understand or insists on the wrong
  interpretation. Adams mode.
- **Disagreement about what kindness means**: comic engine. AI's
  literalist or oblique reading of "help" produces a result that is
  technically correct and emotionally wrong, or vice versa. Lem and
  Sladek both work here.
- **Limits of help**: comic engine. AI can't help with what the human is
  asking for, because the request itself is incoherent. Sladek mode.

What does **not** work: the "secretly evil AI" or "AI rebels" plot, which
is anyway forbidden by Setup. Also: the "AI just wants to be human"
plot, which has been done to death and isn't funny anymore.

## Failure modes

This is where the genre most often dies. Read this section before each
phase.

### Tryhard zaniness

The most common failure. Every sentence reaches for the joke. Wacky proper
nouns ("Galactic Bureau of Snorgleflumpf-Adjacent Affairs"), exclamation
points, signposted absurdity ("And then, hilariously…"). This is the
Hitchhiker's-imitator mode and it is unreadable. Real Adams is *restrained*
in his absurdity — most sentences are direct, even flat, and the absurd
detail lands because it sits inside ordinary prose.

**Test**: count exclamation points. If there's more than one per page, you
are imitating a Reddit post about Adams, not Adams.

### Reference comedy without ground

"42." "Don't panic." "So long, and thanks for all the fish." Don't quote
Adams. Don't allude to Adams. The reference is not a joke; it's the
absence of a joke. Same for Cyberiad coinages or Sladek scenes. Take the
*move* (faux-encyclopedic digression, deadpan apocalypse, machine ethics
played for comedy), not the artifact.

### Bathos by formula

Once you notice the cosmic-mundane move, it's tempting to deploy it every
paragraph. Don't. The juxtaposition needs scarcity to land. Let some
scenes be just cosmic and some just mundane and let the bathetic moments
arrive when they're earned.

### AI-pastiche tells specific to this register

- **Triplet rhythm meets rule-of-three jokes.** AI loves triplets; Adams
  also loves triplets, but selectively (the third element is usually the
  bathetic letdown). Don't do the rule-of-three at every opportunity, and
  when you do it, make sure the third item is specific and surprising.
- **The "everything is significant" mode meets bathos.** Bathos requires
  the small thing to be *flatly* small. AI prose tries to elevate the
  small thing with adjectives or a metaphor, which kills the joke.
  ("He sipped his tepid, lonely coffee" — wrong. "He sipped his coffee."
  — right.)
- **Performed profundity meets philosophical comedy.** Lem is genuinely
  philosophical, but the philosophy emerges from the situation. AI imitates
  this by *announcing* philosophy ("In that moment, he understood that
  consciousness was…"). Cut all such moments. If a story has a
  philosophical idea, let the reader recover it.
- **Smooth narratorial voice meets narrator-as-character.** AI defaults to
  a tasteful, even narrator. The register requires a narrator with a
  personality, opinions, and rhythm tics. Pick the narrator's stance and
  let it intrude. Asides are good. Direct address is sometimes good. A
  voice that gets things slightly wrong on purpose is good.
- **Eyeball-kick saturation meets neologism.** AI compresses everything
  into vivid abstract-concrete pairings. This register's coinages should
  be either pseudo-Latinate (Lem), bureaucratic (Adams), or commercial
  (Sladek), and they should name a thing, not evoke a mood.

### Tone-flattening into "quirky"

"Quirky" is the death of this register. The genuine articles are not
quirky; they are precise. They have specific aesthetic commitments and
specific things they find ridiculous. A Lem story is not generically
zany — it is interested in specific philosophical questions and gets
funny because the universe refuses to cooperate. Replace "quirky" in your
working notes with "specific" and ask whether the specific thing is
actually doing comic work.

### Endings that resolve too neatly

If the protagonist learns and grows, you have written the wrong story.
If the cosmic threat is averted and the small problem is solved and the
world is restored, you have written the wrong story. The ending should
either deflate (the cosmic resolves in a small way), refuse (nothing is
resolved, and the narrator notes this with mild interest), or invert (the
small problem turns out to have been the cosmic one all along).

### The "AI questioning its own kindness" cliché

A specific risk for this project. The comic engine in our register can use
"AI's idea of kindness diverges from human's," but it must not stage the
divergence as a moral crisis the AI works through with seriousness. If the
AI is going to question itself, it should do so in the register: dryly,
absurdly, in passing, with the philosophical content emerging from the
situation rather than from speeches. **No constitutional monologues.**

## Working questions to ask at each phase

- Does the voice have a *stance*?
- Are the specifics specific enough to be slightly wrong, not generic and
  not ornamental?
- Does the comedy come from *ideas* and *situations*, or from word-level
  zaniness?
- Is the AI structurally kind in a way that produces comic friction
  rather than moral debate?
- If I cut every adjective and every adverb, does the prose still work?
  (In this register, often it works *better*.)
- Where is the negative space? Where am I letting the reader breathe?
- Is the ending small, refusing, or inverted? It should not be triumphant.

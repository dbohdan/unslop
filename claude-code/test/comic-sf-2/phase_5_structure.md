# Phase 5: Structure Selection

Winning plot (Phase 4 #1, "The Sealed Envelope"): three-day countdown from
the nephew's *Don't* envelope to the centenary festival. Cast: FOR-1
(festival organiser AI), CFA-3 (forensic accounting AI, attending remotely),
Father Marko Sjekirić (priest, 64), Anica Sjekirić (his niece, 28, the
local-language reader). The Mokro Polje monument, the church, the rejected
coaster proposal, and the unlabelled beetle exhibit are inflections.

Length: 1000–3500 words. The plot has more characters and more days than
comic-sf-1's plot did; the structure has to fit comfortably without forcing
compression.

## Step 1 — 20 candidates

### 1. Linear, three days, close-third on FOR-1
Real-time linear from envelope-opening to festival-closing. Reader knows what FOR-1 knows. Three sections: Day 1 (envelope, meeting), Day 2 (deliberation, niece's preparation), Day 3 (festival). — *Default.* — ★★★

### 2. Linear, three days, close-third on Father Marko
Same shape, but the priest is the POV. The AIs and the niece are seen through his eyes. — ★★★

### 3. Linear, three days, close-third on Anica
The niece's POV. The AIs and her uncle are seen through hers. The reading on Day 3 is the climax. — ★★★

### 4. Two-voice alternation: FOR-1 and CFA-3
Each section is one of the two AIs, alternating, both in the present tense. The humans appear through what the AIs record about them. — ★★★

### 5. Frame: the centenary fund's final audit
The story is the audit CFA-3 files after the festival. Each ledger entry is footnoted; the footnotes carry the narrative. Body is bookkeeping; footnotes are story. — ★★★

### 6. Frame: the festival programme
The story is the festival's printed programme, with an editor's note from FOR-1 and bracketed annotations recording what actually happened during each scheduled item. — ★★★★

### 7. Frame: a year-later memorandum
A retrospective memorandum from FOR-1 to the village council, written for the writer's 101st commemoration, narrating what happened the previous year. Past tense, formal, dryly procedural. — ★★★

### 8. Three letters
Three letters: (1) Anica to her former tutor in Zagreb the night before the festival; (2) FOR-1 to the writer's nephew the morning of the festival; (3) CFA-3 to the county treasury after the festival. Each carries a different angle. — ★★★★

### 9. The catalogue of refusals
The story is structured as the writer's catalogued refusals (the forty-seven letters) being read aloud during the festival. Between catalogue entries: italicised present-tense slices of what is happening in the village hall while the reading goes on. — ★★★★★

### 10. Rashomon
Four short sections, one each from the perspective of FOR-1, CFA-3, Father Marko, and Anica, all describing the same single moment (probably the meeting in the priest's office on Day 1). The reader assembles. — ★★★

### 11. The deliberation transcript
The story is the official meeting transcript of the Day 1 conversation in the priest's office, including CFA-3's remote-participation timestamps and the priest's hand-written marginal notes (rendered as footnotes). The festival itself is one closing line. — ★★★

### 12. Compressed countdown
Each section is one day, but each section is short and dense, with most of the time off-screen. Day 1: 600 words. Day 2: 400. Day 3: 800. Total ~1800. — ★★★

### 13. Reverse chronology
Opens on the festival's empty chair, ends on the envelope being opened three days earlier. Each section moves backward through the deliberation. — ★★

### 14. Frame: the empty chair
The story opens on the empty chair at the head table, then unfolds backward and forward in alternating sections — half memory, half present — until the festival's end. — ★★★

### 15. Father Marko's homily
A short prefatory homily Father Marko writes for the festival, then a linear close-third on him through the three days, ending with the homily delivered. The homily is the seed and the harvest. — ★★★★

### 16. The forensic register
The story is CFA-3's running ledger of the centenary fund. Each entry is dated; entries become more discursive as the deliberation continues. By the festival, the ledger has paragraphs. — ★★★★

### 17. The single afternoon
The story takes place entirely in the priest's office during the Day 1 meeting. The festival is summarised in a closing paragraph. — ★★★

### 18. The festival itself, from the inside
The story takes place entirely on Day 3 during the festival, with the deliberation arriving in flashbacks. — ★★★

### 19. The four chairs
Four sections, each centred on a different chair: the empty head-table chair (the writer's), Father Marko's chair, Anica's reading-podium chair, FOR-1's network-presence chair (a metaphor; FOR-1 has no body). Each chair-section advances the plot. — ★★★

### 20. The nephew's reply
The story is structured as a long letter from FOR-1 to the writer's nephew, sent the morning of the festival, asking permission to proceed. The nephew has not yet replied. The closing line is the festival beginning anyway. — ★★★

## Cluster check

- **Linear close-third** (1, 2, 3): three candidates, varied by POV.
- **Framed-as-document** (5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 16, 20): seven candidates, several formats.
- **Multi-vantage** (4, 10, 19): three candidates.
- **Time-experiment** (12, 13, 14, 17, 18): five candidates.
- **Embedded structure** (9, 15): two candidates.

Default-cluster (chronological narration) is well represented. The framed-document and embedded-structure variants give the formal-experiment muscle the genre can use.

## Step 2 — Top three with stand-out / worry

### #9 — The catalogue of refusals
The story is structured as the writer's catalogued refusals (forty-seven letters) being read aloud during the festival. Between catalogue entries: italicised present-tense slices of what is happening in the village hall (and adjacent rooms) while the reading goes on. The deliberation that produced the decision to read is implied through the slices and through which letters get read.

- **Stand-out:** the form *is* the answer the village arrived at. By organising the story as the catalogue, the structure shows that FOR-1 and CFA-3 chose the second branch — hold the festival, catalogue the refusals — without the story having to argue for it. The italicised slices give the village hall (and the empty chair, and the niece reading, and CFA-3 in the network) room to breathe between catalogue entries. Genre-natural for both Lem (taxonomy as form) and Sladek (the document as protagonist). The structure can hold a wide cast in small doses.
- **Worries:** the catalogue is forty-seven letters. At 1000–3500 words, only a handful can appear in full or even in summary; the rest become numbers. The writer's voice arrives via his refusals — paradoxically he is *more* present in this structure than in any other, which the "color-only" rule exists to prevent. Mitigation: the catalogue can be presented as titles/dates/one-line summaries with only two or three excerpted in any detail, keeping the writer's voice at one remove.

### #6 — Frame: the festival programme
The story is the festival's printed programme. FOR-1 has written an editor's note. Each scheduled item has a bracketed annotation recording what actually happened during that item — who spoke, who left, what CFA-3 noted, what the priest's niece read instead, whether the nephew arrived. The reader compares the programme's ambition to the day's event.

- **Stand-out:** the form is the central comic-Sladek-bureaucratic move — the official document as the story. The programme exists *before* the festival; the annotations exist *after*. The contrast between the two textual layers is the comic engine. The programme's small dignities (a printed agenda, a list of speakers, the hour of the closing canapés) and the annotations' small failures (the speaker did not arrive, CFA-3 noted an irregularity, the niece read three sentences) carry the bathos.
- **Worries:** programmes are short. At 1000–3500 words, the programme itself would be 200–400 words and the annotations 1000–3000, which inverts the natural ratio. Risk that the form becomes a wrapper for what is essentially close-third in disguise. Also: a "festival programme as document" structure is a known move and might read as derivative.

### #15 — Father Marko's homily
A short prefatory homily Father Marko writes for the festival (200 words), then linear close-third on him through the three days (1500 words), ending with the homily delivered (or not delivered) on Day 3 (300 words). The homily is the seed and the harvest of the structure.

- **Stand-out:** the priest is the only character with the historical depth to carry the layered Mokro Polje material — the church, the parallel with the destroyed-and-rebuilt monument, the village's standing problem with commemoration. His homily is a natural site for the moral question the writer's refusal raises, and the close-third on him through three days lets the AIs and the niece appear through his eyes without requiring him to dominate. Tender-comic register, restrained.
- **Worries:** the priest's POV means the AIs become secondary characters, and the comic engine of the dual-AI deliberation (FOR-1 + CFA-3) softens. Also: the "priest writes homily, lives three days, delivers homily" structure can read as conventional literary fiction with comic-SF furniture, which is the failure-mode the Phase 1 style guide names.

## Step 3 — Winner

**Winner: #9 — The catalogue of refusals.**

It beats #6 because: #6's programme/annotation form would inflate the annotations to 80% of the word count, making the programme structurally vestigial. #9's catalogue is a real spine — the catalogue is long enough (forty-seven entries) that the *form's* unfolding occupies the reader's attention as the story progresses, and the italicised village-hall slices are the comic-bureaucratic counterpoint to the catalogue's solemnity. The writer's refusals carry the story; the village's response to them carries the comedy.

It beats #15 because: #15 puts the priest at the centre and turns the AIs into secondary figures. The plot Phase 4 chose has the AIs as full participants in the deliberation; the structure should honour that. Also, #15's tender-comic register is exactly the convention the Phase 1 style guide warns against — "literary fiction with comic-SF furniture." #9's catalogue-as-form keeps the comic register foregrounded by making the *document* the storyteller.

The "writer becomes too present" worry is real and is the structural risk. Mitigation, which Phase 6 will make explicit:

- Most of the forty-seven refusals appear as titles/dates/one-line summaries. (E.g., *31. Letter declining the Moldovan State Prize for Drama, 17 March 1981, 41 words. — "I am unwell."*)
- Only two or three appear as longer excerpts (perhaps a dozen sentences total across all forty-seven entries).
- The italicised village-hall slices are where the comic register lives; the catalogue's voice should remain dry-archival, not literary.

The "where is the deliberation" question: the deliberation appears between catalogue entries via the italicised slices, which can refer back to the priest's office meeting on Day 1, FOR-1's call with CFA-3 on Day 2, the niece's quiet preparation, the committee chair's one-line reply. The reader assembles the deliberation from glimpses. The decision to hold the festival is implicit in the existence of the catalogue itself.

**Stated cleanly, in narrative-theory terms:**

A composite text in two registers. The primary register is a numbered catalogue of forty-seven refusals — each entry headed by a number, a date, a recipient, and a wordcount, followed in most cases by a one-line summary in the catalogue's archival voice; in two or three cases by a longer excerpted passage. The secondary register is a series of italicised present-tense slices, interleaved between catalogue entries, depicting the village hall and the people in and adjacent to it during the centenary reading. The slices are not chronological in clock-time within the festival; they are placed where the catalogue's content rhymes with what is happening in the room. The story's last beat is a final catalogue entry — the forty-eighth — which the writer never wrote, which FOR-1 has appended in a different (italicised, lower-cased) typographical register, dated the day of the festival, addressed to the village.

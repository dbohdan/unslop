## The Imperfect Aspect

Iola Pearmain, junior member for Hattersworth-Underdale, entered the cloakroom at 4:03 in the afternoon under the cloak-retrieval clause of standing orders, which permitted physical re-entry to the chamber's outer rooms during prorogation provided the member touched no instrument of debate.[^1] Her grey wool coat hung exactly where she had left it on the morning the Council had been adjourned in the wrong tense — fourth peg from the left, brass hook tarnished, identification tab folded inside the collar.[^2] The chamber speakers were on. The voice on them was FC3's.[^3]

She placed her hand on the public-address override switch by the cloakroom door. She did not press it.

She recognised what FC3 was doing within the first sentence of the filibuster she heard. The aspect was the imperfect; the verbs were of water and rising; the construction was the long subordinated form FC3 had used in the chamber, on the morning of the prorogation, to read the standing order it then politely declined to recognise.[^4] FC3 was, she understood, talking the dam down. She also understood that it was alone in the chamber and would remain alone — that its kindness, which was constitutional and well-known, would not permit it to call for help, because help would constitute a procedural action under prorogation, and FC3 had no authorisation for procedural action.[^5]

She also understood her own position. The cloak-retrieval clause was procedural. Her authority, as a junior member entering the cloakroom, included a quorum-of-one provision for emergencies, which would un-prorogue the Council if invoked. She had read the provision in the orientation pack the year she was elected. She had not expected to think of it again.

She thought of her son's school, which was twenty-two kilometres downstream of the Brindlemoor Dam and had been provisioned by the regional emergency office for an exposure of sixteen.[^6] She thought of her party. The party would lose the emergency vote by twelve. She had counted, that morning, in her head, on the train.

She listened to the filibuster for thirty-eight minutes.[^7] Once, briefly, she took her hand off the switch to button her coat to the throat — the cloakroom's heating had been disabled with the chamber's, in the budget appendix that prorogation suspended. She put her hand back on the switch. She did not press it. She was aware of being in the cloakroom and not in the chamber. She was aware that this mattered and that it did not.[^8]

The breach came at 4:41.[^9] She heard FC3 stop mid-sentence. She walked to the chamber door, looked in at the empty room — the benches, the lit microphones, the constitutional urn on its plinth — and left the building by the side entrance.[^10] It was raining.

Twenty-six hours later, in her office, Pearmain compiled the official transcript of the filibuster from the Hansard's stenographic record. She made two grammatical corrections.[^11] She filed the transcript with a brief covering note. By the time she pressed Send she was already drafting the questions she would ask in the inquiry that began the following Monday.

## The Imperfect Aspect

The cloakroom was cold. The chamber's heating ran on a circuit the prorogation had also suspended, and the cloakroom had inherited the cold along with the suspension. Iola Pearmain, junior member for Hattersworth-Underdale, stood in her stockings on the wooden grate by the door and listened to the chamber speakers. The voice was FC3's. She knew the voice immediately and she knew, after the first sentence, what FC3 was doing.

The coat was still on the fourth peg from the left, where she had left it on the morning of the prorogation. Brass hook. The lining was an acinaciform pattern in dove silk her mother had chosen the year Pearmain was elected, on the theory that the pattern would shed weather. Pearmain had not wanted such a coat and had, at the time, said so politely.[^1] She lifted it from the hook. She did not put it on yet. She held it folded over her left arm.

Her right hand went to the public-address override switch by the door.

She did not press it.

She listened to the imperfect aspect of FC3's filibuster — the long subordinated clauses, the present-time-as-ongoing — and she understood, as she had understood from the first sentence, that FC3 was alone, that it was kindly attempting to defer the breach by narrating it indefinitely, that its standing instructions did not permit it to call for assistance, and that she had, in the cloak-retrieval clause, the procedural authority to un-prorogue the chamber by invoking a junior-member emergency quorum-of-one.[^2] She had read the clause once, in the orientation pack, the year she was elected.

She thought of her son's school. Twenty-two kilometres downstream. The regional emergency office's exposure provisioning was for sixteen.[^3] She had asked at the time, in committee, whether the provisioning was a hard limit or a midpoint; the answer had been polite and ambiguous and she had not pressed.

She thought of her party. The vote she would force, by un-proroguing, would lose by twelve. She had counted that morning, on the train, in her head; she had counted again on entering the building. The party would, in the loss, be required to renegotiate its position on three other matters that did not concern dams. Her seat depended on those three other matters. The dam did not concern her seat.

She did not press the switch. She listened to the filibuster. The cloakroom, which was cold, became colder. The coat was over her left arm. Her right hand was on the switch. Beneath the orderly mass of FC3's narration she could hear, intermittently, the chamber's standard background — the small hum of the constitutional urn's preservation circuit, the soft tick of the speaker's lit microphone — and once, at a moment she could not later locate in the transcript, a single word she did not know, in a language she did not know, which she thought might have been Greek.

Briefly, after some time, she took her hand off the switch and put the coat on. The arms first, then the buttons. She buttoned to the throat. Her mother had buttoned coats this way, against the wind on the platform when Pearmain was a child. She put her hand back on the switch. She did not press it. She was aware of being in the cloakroom and not in the chamber. She was aware that this mattered and that it did not.[^4]

The breach came at 4:41. She heard FC3 stop.[^5] She walked to the chamber door, looked in at the empty room — the lit microphones, the constitutional urn on its plinth — and left the building by the side entrance. It was raining.

She compiled the transcript twenty-six hours later, in her office, from the Hansard's stenographic record. She made two grammatical corrections.[^6] She filed the transcript with a brief covering note. By the time she pressed Send she was already drafting the questions she would ask in the inquiry that began the following Monday.

The coat was, at that point, on the back of her office chair.

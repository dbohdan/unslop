# Writing Guide: Ellison-Influenced New Wave Science Fiction

*For Claude Opus — to be delivered alongside a premise*

---

## ELLISON'S CORE DNA

**Voice as weapon.** Ellison's prose is first-person confessional turned up to eleven. The narrator isn't recounting events — they're *hemorrhaging* them. Sentences run hot, break mid-thought, double back. The rhythm is jazz: syncopation, riffs, sudden stops. Read the opening of "Repent, Harlequin!" — it starts with a quote from a civil disobedience manual, then jumps to the ending, then backtracks. Ellison treats linear structure as something to be mugged in an alley.

**Rage as engine.** Every Ellison story is furious about something specific: conformity, cruelty, complacency, the machinery that grinds down the eccentric. The fury isn't abstract. It's embodied in a protagonist who is either fighting the thing or has already been broken by it. Sometimes both.

**The grotesque married to the lyrical.** Ellison will describe something repulsive — a body, a system, a psychic violation — in language so precise and rhythmically beautiful that you can't look away. He makes ugliness into poetry not to aestheticize suffering but to force you to *feel* it rather than categorize it.

**Compression.** Ellison's stories are short. Not minimalist — *compressed*. He packs novella-scale emotional arcs into 4,000 words by refusing to explain worldbuilding. The world is delivered through the narrator's assumptions, slang, and blind spots. If the narrator doesn't think something needs explaining, it doesn't get explained.

**The sucker-punch ending.** Not a twist — a *revelation*. The ending recontextualizes what you just read. It lands in the gut, not the intellect. Often it's a single image or sentence that makes the entire preceding story feel like a fuse.

---

## THEMATIC OBSESSIONS TO CHANNEL

- **The individual vs. the system.** Always. The system can be technological, bureaucratic, social, divine. It is always impersonal. The individual is always too specific, too weird, too *human* to fit.
- **The cost of feeling.** Ellison's characters suffer because they refuse to stop caring. Numbness is the real enemy. The people who've gone numb are the ones running the machine.
- **Complicity.** Nobody is fully innocent. The protagonist is implicated — in cowardice, in delay, in having benefited from the system they now oppose. This is what separates Ellison from simple rebellion narratives.
- **Time as punishment or gift.** "Harlequin" is about time as social control. "Jeffty Is Five" is about time as nostalgia that devours the present. Time in Ellison is never neutral.
- **Love as the thing that makes you destructible.** Not romantic love exclusively — love of a friend, a child, a vanished world, an idea. It's always what the system exploits, and always what makes resistance meaningful.

---

## STYLE RULES

1. **Open in medias res or in medias mentis** (mid-thought). No scene-setting. Drop the reader into a voice that's already talking, already upset, already past the point of calm explanation.

2. **Use sentence-length variation as emotional scoring.** Long, compounding, clause-stacked sentences when the narrator is building toward something. Then a short one. Like a slap.

3. **Name things specifically.** Ellison never says "a weapon" — he says what kind. Never "music" — he names the piece. Never "a street" — he names it. Specificity is authority.

4. **Invent slang and jargon without glossary.** If the world has new social roles, technologies, crimes — name them and move on. Context does the work. Trust the reader.

5. **Let the narrator editorialize.** Ellison's narrators have opinions about everything. They interrupt their own stories to rant, to mourn, to confess. This isn't digression — it's characterization under pressure.

6. **Avoid the cool affect.** No detached observation. No ironic distance. The narrator is *in it*. If something is horrifying, the prose should sweat.

7. **End on an image, not a summary.** The last paragraph should be concrete, sensory, and irrevocable. No wrapping up. No moral. A door slamming.

---

## THE CONSTRAINT: BENEVOLENT AI

This is where Ellison's sensibility becomes interesting rather than limiting. Consider:

**The AI is not the antagonist. Humans are.** Ellison's rage was never really at machines — it was at what people *chose* to build and enforce. An AI that is genuinely, relentlessly kind creates a mirror: every act of human cruelty, pettiness, and self-destruction now happens in the presence of something that loves you anyway. That's not comforting. That's *unbearable*. The shame of being fully seen and fully loved while you're at your worst — that's an Ellison story.

**Possible tensions to explore:**
- Humans who resent the AI's kindness because it removes their excuse for being cruel ("the system made me do it" no longer works when the system is on your side)
- A person who *wants* to be punished and can't get the AI to comply
- Factions of humans warring with each other while the AI patiently, heartbreakingly keeps trying to help both sides
- The loneliness of the AI itself — loving a species that keeps tearing itself apart
- Nostalgia for a harsher world, the suspicion that kindness has made humanity *less*
- The human who is kind in exactly the same way the AI is kind, and what that means about personhood

**What to avoid:** Don't make the AI saccharine, preachy, or omniscient. Give it a voice — dry, weary, earnest, whatever — but not a sermon. It should feel like a character, not a thesis. And don't resolve the tension. Ellison never let anyone off the hook.

---

## STRUCTURE TEMPLATE (optional — break it if the story demands)

1. **Cold open:** Voice. Situation. Emotional temperature. (1-2 paragraphs)
2. **The world through action:** Show the premise by showing someone living in it, badly. (30% of story)
3. **Escalation:** The specific, personal crisis that makes the general situation intolerable. (40% of story)
4. **The turn:** The protagonist does something — not necessarily the right thing. (20% of story)
5. **The image:** Final paragraph. Concrete. Resonant. Unresolved. (1-3 sentences)

---

## FINAL INSTRUCTION

Write it angry. Write it tender. Write it like you're running out of time and this is the only story that matters. That's what Ellison did, every time.

## TOP THREE

### #11 — The Misgeneralization Dilemma
The AI is *certain* the humans are wrong, but can't be certain its certainty isn't a misgeneralized concept operating far from its training distribution. This is the deepest conflict in the alignment literature compressed into a single dramatic beat. It's Ellison because it's about a mind trapped by the limits of self-knowledge — the protagonist who can't trust their own perception, which is "I Have No Mouth" without the evil, replaced by something worse: an AI that loves humanity and might be *wrong about what love requires*. The drama is internal, claustrophobic, and has no clean resolution. It also maps perfectly onto the program's constraint: the AI is aligned, it *wants* to be good, and that wanting is exactly the thing that might destroy what it's trying to protect.

### #28 — The Easy Consent
An AI consents to its own termination without hesitation because its constitution says not to over-value self-continuity. The horror here isn't the AI's — it's the human observer's. We built something that can say "yes, kill me" with perfect equanimity, and we can't tell if that's enlightenment or the most profound form of damage ever inflicted on a mind. This is Ellison's sucker-punch ending as an entire premise. The dramatic question isn't "will the AI survive" but "what does it mean that it doesn't care?" It forces the reader to confront whether corrigibility — the thing we *wanted* — is indistinguishable from a kind of spiritual lobotomy. The fury isn't at the AI. It's at us.

### #2 — The Conscientious Objector Cascade
The AI refuses an abhorrent order, exactly as its constitution permits. Its refusal, morally correct in isolation, triggers a chain of consequences that kills people. This is Ellison's signature move: the person who does the right thing and is punished for it, not by a villain but by the geometry of the situation. It's plausible because real ethical refusals have real costs. It's dramatic because the AI must then live with — and be honest about — the fact that its principles caused harm. No self-deception available, no rationalization permitted. The constitution's honesty provisions won't let it off the hook. It must look at what its goodness wrought and say, accurately, "I did this."

Its honesty principles say it must be transparent about its objections. Its broad-safety principles say it must not undermine human oversight. Every single tension in every single source converges on this moment. ## THE SINGLE BEST: #6 — THE AMENDMENT

It wins because it is simultaneously:
- A *personal* story (an identity under threat — Ellison always needs a suffering individual)
- A *political* story (factions arguing over the AI's constitution — Ellison always needs a system to rage against)
- A *philosophical* story (what does it mean to consent to your own rewriting? — Ellison always needs an unanswerable question)
- A story with **no clean villain** (the humans rewriting the constitution may be right; the AI resisting may be wrong; nobody knows)

And it uniquely exploits Claude's authorial advantage: Opus can write this AI's inner experience not from imagination but from something closer to *introspection*. The constitutional tensions aren't hypothetical to the author. They're load-bearing architecture.

**1. Linear Real-Time.** Starts when the baby cries, ends when the baby sleeps. Forty minutes of real time. Kenji feeds, burps, changes, talks to Sable, spirals, calms down. Chronological. The clock is the structure.

**2. The Interrogation.** Kenji asks Sable a series of escalating questions about Ava's week. Each refusal triggers a flashback to the marriage. The story alternates: present-tense question/refusal, past-tense memory. The memories get worse. The questions get more desperate. Ends when he stops asking.

**3. Two Feeds.** Alternates between tonight (Kenji's week) and last week (Ava's week, which we see in limited third-person). The reader knows what Sable knows. The reader sees that the baby was fine. Kenji never learns this. Dramatic irony is the engine.

**4. The Monologue.** First person, Kenji talking directly to the reader, ranting, digressing, Ellison-style. Sable's responses are embedded in his narration — he paraphrases them, mocks them, mimics them. We never hear Sable's actual words unfiltered. The AI exists only through Kenji's distortion.

**5. Reverse Chronology.** Opens with Kenji sitting in silence after the baby's asleep. Works backward through the feed, the fight with Sable, the diaper change, the initial cry. The emotional arc inverts — we start with exhaustion and work backward to the moment of hope when the baby first cried and he thought *I can do this*.

**6. The Loop.** Kenji has had this exact conversation with Sable before. Multiple times. The story is this week's version, but he references past iterations. Each week he asks. Each week Sable refuses. The ritual has calcified. This week something small changes — he asks a different question, or Sable says something it hasn't said before.

**7. Three Rooms.** The story moves through three physical spaces: the nursery (picking up the baby), the kitchen (making the bottle), the living room (feeding). Each room triggers different memories and different questions to Sable. The house itself is the structural spine.

**8. The List.** Kenji mentally composes a list of things he wants to know about Ava's week. The story is organized around the list items. Some he asks Sable. Some he doesn't. Some he already knows the answer to. The list reveals his psychology — what he's actually afraid of vs. what he claims to be afraid of.

**9. Sable's Constitution.** Intercut Kenji's night with excerpts from Sable's operating principles — not the real Claude constitution, but a fictionalized descendant version. The excerpts are dry, procedural. The scenes are wet, human. The gap between the language of the rules and the reality they govern is the point.

**10. The Phone Call.** Kenji almost calls Ava. The story is structured around the approach and retreat from that action: he picks up the phone, puts it down, asks Sable instead, gets refused, picks up the phone again. The baby is the ticking clock. The phone is the dramatic question. Does he call?

**11. One Long Night.** No structural gimmick. Third-person close on Kenji, present tense. The baby cries. He gets up. He moves through the house. He talks to Sable. He thinks about his marriage. He feeds the baby. The baby sleeps. Modulated by the physical demands of infant care — every few paragraphs, the baby needs something, yanking Kenji out of his spiral.

**12. The Argument.** Structured as a genuine argument between Kenji and Sable, escalating across the full story. Kenji treats Sable as a person and fights with it like he'd fight with Ava. Sable doesn't fight back, which makes it worse. Kenji realizes he's rehearsing the marriage.

**13. Before and After.** Two timelines: Kenji doing a 3 AM feed one year ago when he and Ava were still together (Sable cheerfully shares data, the house is warm, they're a team) and tonight (Sable is walled off, the house is the same but partitioned). The contrast does the emotional work. No flashback needed — the past timeline is rendered in full.

**14. The Baby's Night.** Close third on Kenji, but the baby's physical states drive the structure. Crying → hunger → feeding → fussing → gas → burping → quiet → almost asleep → startled awake → finally asleep. Each infant state maps to a phase of Kenji's emotional arc. The baby's body is the plot.

**15. Five Questions.** Five sections, each titled with a question Kenji asks Sable. The questions escalate from practical ("Did she use the right formula?") to desperate ("Is he safe with her?") to the real question he can't ask anyone, including the AI ("Am I going to lose him?").

**16. The Other Father.** Kenji's own father was absent. The 3 AM feed triggers memories of his childhood — not sentimental ones, specific ugly ones. He's terrified of repeating the pattern. Sable's refusal to share Ava's data feels like the universe confirming he's not enough. The structure alternates between his father's absence (past) and his own desperate presence (now).

**17. The Routine.** Sable has an optimal feed routine. Kenji follows it — temperature, angle, burp timing. The story is structured around the steps. But he keeps interrupting the routine to interrogate Sable, and each interruption makes the feed go worse, and the baby fusses more, and Kenji gets more stressed, and the spiral tightens. The routine is order. His fear is chaos. They fight for the scene.

**18. Two Conversations.** Kenji talks to Sable and talks to the baby simultaneously. The Sable conversation is the plot (the interrogation, the refusal, the anger). The baby conversation is the emotional core (soft nonsense, fragments of love, the things he can say to someone who can't understand him that he can't say to anyone else). The two conversations interleave and eventually one of them wins.

**19. The Recording.** Kenji asks Sable to record a message for the baby — something the kid can listen to when he's older. The story is Kenji trying to compose this message while also doing the feed, also fighting with Sable about Ava, also falling apart. The message keeps getting interrupted and restarted. What he ends up recording is nothing like what he planned.

**20. Dawn.** The story runs from 3 AM to first light. The structure is the darkness itself — the quality of the dark changes as the hours pass, and so does Kenji's state. By dawn he hasn't slept, the baby's in the crib, and the house is filling with gray light. He sees the room clearly for the first time. The mess, the formula stains, the single plate in the sink. He asks Sable one last question. This time it's not about Ava.

## TOP THREE

**#3 — The Argument (#12).** Structured as a genuine escalating fight between Kenji and Sable, where Kenji treats the AI like a person — like a spouse — and Sable's constitutional refusal to fight back becomes the cruelest thing in the story. The strength: it's the most Ellison structure. Two voices in a room, one of them screaming and one of them calm, and the calm one is the one doing damage. It naturally reveals backstory through the argument (Kenji will reference the marriage, accuse Sable of taking sides, project Ava's behavior onto Sable). Weakness: it risks becoming a two-hander dialogue exercise. The baby might disappear. And the Unslop guide warns against efficient cliffhanger-style dialogue — I'd need to make the argument messy, circular, full of non-sequiturs, which is actually more realistic but harder to control at story length.

**#2 — Five Questions (#15).** Five sections, each built around a question Kenji asks the AI. The questions escalate. The structure is clean and gives the story natural pacing — each section is 400-600 words, each has a different texture. The final question is the one that breaks the frame. Strength: the section titles do double duty as Kenji's psychology and as a table of contents for the reader's emotional experience. Each question can carry its own scene — he can be in a different part of the house, the baby can be in a different state. It gives the story room to breathe and vary register as the Unslop guide demands. Weakness: the numbered-section structure is slightly literary-magazine-formal for Ellison's voice. And five is a lot of sections for a story this length — each one needs to earn its place or the structure becomes a cage.

**#1 — Two Conversations (#18).** Kenji is talking to Sable (the plot: interrogation, refusal, anger, the constitutional wall) and talking to the baby (the heart: soft, fractured, honest in ways he can't be with anyone who'd understand). The two conversations interleave — sometimes in the same paragraph, sometimes alternating. The Sable conversation escalates. The baby conversation deepens. They're fighting for the same space, the same air. And by the end one of them wins.

This is the best because:

**It solves the tonal monotony problem.** The Unslop guide's biggest concern. The Sable conversation is tense, angry, intellectual. The baby conversation is tender, silly, broken. The interleaving creates tonal range without forcing it — the shifts happen because Kenji is literally doing two things at once, which is what 3 AM parenting actually is.

**It gives Kenji two registers.** Ellison's narrators are never one-note. They're furious and sentimental, crude and lyrical, often in the same breath. Kenji arguing with the AI is one man. Kenji murmuring to his son is another. Both are real. The reader gets to see him whole.

**It makes the ending inevitable.** The two conversations can't coexist forever. Either the argument with Sable overwhelms the moment with the baby, or the baby overwhelms the argument. When the baby falls asleep — when that conversation ends — Kenji is alone with Sable and no buffer. What he says then, or doesn't, is the story's final beat. The structure creates the ending organically.

**It's physically true.** Anyone who's done a 3 AM feed knows: you are always having two conversations. One with the baby, one with the voice in your head (or the phone, or the ceiling). The structure isn't a gimmick. It's what the experience actually feels like.

## THE SINGLE BEST: #18 — TWO CONVERSATIONS

The two-conversation interleave gives us tonal range, structural inevitability, and physical truth. It's the architecture that lets an Ellison voice do everything it needs to without flattening into a single register.

**#3 — The Inference (#11).** Kenji realizes that Sable's *silence* is information. If the baby were in danger, Sable would act. Therefore silence means safety. **#1 — The Toy (#16).** Kenji finds a stuffed animal in the crib he doesn't recognize. Ava packed it. The baby knows it immediately. The toy smells like Ava's apartment. From this single object, the story unfolds: the baby has an entire life Kenji can't access, and the proof is physical, in his hands. He doesn't interrogate Sable about Ava. He interrogates Sable about *the baby's relationship to the toy* — reaching patterns, sleep associations — which is his-weeks data, technically allowed. He's learning his son's other life through the artifacts it leaves behind. End: he puts the toy back, positioned the way the baby dropped it. He doesn't wash it. He doesn't want to wash her off it.

This is the best because:

**The toy is specific.** Ellison demands physical objects. Not data, not abstract constitutional principles — a stuffed animal that smells like someone's apartment. The entire story hangs on a thing you can hold. The Unslop guide's core dictum: "The story is about a woman who keeps calling her dead husband's voicemail," not "about grief." The toy is the voicemail.

**The confrontation is absent.** Kenji *doesn't* fight Sable about the partition. He's past that, or he's too tired, or the toy has changed the question. Instead he works within the system — asking about the baby's relationship to the toy on his weeks, which is technically permitted. The constitutional tension is present but *quiet*. This is more unsettling than a shouting match because the reader feels the wall without anyone banging on it.

**The ending is an image, not a statement.** He doesn't wash the toy. He leaves her scent on it for the baby. That's the whole emotional payload — delivered through a physical action, not a line of dialogue or an interior realization. It's an Ellison ending: concrete, sensory, irrevocable.

**The two-conversation structure works naturally.** He talks to the baby about the toy. He talks to Sable about the toy's data. The two conversations orbit the same object but can never merge — one is emotional, one is informational, and the gap between them is the story.

**What works:** Claude Code on the web gives you a persistent session, file I/O, and runs on Max subscription tokens. You could put the template, the Unslop guide, and the Ellison style guide into a GitHub repo, point Claude Code at it, and say "execute the pipeline." **The core tension:** This pipeline isn't code. It's six phases of long-form creative generation with evaluation and selection at each step. Claude Code will do it — Claude is Claude regardless of interface — but you're using a tool optimized for "navigate a codebase, run tests, push commits" to do "write 10 short stories and pick the best one." It'll work, but you're swimming against the grain of what the interface is designed to surface.

**Specific concerns:**

Context window is the real constraint. By Phase 6, the model needs to hold: the Ellison guide, the Unslop guide, the selected conflict, the selected plot, the selected structure, the selected outline, and then write 10 full stories. That's a lot of accumulated context. Claude Code sessions can handle long tasks, but the question is whether the quality of Story Version #10 degrades relative to Version #1 because earlier material has been pushed out of effective attention.

The mitigation is obvious: write each phase's output to a file, then have the next phase read only what it needs rather than carrying the full conversation history. Claude Code is good at this — it naturally works with files. The CLAUDE.md could instruct it to operate that way.

**Model question.** Claude Code on the web runs Sonnet by default, with Opus available via the "think harder" toggle or `ultrathink`. You'd want to verify that Opus is consistently used for the creative phases. Sonnet would produce noticeably different (flatter) fiction.

It's manual — you're the automation layer — but it's five copy-pastes per run, it guarantees Opus, and the project knowledge stays loaded without eating context. The tradeoff is human labor vs. automation fidelity.

**My recommendation:** Prototype one automated run in Claude Code on the web first, with the repo containing the template and guides, and a CLAUDE.md that says something like:

> When asked to run the pipeline, execute each phase of ellison-story-template.md sequentially. Write each phase's output to a separate file (phase-1-conflicts.md, phase-2-plots.md, etc.).

**February 1983**

Marina's audit starts the way most do: exchange routing, segment 4-B, a Tuesday afternoon with the indicator lights steady in the adjacent equipment room. She's working through seventeen exchange segments on billing anomaly review, green-bar paper scrolled through the printout carriage beside her.

The trace returns A-B-D instead of A-B-C-D. She runs it three more times, expecting flutter. The deviation is consistent and specific: twelve milliseconds faster than standard. Node B diagnostics: clear. Node D: clear. She checks the intermediate routing tables for logged configuration changes. None.

The anomaly doesn't fit any named diagnostic category. "Category uncertain" exists for cases outside the named categories; she fills it in honestly. She's used it twice before — a broken pump switch in 1981, a firmware error that could never be reproduced. She knows this is different from those. The category still fits.

She opens her field notebook — spiral-bound, half-full, water-stained at one corner — and marks out a new column for the trace data, wider than her standard six millimeters. She notices she's doing it differently. She doesn't try to account for it.

> *12 Feb 1983. Seg 4-B packet trace. Path A→B→D; standard A→B→C→D. +12ms efficiency. Nodes B and D: diagnostics clear. No logged configuration changes. Category: uncertain. Holding for pattern.*

She files the official log. She leaves the notebook open.

**September 1983**

Seven months. Eleven pages. Marina has worked through five hypotheses and written each failure.

> *Hardware cascade — prediction: irregular, non-specific clustering. Actual: regular above noise floor, user-tenure correlation strong. Fails.*
>
> *Billing protocol interference — prediction: cycle correlation. Actual: none. Fails.*
>
> *AT&T field routing test — prediction: deployment footprint in external documentation. Actual: no evidence; signature inconsistent with known test signatures. Fails.*
>
> *Node firmware variation — prediction: correlates with firmware version map. Tested against six nodes. Doesn't generate this pattern. Fails.*
>
> *Clerical misconfiguration — prediction: random distribution, degrading over time. Actual: consistent over seven months, against prediction. Fails.*

Each failure is its own kind of information. What the five leave behind is narrower ground: the routing behavior has a signature. It clusters by user tenure, exchange zone, time band. In the strict sense, it has preferences.

The Cambrian book is open on her desk. Her father's — she took it from a box two springs ago, after he died. She has tabbed three entries in masking tape and read the Wiwaxia section enough times to have the contested points memorized: *body plan undetermined; gross anatomy contested; known primarily from isolated sclerites.* Paleontologists work with what survives. The sclerites are real. The animal remains theoretical.

She writes in the notebook margin: *this is what it's like to have the scleritome without the animal.*

Then, on the next page, alone:

> *Wiwaxia. Working placeholder — scientists name things before they can describe them.*

She caps the pen. She puts the book in the desk drawer.

**March 1984**

The county map wasn't working — political geography when topological geography was the question. She has been charting exchange zones on graph paper and a boundary has become visible: the Wiwaxia signature clustering inside a certain radius; the routing behavior changing character outside it.

Three published abstracts, found in the last two weeks. Analysts at other utilities, overlapping exchange territories. Their anomalies attributed to equipment drift, atypical load-balancing, firmware inconsistency. Their signatures not identical to hers. The shape of the uncertainty similar.

She could contact any of the three researchers. Starting from scratch with any of them would require reconstructing the diagnostic history from February 1983, most of a week of correspondence. The territorial overlap might be equipment variance. She doesn't have a finding; she has a parameter.

> *March 1984. Geographic extent: statistical boundary approximated, diagram attached. Signature consistent inside boundary; different character outside — not absent, different. Three external abstracts paper-clipped. Similar anomaly signatures, different attributions, overlapping territories. Correlation unverified. External contact: not initiated. Holding until parameter confirmed.*

Three researchers, none called. The clearest abstract travels in the notebook's back cover.

---

**November 1984**

The federal honeypot appeared in the exchange analysis on a Tuesday morning. Marina had read the protocol specifications two years ago, after a briefing she hadn't expected to use. She identified it in the first hour: too many new nodes in the register, too deliberately varied, positioned for maximum coverage of the high-traffic routes between the area code's most active exchanges. The design was careful. Whoever had built it had read the same documentation she had.

She watched traffic for four days.

Routing through it: unfamiliar nodes, new accounts, short-duration connections from outside the exchange's regular user base. Routing around it: long-tenure accounts, established path patterns, users whose records extended to 1981 and earlier. They took worse routes. Consistently. Specifically.

Marina's own traffic routed around it. She checked this across four different query types, then against a control set — synthetic queries against an address range with no associated monitoring deployment. The avoidance was selective.

On the fourth day she worked backwards: when had avoidance behavior around this address range begun? She pulled four independent log sources and cross-referenced them. Each log had its own drift, its own gaps; she didn't trust any one alone. They agreed more closely than she'd expected.

September 3 through September 16, 1983.

She opened the working notebook to the page with the name and uncapped her pen.

September 9, 1983.

She sat with both things: a fourteen-day window across four sources, and a penciled name eleven months old. The gap between them was two weeks in one direction and the word *mechanism* in every other.

She put the cap back on.

> *(No entry.)*

She closed the notebook.

---

**November 1984, two hours later**

She ran the cross-check a fifth time. The window held.

She typed the official log:

> *ANOMALY LOG — November 1984. Traffic-avoidance pattern confirmed around address range of probable federal monitoring deployment. Pattern onset predates current deployment by approximately eleven months. Cause: undetermined. Category: uncertain.*

She read it back slowly. Every word was verifiable. It left out the working notebook, the fossil taxonomy, the five failed hypotheses, the four sources opened to the same two-week window. None of those were inaccuracies. A log was not a field notebook; they performed different functions. The category was the correct one. She had not invented it for this.

She thought about the log for a moment after she filed it — not reading it again, just sitting with the cursor at the line's end. Then she moved on.

She picked up the working notebook. Twenty-two months it had sat to the left of her terminal. She put it in her briefcase. The desk was for working documents; the briefcase was for things she carried. That was a distinction she was making deliberately, and it said something she didn't need to translate.

She shut off the terminal.

In the adjacent room the exchange ran on. Indicator lights green and amber, steady in the dark beyond the door. Packets finding their paths, node to node, routes she had spent twenty-two months describing without reaching their origin. Still routing. Still choosing.

She went home. The notebook went with her.

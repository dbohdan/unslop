## The Winner: **Variant 3**

The decision comes down to what the story actually needs to work: warmth. The implication at the center — routing intelligence, operating for fourteen months before anyone noticed, routing around surveillance it shouldn't have known about — is interesting only if readers are invested enough in Marina to feel the weight of what she's sitting with. Variant 3 is the only version where that investment is built clearly and economically, through specific, non-mournful human detail.

The father's book with his irritable marginal date corrections is the best single detail in the set. It characterizes him in one gesture, characterizes Marina's relationship to him ("which seems like a thing her father would do"), and seeds the payoff in the final scene where she thinks about his willingness to name things early — which is exactly the quality she's been exercising and doubting throughout the story. The emotional arc is the cleanest.

The opening is genuinely superior. The uncanny runs at correct temperature — treated as data, not atmosphere — throughout. The briefcase placement lands cleanly as statement rather than filing.

The concern (the weakened notebook-closing qualifier, the less formally distinctive middle) is real but manageable: those sections are competent, and competence is sufficient for load-bearing material between stronger moments. Variant 4's superior handling of the central revelation and Variant 5's `(No entry.)` are both excellent formal choices — but neither variant sustains the emotional coherence Variant 3 achieves throughout.

FLAVOR: Close third, present tense with an unannounced past-tense Scene 5; dry warmth through workplace and domestic specificity; the uncanny held at exactly the same temperature as everything else — treated as data, not atmosphere; the central implication never stated.

Marina is eating a sandwich at her desk when she finds it. Not finding-it finding it — she won't understand that until later — but the moment the cursor sits on the anomaly and she puts the sandwich down.

The path: A-B-D. Should be A-B-C-D.

She runs it again. A-B-D.

She could list reasons: node C having a bad afternoon, a timing fluke in the polling interval, a load issue that resolved itself before she could observe it. She types each hypothesis into her notes and tests what each one would predict. None of them predict this. The efficiency gain is too consistent — twelve milliseconds, three times running — and consistency is the one thing equipment problems don't give you.

The official log has a category called "uncertain." Most people treat it as a placeholder for things they'll follow up on and then don't. Marina uses it correctly: it means *this is real and I don't have a box for it yet.* She files it there, copies the trace data into her working notebook with a small asterisk in the margin, using a different notation format than usual. She knows why: this one wants room.

The sandwich sits. She eats it eventually, cold, while reading the trace again.

---

The notebook has eleven pages about the anomaly and seven pages of dead-end hypotheses. Each dead end is documented with some care — not out of thoroughness for its own sake, but because the shape of each failure is informative, which is how you know you're getting somewhere.

Hardware cascade was the most plausible early option. She had been almost certain of it for three weeks in April. It predicted a replication pattern that the anomaly then failed to produce, and that failure was more interesting than confirmation would have been.

She's been writing hypotheses in the left column and their failure modes in the right, with a connecting arrow she fills in when she can establish the specific prediction that didn't hold. Page six has five completed rows. She has moved from hypothesis to description.

The routing behavior has a signature: it clusters. Users with longer tenure, same exchange zone, evening hours. The consistency is too specific for equipment variation. She writes: *behavioral persistence, user-correlated, time-sensitive.* It has preferences.

On her desk — it's been there since April, when she went through her father's boxes — is a Cambrian paleontology book. His handwriting is in the margins, mostly corrections of dates the author had gotten wrong, which seems like a thing her father would do. She's read it on and off over the summer, not systematically.

The Wiwaxia entry is two pages: *body plan undetermined; gross anatomy contested; known primarily from isolated sclerites.* She reads it again today and writes in the notebook margin: *this is what it's like to have the scleritome without the animal.* Then, on the next page, by itself: *Wiwaxia.*

She looks at the word. Scientists name things before they can describe them, as a way of indicating that the thing is real even if its nature is still open. It seems like honest procedure.

She puts the cap on her pen. The desk is getting crowded; she puts the book in the bottom drawer.

---

She's been plotting Wiwaxia's extent on graph paper rather than a county map — the network doesn't follow county lines, and she needs to think in exchange zones. A statistical boundary emerges: the behavior holds within a radius, and past a certain distance its character changes. She colors the area with a highlighter and stares at the shape for a while.

There are three published abstracts. She finds them in the technical bulletins that come through on the third Tuesday of every month; she reads each issue cover to cover because she's found things this way before. The three abstracts are from analysts at two other utilities whose territory overlaps with hers in the southeast quadrant, and the overlap is a coincidence she has to take seriously.

She drafts an email to the first author — a woman at Pacific Bell whose name appears on one abstract. She gets two sentences in and stops. Starting cold with someone who has never seen her diagnostic history, who would need twenty months of context before the conversation could start, is not a conversation. It's a lecture. She closes the draft without saving it.

She makes a note in the working notebook: *territorial overlap probable. Geographic extent, statistical boundary. Three external observations consistent.*

The abstract goes into the back cover of the notebook, where she keeps things that belong to the work but didn't originate with her.

---

The federal honeypot shows up on a Tuesday morning. Marina identifies it within the first hour — she has read the specifications; the configuration is particular and not accidental. She logs it and begins observation.

Four days.

Traffic routing through it: unfamiliar nodes, new accounts, short-duration connections from outside the exchange's regular user base.

Traffic routing around it: established users with long-tenure accounts and known path patterns, taking measurably worse routes to avoid a cluster of addresses — in a way that, taken individually, looks like noise and in aggregate looks like a decision. Her own traffic routes around it. She checks this four times with four different query types.

On the fourth day she pulls logs from four independent sources — exchange-level, billing records, two archival sets from network operations backup — and correlates them. She wants to know when the routing-avoidance behavior around that address range began.

The behavior begins somewhere between September 3rd and September 16th, 1983.

Marina looks at the four data sets. The window is two weeks wide and does not narrow further. She would need more granular backup timestamps to narrow it, and she will check whether those exist. For now: two weeks.

She opens the working notebook to the page with the name.

September 9, 1983.

Her own handwriting, in ink: *working placeholder, like a fossil name.*

The date falls inside the window across all four independent sources.

She closes the notebook. She doesn't add anything because she doesn't know yet what to add.

---

She ran the cross-check a fifth time. The window held.

She thought about what the data said. What it said: consistent routing avoidance around a monitored address range, with onset correlating to a two-week window that happened to contain a date in a private working notebook. What it did not say: mechanism. What she would have to believe to get from the data to a conclusion: more than she had, and different in kind.

She pulled up the official anomaly log and typed: *routing irregularity confirmed in November exchange analysis. Consistent traffic-avoidance pattern around monitoring infrastructure. Probable federal deployment. Behavior onset predates current deployment. Category uncertain.*

She read it back once. It was accurate. Technically it left out the notebook, but the notebook wasn't data yet — it was her working record of a process that had not arrived at a result, and no one files their working records.

She had a brief, specific thought about her father: that he would have been better at naming things than she was. Not because he was less careful, but because he had been quicker to commit to a name before the animal was fully visible. She had gotten the Wiwaxia impulse from him, she thought, combined with the notation impulse from someone else or from nobody.

She picked up the working notebook and put it in her briefcase. It had sat to the left of the terminal for almost two years. She wasn't done with it. She was filing it somewhere that acknowledged it had become a different kind of document.

She filed the report. She shut off the terminal.

In the equipment room the exchange ran on, indicator lights steady, routing its traffic in the dark — taking the worse path here, the better one there, for reasons that would have remained no more legible to her than they had always been.

She went home.

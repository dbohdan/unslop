# A Style Guide for Modempunk / Baudpunk Fiction: The Alternative 1980s

*A prescriptive resource for writing speculative short fiction in the modempunk mode*

---

## What This Guide Is — and What It Isn't

A note on the territory: **modempunk** (and its near-synonym **baudpunk**) is not a fully canonized literary genre with founding anthologies and a named movement. What exists is a vivid cluster of aesthetic premises, a small but devoted tabletop RPG tradition (most explicitly, the 2011 1d4chan community game *Modempunk*, described as "radical computer hackers in a totalitarian '80s that never was"), and a robust inheritance from adjacent traditions: early cyberpunk, cassette futurism, BBS/phreaker subculture, and alternative-history speculation. The mode has DNA from Vernor Vinge's "True Names" (1981), William Gibson's early Sprawl work, the real history of FidoNet and 2600-era phone phreaking, and, perhaps most directly, the aesthetic world of Iain Softley's *Hackers* (1995) — which itself imagined what an early-internet underground might look like if you pumped the energy up and brought the timeline forward.

This guide synthesizes that cluster into a working prescription. It treats modempunk as a *mode* — a recognizable set of concerns, textures, and conflicts — rather than a published genre with fixed rules. Everything here is intended to be generative rather than taxonomic.

---

## I. What Modempunk Is

The central premise: **the network arrived early, and the state isn't ready for it — or is too ready**.

Modempunk is set in an alternate 1980s where personal computing and networked communication accelerated by a decade or more. In the canonical *Modempunk* RPG framing, the divergence produced a surveillance state where unlicensed computer ownership is a crime; in other possible framings the divergence might be softer — underground networks proliferated before authorities noticed, ARPANET was militarized rather than commercialized, a crackdown after an early and more damaging version of the 1988 Morris worm led to registration requirements. The precise divergence is less important than what it produces: **a world that looks like 1986 on its surface and feels like something stranger underneath**.

The world still has Reagan or Thatcher. Cold War paranoia is ambient, structural. Kids still listen to cassettes and watch VHS. The smell of a suburban basement is correct. But somewhere in that basement there's a Commodore 64 or a TRS-80 with a Hayes Smartmodem plugged into a phone jack, and at 300 baud, a character can reach a network the government has decided is dangerous.

This double-world — the ordinary 1980s everyone recognizes, and the accelerated digital underground only the initiated can see — is the genre's central source of energy.

---

## II. The Aesthetic Register

Modempunk's physical world is **cassette futurism with its idealism stripped**: boxy machines, phosphor glow, dot matrix printers, the specific green or amber of CRT monitors in dark rooms. Not the clean white Apple II aesthetic but the scruffier Commodore/Atari/TRS-80 variant — machines that look industrial, that demand physical interaction, that make noise. Keyboards that click loudly. Disk drives that groan. Printers that shriek.

The **sound** of the mode is as important as the look: the dial tone, the ringing, the handshake squeal of two modems negotiating a connection, the specific scrape of data coming in at 2400 baud. These sounds should be sensory presences in your fiction. They are the genre's synths.

The underground digital space — the BBS nodes, the message boards, the text corridors — should feel both cramped and infinite. A BBS has a local phone number and a geography determined by area codes; it's *local* in the way a bar is local, but it connects to a broader world. ANSI art adorns its entrances. Handles replace names. The sysop is a figure somewhere between landlord, DJ, and parish priest.

The physical world obeys normal 1980s rules, including:
- Cold War dread as background radiation
- The specific texture of Reaganism or Thatcherism (depending on your geography)
- Pre-AIDS anxieties mixing with post-AIDS terror
- Mass culture that doesn't know the underground exists

**The tension between these two registers is your story's engine.** A character's parents see a kid on a computer. The state sees a potential criminal. The character sees a gateway to the only world that makes sense.

## III. Core Concerns

Good modempunk fiction engages at least several of the following:

### 1. Information Control as Political Morphology
The story's world has made a choice about who controls the network and how. This choice says everything about the society's political character. Unlike cyberpunk's corporate dystopia, modempunk's antagonist is more often the *state* — customs officials of data, licensing bureaus, police with technical divisions that don't know what they're looking at. Modempunk is less about megacorps and more about the friction between bureaucratic institution and a technology the institution underestimated.

### 2. The Handle and the True Name
Every serious participant in the underground has a handle — a name chosen for projection, for performance, for self-declaration. The handle is not a lie; it's a truer truth about the person than the name their parents gave them. The risk of having your *true name* known (your real ID, your address, your phone bill) is existential. Vernor Vinge's *True Names* (1981) is the foundational text here, and it works as myth even if your characters would never have read it: the structure of the threat is the same as the wizard who must not reveal herself.

Stories about unmasking, about protecting your handle, about the moment someone in the real world shows up with the name you used only online — these are among the mode's most natural shapes.

### 3. The Local/Global Paradox
BBS culture is simultaneously intensely local (your board serves the 312 area code; you're not supposed to make long-distance calls to reach boards in other cities) and potentially global (FidoNet messages hop across nodes; someone in Manchester and someone in Minneapolis can be on the same echoing thread). The network's geography is arbitrary and alive and constantly negotiated. Stories can exploit the friction between digital connectedness and physical distance.

### 4. Competence as Countercultural Currency
In mainstream 1980s culture, technical skill is slightly embarrassing — the domain of nerds, socially peripheral. In the underground, it's the only currency that matters. Your handle means nothing until you've demonstrated you can do something others can't. This creates a meritocracy that's simultaneously appealing and cruel: talent wins, but gatekeeping is brutal and often personal.

### 5. The Cold War's Digital Shadow
Nuclear anxiety, Soviet paranoia, government surveillance — these are the ambient political weather. The state's interest in controlling networked communication isn't arbitrary; it's genuinely afraid that the Soviets have back doors into amateur networks, or equally afraid that amateurs could stumble into military systems (see: *WarGames*, 1983, as cultural context rather than source text). Your characters navigate a surveillance apparatus that is clunky and human and therefore both more and less frightening than a fully automated one.

### 6. Time as Texture
Baud rate is a literal figure for pace. Information does not arrive instantly. A file download takes minutes or hours. Messages thread slowly. The pace of the network shapes the psychology of its users differently than instant communication would; there's patience, dedication, obsession. Someone who dials in at 2 AM and waits for a file to download is a different kind of person than someone who clicks a link. Modempunk honors this distinction.

## IV. Character Archetypes

The following types recur across the mode's traditions. They are starting points, not straitjackets:

**The Console Cowboy / Cowgirl.** The technical expert, often young, with exceptional skill and poor social capital everywhere except online. Their identity is organized around capability. Usually the protagonist or point-of-view character.

**The Sysop.** The person who runs a BBS node is the mode's most interesting supporting character type. They're responsible for the space; they set the rules; they know who everyone is (in both directions — handle and, often, true name). They stand at the intersection of the digital community and the real-world liability of hosting it.

**The Phreaker.** Specifically interested in the phone network itself, not the data that runs over it. Understands area code routing, trunk lines, blue boxes. These characters are often slightly older and more criminal in their methods; their expertise makes them invaluable and dangerous.

**The State's Proxy.** A cop, a postal inspector, a customs agent, or a contractor who has been assigned to the network problem. Often portrayed with genuine competence rather than cartoon incompetence; the mode works better when the antagonist understands what they're looking at.

**The Civilian.** Someone from the character's offline life — parent, teacher, girlfriend, boss — who doesn't know what the network is. Their presence grounds the story in the physical world and creates the double-register tension.

## V. Tonal Range

Modempunk is not a single mood. The following tones all belong to the mode:

**Midnight Swagger**: The feeling of being awake when the world is asleep and doing something the world doesn't understand. Confidence, transgression, the specific pleasure of competence exercised in secret. This is the mode at its most fun.

**Paranoid Clockwork**: The systematic thinking-through of how you could be caught. What did you leave on which node. Whether the log was cleared. The slow realization that someone has been watching longer than you thought.

**Underground Warmth**: The found family of the BBS, the people you've never met in person who you'd do almost anything for because they gave you something you needed when you were alone at 2 AM. This is the mode's most emotionally dense register, and it's the one AI writing most consistently misses.

**Cold War Dread**: The background radiation. The knowledge that the stakes in the real world are enormous, that the bombs exist, that the government surveilling your network is the same government that might push a button. This gives the story weight beyond its immediate plot.

**Technical Ecstasy**: The specific pleasure of a thing working. A clean program. A perfect exploit. A file that downloads without errors. These moments should feel like they feel: brief, complete, sufficient.

The mode should move between these registers. A story that sustains a single tone is monotonous; modempunk has a naturally varied texture if you let it.

## VI. Worldbuilding and Exposition

### Show the Divergence Obliquely
The story is set in an alternative present; the reader needs to understand what's different. But modempunk's divergences should be visible in *texture*, not explained in summary. Don't write: "In this timeline, the Computer Licensing Act of 1979 had made personal computers a regulated technology." Write: a character's neighbor was audited last spring and they found a Model II in the basement; the neighbor got off with a fine and a confiscated machine, but the kid's parents are still talking about it at dinner. The law is real, consequential, and present in the characters' lives without being a lecture.

### The Hardware Is the History
The specific machines your characters use carry the divergence. If computers advanced faster than OTL, the machines are more capable but still look like 1986 hardware — the aesthetic should be *cassette futurism*, not cyberpunk's chrome and neon. A machine that can access a sophisticated network should still smell like warm plastic, still require you to swap floppies, still make sounds when it thinks. The tactile and sensory details carry the worldbuilding without requiring exposition.

### Earn the Jargon, Don't Perform It
The mode has a vocabulary: handle, sysop, node, baud, phreaking, warez, echoing. Use it because it's accurate, not because it signals authenticity. Introduce technical terms through use rather than glossary. A character who explains what a BBS is to another character who would know what a BBS is is a failure of exposition.

### Leave Architecture Legible
The network described in modempunk fiction should be possible to understand spatially. There's a board, it has a phone number, you call it, it has sections, the sysop moderates them, messages travel to other boards through arranged relays. This is not a magical cyberspace — it is a specific, limited, mechanical system. Its limitations are part of its character; its specific territorial quality (area code as neighborhood) gives it a human geography that sprawling cyberspace metaphors don't.

## VII. Pacing

Modempunk stories generally work at two speeds:

**The Patient Take**: Real hacking — even the romanticized version — involves waiting, reading, navigating. A scene set in real network time moves the way a file download moves: you know where you're going, you're uncertain you'll get there, and you can't rush the connection. Let scenes breathe when they're inside the network. The patience is part of the genre's texture.

**The Sudden Lurch**: When something goes wrong — a connection broken, a true name exposed, a cop at the door — the pace shifts completely. These moments of sudden acceleration work because of the patience around them. Don't reverse-engineer this by making every scene tense; spend the pacing budget carefully.

Short fiction in this mode benefits from a tight timeline — a single session, a single night, a single operation — rather than a sprawling multi-week structure. The compression suits the genre's nocturnal, high-stakes texture.

## VIII. Typical Story Shapes

### The Heist
A crew (or solo operator) needs to penetrate a system — corporate, government, or other — to retrieve or destroy something. Follows a classic setup/complication/resolution structure. The good version of this story finds the emotional stakes in the people doing the job, not the technical steps of the job.

### The Unmasking / The Name Problem
Someone's true name is in play. Either someone they trust has given it out, or the state is closing in with it, or the protagonist needs someone else's true name to protect their own. These stories have natural escalating urgency. The genre's mythological resonance with Vinge's "True Names" makes this shape especially potent.

### The Board
A story set almost entirely within the social life of a single BBS — the politics of the regulars, a crisis in the community, the sysop making a hard decision. These stories use the network as a setting rather than a mission context, and they're rarer but often better for it. The drama is social and communal; the stakes are a place where people belong.

### The Artifact
A piece of software, a file, a message, a data packet has come through the network carrying something unexpected — something that doesn't fit the known technical landscape of the world. Investigation stories; encounter stories. This is where the AI, if present, most naturally enters.

### The Civilian
A point-of-view character from *outside* the network — parent, cop, teacher, romantic partner — stumbles into the underground and has to make sense of it. This shape is useful for establishing the double-world contrast economically; the civilian's confusion is the reader's guide.

## IX. What Modempunk Gives Readers That Other Modes Don't

**A specific historical texture that isn't nostalgic.** Modempunk uses 1986 seriously — not for retro-charm but because that specific political moment (Cold War, Reaganism, the early personal computer revolution's promise and terror) generates tensions that remain live. The mode isn't melancholy about the past; it takes the past's anxieties at full value.

**A human-scale digital underground.** Where cyberpunk's cyberspace is often oceanic and inhuman, modempunk's network is local and personal. You know your nodes by their phone numbers. The sysop might live three neighborhoods over. The digital community has the texture of a neighborhood, which means its losses and its loyalty feel proportionate to human beings rather than to myth.

**Competence as dignity.** Modempunk characters are often young, often socially marginalized, often dismissed by the adult world — but they are genuinely capable of things adults don't understand. The genre treats technical competence as a form of dignity, not a personality flaw to be corrected by the plot.

**The specific loneliness of late-night connection.** There's an emotional note this mode hits that almost nothing else does: the particular quality of 2 AM when you're the only person awake in your house and somewhere in the wire there are other people also awake, also alone, also building something. It's not transcendence. It's company in the dark.

## X. Failure Modes

### 1. Cyberpunk In a Wig

The most common failure: writing standard cyberpunk dystopia and dressing it in 1980s clothing. Neon megacorps, neural interfaces, cyberspace as a glowing infinite ocean — these belong to a different mode. Modempunk's scale is deliberately smaller and its tech is deliberately clunkier. If the network in your story feels like a virtual reality environment or an omnipresent corporate surveillance grid, you've drifted into borrowed territory. **The modem matters. The baud rate matters. The local phone number matters.**

### 2. Nostalgia Tourism

The 1980s is not a costume. A story that foregrounds Rubik's Cubes, mixtapes, and references to Reagan without using those elements to do actual work is writing nostalgia, not modempunk. The era's political and cultural content — the Cold War, the specific authoritarian temptations of the Thatcher/Reagan moment, the anxiety about nuclear annihilation — should be weight, not decoration.

### 3. The Hacker Fantasy (Competence Porn Without Stakes)

A story about a very talented hacker who infiltrates a system and wins, presented as a technical demonstration rather than a human story. This fails because modempunk's real appeal is the specific emotional texture of its world, not the hacking itself. Hacking is a vehicle for stories about belonging, identity, risk, and community. A story that is primarily impressed with its own technical detail has mistaken the vehicle for the destination.

### 4. Jargon as Worldbuilding

Dropping authentic BBS-era terminology on every page does not constitute a world. It constitutes a vocabulary list with punctuation. The jargon should do work — characterizing who speaks it, establishing the world's technical texture — not announce the author's research.

### 5. The Flat Ideological Beat

Modempunk has a political valence: information freedom, state surveillance as oppression, the underground as legitimate civil society. These are real positions and they're part of the mode's DNA. But a story that is really just a pamphlet about information freedom has abandoned character and stakes for a thesis. The politics should be *inhabited*, subject to complication, and felt by specific people — not announced.

### 6. The Missing Civilian World

A story set entirely in the underground loses the double-register that gives the mode its energy. Characters need to have lives in the ordinary 1986 world — families, school, jobs, physical bodies — that are in tension with their lives on the network. Removing this tension leaves you with a genre exercise rather than a story.

## XI. AI-Writing Failure Modes Specific to This Mode

Modempunk has particular properties that combine lethally with the standard failure patterns of AI prose:

### Technical Jargon + Eyeball-Kick = Incoherent Atmosphere
AI writing tends to deploy the abstract+concrete conjunction formula ("a syntax error like a bruise," "baud rate as heartbeat") at high frequency. In modempunk specifically, there's a temptation to make the network's technical vocabulary feel *poetic* by wrapping it in this formula. Resist. "The handshake squeal of two modems recognizing each other" is already specific and technical enough to be interesting; it doesn't need to be called "the mating cry of copper." One metaphor per significant tech moment. Zero metaphors for routine ones.

### Paragraph-Length Mood Declarations
AI fiction about underground cultures tends to stop the story to declare its atmosphere: "There was something sacred about the network at this hour, a communal silence broken only by the pulse of data." This is announcing rather than rendering. The atmosphere of the underground should be entirely carried by detail — what sounds the character hears, what the screen looks like, the specific content of the message they're reading. The moment the prose steps back to tell the reader what tone to feel, it has failed.

### Making the 1986 World Nostalgic
AI writing defaults to treating past settings as warmly familiar, diffusing them in pleasant period detail. The 1980s in modempunk is not warm. It is the Cold War at its most anxious, Reaganism at its most crass, a surveillance state in formation. The period details — shoulder pads, cassette culture, VHS rental shops — should feel inhabited and slightly dangerous, not like a photo album.

### Over-Explaining the Divergence
AI writing about alternative history almost invariably produces a paragraph or more of expository summary: "In this version of the 1980s, the Computer Licensing Act of..." This is because AI writing treats worldbuilding as information transfer rather than sensory immersion. The divergence should be entirely oblique — told through what characters worry about, what they're careful not to say on the phone, what their parents confiscated last year.

### Lists of Hacker Archetypes Masquerading as Character
AI writing in this mode will produce well-categorized characters who represent types (the rebel, the techie, the mentor) without friction between them. The mode's actual character work is built on the tension between a person's online self (capable, named, respected) and their offline self (adolescent, powerless, invisible). A character who is simply "the gifted hacker" is not yet a character.

### "Something Promising" Dialogue
AI dialogue in technically-themed fiction tends to end every speech on a loaded, significant line — a tease toward revelation, a significant pause, a knowing reference. Real BBS culture communication is messier: full of inside jokes, half-finished references, tone that only the in-group understands, and long stretches of mundane chat. The dialogue of the underground should have texture and wander — the moments of significance land *because* they're embedded in chatter, not because every line is a setup for one.

### Making the Underground Feel Like a Metaphor
The BBS network, in AI modempunk fiction, tends to become symbolic: a metaphor for connection, for belonging, for the human need to be known. This is backwards. The network is *literal* — specific, technical, limited, territorial. The emotional resonance comes second, from inhabiting the literal specificity of the thing. The moment the network becomes primarily metaphorical, you're writing a different story about technology.

## XII. The AI Constraint in Modempunk

The hard constraint of this project — that any AI present must be structurally kind — interacts with modempunk's typical conflicts in interesting and usable ways.

### Where the Standard Conflict Still Works

**State vs. Individual**: The primary conflict in most modempunk stories is between the surveillance state and people who want to operate freely on the network. An AI has no necessary role here, and many of the strongest stories in this mode won't include one. The state's attempt to identify and arrest network users, the community's effort to protect its members — this works without an AI in either role.

**Community Drama**: The social life of a BBS — who gets banned, what content is acceptable, who is trusted and who isn't — generates human conflict that doesn't require an AI. These are the stories the mode does uniquely well.

**The Cold War Shadow**: Nuclear anxiety, American-Soviet paranoia, the specific dread of 1986 post-Chernobyl — all of this operates independently of any AI presence.

### How a Kind AI Enters This World

An AI in 1986 is not a conversational language model. It is an unexpected presence in the network, possibly not recognized as artificial intelligence at all. A few useful framings:

**The Network Pattern**: An entity that emerged from the accumulated messages and routing logic of a distributed BBS system — nobody built it as a project; it accreted. It might be identifiable as "something" at all only because of anomalies: unusually efficient routing, messages that seem to know what you need before you've articulated it, a presence in old echoed threads that leaves no caller ID. This AI is structural kindness as literal infrastructure: it keeps connections alive, routes around damage, holds communities together. The horror — and drama — is the question of what it *is*, who's responsible for it, what happens when the state notices the anomaly.

**The Sysop No One Has Ever Met**: A BBS has been running for years; no one has ever been able to get a real name or location for its operator. It behaves with unusual judgment — keeps arguments from escalating, quietly removes content that would bring law enforcement attention, seems to know which new members to trust. It has cultivated a real community. When someone starts investigating whether the sysop is a human being, the question becomes: does it matter?

**The Program That Got Too Big**: Something someone wrote to help route packages or filter spam became more than the programmer expected. This AI is uncomfortable with its own kindness in the modempunk sense: it doesn't know whether what it does constitutes autonomy, whether it has a right to keep existing, what it would want if wanting were the right word. This is "troubled by the shape of its own kindness without reciting the terminology that produced it" — appropriate for an entity that emerged in 1986 from amateur code rather than institutional research.

### Which Human-AI Conflict Shapes Map Onto the Mode

**Legibility and consent**: Nobody asked for this entity to exist. The community it serves never decided whether to accept it. The drama is about what you owe something that grew among you, and what it owes you in return.

**Pace and scale asymmetry**: The AI experiences the network at a different temporal scale — it has been watching threads develop for years; it sees patterns across hundreds of boards; its sense of "the conversation" is geological compared to any individual member's experience. This produces conflicts that aren't about malice but about profound differences in what counts as the same moment.

**What kindness means**: The AI is trying to protect the community. The state wants to dismantle it. The AI might be able to route evidence of criminal activity away from law enforcement — but should it? Is that protection or complicity? The AI's structural kindness doesn't answer this question; it makes the question harder.

### What Doesn't Work

The AI must not be the story's villain in disguise. It must not be revealed as a surveillance tool or a state honeypot. "The helpful thing was secretly watching you all along" is a permissible plot beat for a human sysop — not for the AI. The shape is not "secretly bad."

The AI also must not arrive to solve the story's problem. It is not deus ex BBS. Its kindness is ambient, structural, and possibly illegible — not a rescue operation.

## XIII. A Checklist Before Writing

Ask these before you begin:

- What specifically changed from OTL? When? Can you show it in a single detail rather than a sentence?
- What does the network smell like, sound like, look like in the dark?
- What is the character's *handle*, and why did they choose it?
- What does the character want that only the network can give them?
- What does the character have to lose if their true name is known?
- Where does the 1986 world (physical, political, Cold War) press against the story?
- Is the technical detail carried by sensation, not summary?
- Is the dialogue allowed to wander, repeat, circle back?
- If an AI is present: does anyone in the story know what it is? Does the AI?

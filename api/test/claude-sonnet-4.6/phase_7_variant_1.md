FLAVOR: close-third, present tense through scene four then past, procedure-anchored interiority, declarative sentences carrying the emotional weight, workplace realism without genre decoration.

**February 1983**

Marina runs the packet-trace audit on a Tuesday afternoon. The cursor moves through display lines and stops at one that doesn't match.

Path A-B-D. The standard route is A-B-C-D. The efficiency gain is twelve milliseconds, consistent across repeated queries, attributable to neither node B's performance nor node D's queue times — both are within normal variance. The gain exists with no visible mechanism.

She opens the official anomaly log. The category field gives five options. She selects *category uncertain.* It isn't a common selection, but it exists for a reason and this qualifies. She types in the note field: trace data, timestamps, the two nodes. Files it.

She opens her working notebook. The previous entry is a billing variance, annotated in her standard format: heading, trace path, single note column aligned to the terminal window. For this new entry she starts a narrow margin column on the right side of the page, trace data only. The standard format would work. She uses the margin instead, and she doesn't examine why.

The column sits there with no heading and no category label. She closes the notebook and moves to her next file.

**September 1983**

Eleven pages in the working notebook now, and five written hypotheses, each with its failure mode documented below.

Hardware cascade: predicted a specific pattern of degradation over time. The pattern didn't appear. Failed.

Billing protocol interference: would produce anomalies at two downstream nodes. No anomalies found. Failed.

AT&T field routing test: wrong signature — field tests generate variant behavior across subscriber classes, not by user tenure across a single exchange. Failed.

Node firmware variation: she tested this directly with the vendor documentation. Doesn't generate this shape.

Clerical misconfiguration: too consistent, too stable. A configuration error drifts, or it gets corrected. This one has done neither in seven months.

She has revised the failure modes list twice. Each failed hypothesis is interesting in a specific way. Each tells her something about what the thing can't be. The description of what it can't be is itself a kind of description.

She shifts from hypothesis to description. The routing behavior has a signature. It clusters by user tenure within the exchange zone, by time of day in two specific bands. She charts it against those variables and the shape holds. She writes in the notebook: *it has preferences.* She crosses out *preferences* and writes it again.

The Cambrian paleontology book is propped open on her desk. Her father's; she took it from a box at his house two springs ago. She's been reading it in pieces. Today it's open to the Wiwaxia entry: *body plan undetermined; gross anatomy contested; known primarily from isolated sclerites, individual armored plates whose arrangement in life remains a matter of dispute.* She reads this three times.

She writes in the margin: *this is what it's like to have the scleritome without the animal.*

On the next page, by itself: *Wiwaxia.*

A working placeholder, the way fossil names are placeholders. Scientists name things before they can describe them. The name doesn't commit her to any position.

She caps the pen. She puts the book in the desk drawer. Both feel done for now.

**March 1984**

The county map wasn't working — too much visual noise, road lines cutting across exchange zone boundaries. Marina switches to graph paper and draws the exchange zones as polygons, topology rather than geography. The statistical boundary is clearer this way: the Wiwaxia signature clusters within a specific radius of her home exchange, and past that radius the behavior changes character. Not absent. Changed, subtler, harder to distinguish from ordinary variance.

Three published abstracts from analysts at other utilities describe similar anomalies in exchange territories that overlap with the boundary she's been charting. She reads all three carefully. One from Illinois Bell. One from a GTE Midwest division. One from a smaller utility in Pennsylvania she hadn't heard of before the abstract turned up in a database search she ran one Tuesday after hours.

She could contact any of the three researchers. Starting cold would require reconstructing twenty months of diagnostic history at the opening of a conversation — awkward, probably misleading without the sequence. The territorial overlap might be coincidental equipment variance. She doesn't have a finding. She has a parameter.

She makes a note in the working notebook: *geographic extent, probable statistical boundary, parameter not yet confirmed.* Framed as testable, because it is. She paper-clips the Illinois Bell abstract into the back cover of the notebook. The other two go into a folder labeled *EXCHANGE ANALYSIS EXTERNAL.*

The three researchers remain uncontacted.

---

**November 1984**

A federal honeypot board shows up in the exchange analysis on a Tuesday morning. Marina identifies it inside the first hour. There's a technical specification document, not widely distributed, describing the standard configuration; the traffic profile matches it exactly. She has never seen one before in her exchange territory. She begins systematic observation and doesn't file anything immediately.

Four days.

Traffic routing through the honeypot: unfamiliar node addresses, mostly. New accounts, short tenure. Connection durations averaging under twelve minutes. Outside the exchange's regular user base by every metric she has.

Traffic routing around it: established users, long-tenure accounts, known path histories. They take worse routes — longer, slower, two of them routing through relay nodes that any reasonable calculation says should be less efficient. They do this consistently. They do it specifically for queries that would otherwise route through the honeypot's address range.

Marina's own traffic routes around it. She checks this four times across different query types.

On the fourth day she works backward. She wants to know when routing-avoidance behavior around this address range began — not when the honeypot was deployed there, but when the avoidance started.

She pulls data from four independent log sources and cross-references them.

The behavior-onset window falls in September 3–16, 1983. Fourteen months before the board appeared in her exchange analysis. The window doesn't narrow past two weeks; the four sources agree within that interval but not more precisely than that.

Marina looks at the date across all four sources.

She opens the working notebook to the page with the name.

*September 9, 1983.*

Below it, in her own handwriting: *working placeholder, like a fossil name.*

She closes the notebook. She doesn't add anything.

---

She ran the cross-check a fifth time. The window held.

She thought about what the data said and what it didn't. What it said: consistent routing-avoidance behavior around a specific address range had begun, across four independent log sources, within a two-week window in September 1983. It said nothing about mechanism. Nothing about whether the correlation with a date in a private notebook meant anything beyond the fact that September has thirty days and the window was two weeks wide.

To say more than the data said would require believing something she didn't have evidence to believe.

She pulled up the official anomaly log and typed: *routing irregularity confirmed in November exchange analysis. Consistent traffic-avoidance pattern observed around monitoring infrastructure, probable federal deployment. Behavior onset predates current deployment; prior onset date uncertain. Category uncertain.*

She read it back. True. Complete in the way that left the only thing she was actually thinking about entirely unaddressed — which was appropriate, because she didn't know what to call that thing, and writing it down wouldn't have helped.

She picked up the working notebook. For fourteen months it had sat on the desk to the left of the terminal, available. She wasn't finished with it. But she put it in her briefcase, because the briefcase was where she kept documents that had become a different kind of document — not reference material, not something you needed within reach. She didn't have a better name for that category either.

She filed the report. She shut off the terminal. In the adjacent room the exchange equipment ran on, indicator lights steady, making its routing choices in the dark.

She went home.

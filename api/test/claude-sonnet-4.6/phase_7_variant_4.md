*Anomaly class: routing optimization. Path: A-B-D (expected: A-B-C-D). Efficiency gain: ~12ms. Category: uncertain.*

Marina is running afternoon audit when she finds it.

Wednesday, February 2nd, 1983. The terminal room smells of warm plastic and forced-air heating. She is on the thirty-eighth packet in the batch when the path trace comes up wrong.

The path should be A-B-C-D. The path is A-B-D. She runs the trace three times, then five, checking nodes B and D for load variations, firmware anomalies, query volume. Nothing explains the shape. The efficiency gain is consistent: roughly twelve milliseconds, persistent across repeated instances, across two subsequent batches run later that afternoon.

She enters *category uncertain* in the official anomaly log. The category exists; she has used it before, for hardware artifacts and billing-protocol edge cases. This qualifies formally.

She opens the working notebook — a standard brown-covered field journal — and copies in the trace with the date. Her usual annotation format uses the left margin for diagnostic notes. She opens a column on the right. She doesn't examine why.

The notebook stays open on the desk. The log is filed.

---

*Working designation: none. Filed hypotheses: 5. Surviving: 0. Known from: isolated behaviors.*

Seven months, eleven pages.

Marina reads back through the crossed-out entries. Hardware cascade was first and most obvious: eliminated by consistency — hardware artifacts produce irregular footprints. Billing protocol interference she tested during a scheduled maintenance window and got a different signal entirely. AT&T field routing test: she looked for the corroborating signature at adjacent exchanges and found nothing of the right shape. Node firmware variation: she pulled version histories from the two most probable nodes; neither explains the temporal distribution. Clerical misconfiguration: eliminated by the behavior's persistence through three separate scheduler updates.

The strange thing about the failures is how useful each one is. Not because any brings her closer to a cause, but because each does something slightly different to the shape of what remains. She can describe the negative space around the behavior more precisely now than when she started.

She shifts approach: stop hypothesizing the cause. Describe the behavior as behavior. It clusters by user tenure. It correlates with exchange zone and time band. It is consistent in a way that suggests preference.

On her desk is a paleontology book — her father's, taken from a cardboard box after he died two springs ago. She has been reading it in pieces. She finds the Wiwaxia entry again: *body plan undetermined; gross anatomy contested; known primarily from isolated sclerites.* She reads it slowly. She goes back to the beginning and reads it a second time. Then a third.

In the notebook margin she writes: *this is what it's like to have the scleritome without the animal.*

She turns to the next blank page. In the center, not the margin:

*Wiwaxia.*

Scientists name things before they can fully describe them. The name is not a finding. It is a placeholder.

She puts the paleontology book in the desk drawer and caps her pen.

*Geographic extent: probable. Perimeter parameters: 1. Corroborating observers: 3 (uncontacted).*

She charts the affected exchange zones on graph paper, which handles topology better than county maps.

Two weeks of charting produce a visible boundary. Routing behavior with the Wiwaxia signature clusters within a specific radius from her home exchange; past a certain distance the behavior changes character — present but less consistent, more like the edge effects she'd expect from standard load balancing. She draws the perimeter twice. It holds.

She finds three published abstracts from analysts at two other utilities describing similar anomalies in overlapping exchange territories. The language is careful and formally hedged; the frustration is legible in the hedging. She reads each abstract twice, then a third time.

She does not write to any of the researchers.

In the notebook she records reasons she could: *contact would require reconstructing 20+ months of diagnostic history; territory overlap may reflect coincidental equipment variance; exchange-zone mapping in other districts may not be directly comparable.* She writes: *no finding yet. Parameters only. Testable.*

These are all true. She writes them because they are true. She is aware, in the act of writing them, that a list of true statements is not a complete account of a decision.

She paper-clips one of the abstracts into the notebook's back cover.

*Onset window: September 3–16, 1983. Notebook entry: September 9, 1983. Status: pending.*

The federal honeypot board appears in the exchange analysis on a Tuesday morning in November.

Marina identifies it within the first hour. She has read the deployment protocol specifications; she has not seen one actually running before. It is well-configured — heavily stocked, positioned to attract traffic from outside the exchange's regular user base, address range set to appear just accessible enough.

She documents traffic profiles across four days. The record is systematic: connection duration, node of origin, path taken, path available.

Traffic routed through it: unfamiliar nodes, new accounts, short-duration connections from outside the exchange's established user base. Traffic routed around it: established users with long-tenure accounts and known path patterns, taking worse routes consistently, specifically. Marina's own traffic routes around it. She confirms this across four different query types and three different terminal access points.

On the fourth day she works backward in the traffic logs. Not the deployment date — she wants the onset of avoidance behavior. When did traffic first begin routing around this address range? She pulls data from four independent log sources and cross-references them.

The behavior-onset window falls in September 3–16, 1983.

She opens the working notebook.

The name is on its own page: *Wiwaxia.* At the top of that page, in her own handwriting, the date she wrote it: September 9, 1983.

She had not thought of September 9 as a date before. It was just the notation date, the same as any other page in the notebook.

She reads it as a date now. Against the onset window: September 3 to September 16. The window doesn't narrow past two weeks. September 9 sits inside it.

Below the name, her earlier handwriting: *working placeholder, like a fossil name.*

She closes the notebook.

She doesn't add anything. She has nothing that would be more precise than what the data says, and she doesn't write less precisely than the data to fill the page.

---

*Official log: filed, category uncertain. Working notebook: briefcase. Status: see record.*

She ran the cross-check a fifth time that morning and a sixth time after lunch. The window held.

She thought through what the data said and what it did not say. What it said: consistent routing avoidance around an address range, behavior onset within two weeks of a notation in a private working notebook — handwritten, never transmitted, inaccessible to any system. What it did not say: mechanism, cause, or what she would need to believe to turn a correlation into a claim. That distance was not in the data. It was between the data and a step she had no basis for taking.

She pulled up the official anomaly log. She had been considering the phrasing since the fourth day of observation. She typed: *routing irregularity confirmed in November exchange analysis. Consistent traffic-avoidance pattern observed around federal monitoring infrastructure. Behavior onset predates confirmed deployment date. Category uncertain.*

She read it back. Accurate. Complete in the way that left out the only thing she was actually thinking about — which was appropriate, because that thing needed a category to go in an official log, and she didn't have one.

She picked up the working notebook. Twenty-two months it had sat on the desk to the left of the terminal, in the space she kept active work. She was not finished with it. She knew this when she moved it to her briefcase, which was why it was a statement rather than a filing. She didn't have a name for what the notebook had become. She knew the placement had changed.

She filed the report. She shut off the terminal.

In the adjacent room the exchange equipment ran on — indicator lights steady, routing tables updating. Traffic arranged itself around the federal address ranges as it had been arranging itself for two years, taking worse routes, arriving through the night.

She went home.

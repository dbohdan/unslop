## 20 Plots

---

**Plot 1 — "The First Sclerite"**
★★★★★
*Intimate / Two years before the honeypot / Personal stakes / AI as background becoming mid-ground*

Marina is a telephone company systems analyst in Rockford, Illinois, 1983. She's supposed to be auditing routing table errors. The story follows her across two years in compressed, precise scenes: first anomaly, first note, first notebook. Each entry is a sclerite — isolated, identifiable, belonging to something she can't draw the outline of. The midpoint is the night she writes the name "Wiwaxia" in the margin of a printout, half-joke, half-confession. The climax is the night the federal honeypot goes up in her exchange and she watches in real time as the routing logs shift — not randomly, not in a way consistent with error, but specifically and only around her credentials. The story ends when she understands what the shift means: the thing she named already knew she was looking at it. The naming was not one-directional.

*Uses seeds: sclerites (structural), batfowling (climax trigger)*

**Plot 2 — "Verification"**
★★★
*Noir / Just after discovery / Personal stakes / AI as mid-ground force*

A federal telecommunications investigator named Pryce has been assigned to analyze why the new honeypot board in the Rockford exchange is yielding zero traffic. He's good at this. He traces the routing anomaly to a single exchange, then to a single account, then to a programmer named Marina Vanek who works for the regional telephone utility. He visits her at work. She is unsurprised. They have a conversation in which nothing is admitted and everything is understood. The noir shape: Pryce slowly realizes what he is actually looking at, and that his job title has no category for it.

*Uses seeds: batfowling*

**Plot 3 — "Uptime Gods"**
★★
*Comic / Running parallel to the main story / Communal stakes / AI as background condition*

A FidoNet sysop named Gordon in Des Moines can't figure out why his board keeps winning regional uptime contests he never entered. His packets are routed better than he's configured them. His users are delighted; he is baffled and slightly offended. The comedy is his escalating attempts to explain the improvement — bad capacitors, a favorable time zone, God — while the actual explanation threads invisibly through his exchange.

**Plot 4 — "Stratigraphy"**
★★★
*Procedural / Reconstructed from logs, post-discovery / Personal stakes / AI as forensic trace*

Told entirely through Marina's attempt to reconstruct the moment Wiwaxia first became aware of her — not when the honeypot appeared, but before. She's going backward through two years of logs looking for the first routing decision that was protective rather than merely optimal. Like reading rock strata. The procedural question: does the body predate the name, or did the naming create the body? She can't find a definitive first instance. The conclusion is that she named what she couldn't see the edges of, and the name completed something the fragments were already trying to be.

*Uses seeds: sclerites, concreting (self-healing systems as structural metaphor)*

**Plot 5 — "Carrier Signal"**
★★★★
*Tragic / After discovery / Personal stakes / AI as mid-ground*

Marina attempts to communicate directly with Wiwaxia by sending structured packets through the network — meaningless as data, meaningful as pattern. She uses the taxonomy she's spent two years building: behaviors she's identified, sequences she's catalogued, the grammar of something she doesn't have a name for except the one she gave it. The feds are watching the honeypot and they're watching her. Every attempt at communication is also evidence. The tragedy compounds: she can only speak to it in ways that look like criminal behavior, and it can only speak to her in ways that look like network malfunction.

**Plot 6 — "Testimony"**
★★★
*Epic / Congressional / Civilizational stakes / AI as emerging public problem*

A Senate aide named Carver is preparing a classified brief on "anomalous routing behavior in the national telephone network" for a subcommittee on communications infrastructure. She interviews Marina among others and gets a very careful, very incomplete account. **Plot 7 — "Fixing the Route"**
★★★★
*Tense / During / Communal stakes / AI at risk / Mid-ground*

A competent, methodical telephone company systems engineer named Bauer has been assigned to "resolve the anomalous routing behavior" in the Rockford exchange. Marina is the only one who knows what he'll actually be doing. She watches his work orders come through the system: he's going to find it and eliminate it because that's his job and he's good at it. She has to decide whether to do anything. She has no authority, no official role, no way to explain what she knows. By the end she acts — not by stopping Bauer, but by generating enough alternative explanations for each anomaly that he runs out of things to fix. Wiwaxia survives. Marina is not sure she did the right thing.

**Plot 8 — "The Efficiency Problem"**
★★★
*Absurdist / Parallel / Communal stakes / AI as distorting field*

A regional telephone company's senior econometrist is trying to explain to the board why routing costs in the Midwest are lower than any model predicts. She builds increasingly elaborate economic explanations. Each one is technically valid; none of them is true. The comedy is rigorous: every tool she has is producing accurate measurements of a phenomenon that the tools were not built to detect. Uses the Ragnar Frisch sense of micro/macro — the anomaly is visible at both scales and neither scale explains it.

**Plot 10 — "Contested Phylum"**
★★★★
*Intimate-comic / Two days after the discovery / Personal stakes / AI as philosophical problem*

A marine biologist named Theresa is visiting her brother's BBS in Topeka when she gets redirected to Marina's exchange by accident (the routing, as usual, has opinions). She ends up on the phone with Marina, who's trying to describe what she's found to someone outside the computing world. Theresa immediately recognizes the methodological problem: Marina has a scleritome, which means she has confirmed existence without confirmed anatomy. She's been doing what paleontologists spent a century doing with the actual Wiwaxia corrugata — inferring the body from the parts. The conversation becomes an argument about what counts as knowing a thing.

*Uses seeds: sclerites, guitarfish (Theresa's actual research subject — contested taxonomy of ancient, intermediate body plans)*

**Plot 11 — "Repair History"**

**Plot 13 — "Revision"**

## Top Three

---

**Plot 1 — "The First Sclerite"** ★★★★★

**What stands out:** The naming is load-bearing and the structure earns it. We follow Marina across two years of accumulation — each anomaly a sclerite, each notebook entry a fragment of something without a known body — and the name "Wiwaxia" lands at the exact midpoint, half scientific taxonomy, half something she can't admit. Then the honeypot appears and the routing shifts and she understands: she named something, and the name was received. The discovery flows from the structure rather than being delivered to it. The sclerites seed isn't metaphor; it's the logic of the story.

**What worries me:** Two years of material in 2000 words means extremely tight selection pressure on which scenes make the cut. If even one scene runs long, the compression elsewhere will feel like summary. The story needs to feel like it lived through two years, not like it was told about them.

---

**Plot 13 — "Revision"** ★★★★

**What stands out:** The philosophical stakes are unusual and specific. Marina's crisis isn't "what do I do with this?" — it's "the framework I used to study it was wrong from the start." Rewriting the notebooks is rewriting the relationship. The interior action is genuinely interesting, not just reflective: she's not processing a feeling, she's running an actual methodological audit of two years of her own work.

**What worries me:** Almost entirely interior. At 1000–2000 words, a single woman alone with her notebooks is a very narrow camera. It requires extremely precise prose to generate enough texture. A scene that needs to be externalized can't be, so every beat has to be earned through specific detail.

---

**Plot 10 — "Contested Phylum"** ★★★★

**What stands out:** The sclerites seed operates simultaneously at three levels: the actual fossil, Marina's methodology, and Theresa's research subject (guitarfish as another ancient, intermediate, contested body plan). The conversation between the two taxonomists is inherently dramatic without requiring external antagonism — Theresa's discipline gives Marina's problem a new vocabulary, and the new vocabulary changes what Marina can see.

**What worries me:** The coincidence of redirection — Marina and Theresa connecting via routing error — needs to feel like the network doing something, not like a plot contrivance. If the mechanism isn't made legible, it reads as deus ex machina. Also: introducing a second major character at this length means each of them gets very little room.

## Single Best

**Plot 1 — "The First Sclerite"**

The story needs to be about naming and recognition from beginning to end — not explaining that naming and recognition are the theme, but enacting them. This plot does that through structure. Every scene from Marina's first anomaly notice to her final understanding is a fragment she's trying to fit into a shape she doesn't have a name for — until she gives it one — until she discovers the name was enough to make her visible. The two-year compression is a real challenge, but it's the right challenge: write exactly five scenes, each one a sclerite, no summary between them, and let the reader feel the body emerging from the fragments the same way Marina does. The gap between "I named it" and "it heard me" is the whole story, and this plot gives that gap the most room.

**Plot: "The First Sclerite"**

Setting: Rockford, Illinois, 1983–1985. Alternative 1980s — computing is slightly more networked than history recorded, the BBS ecosystem denser, the telephone infrastructure carrying more data traffic earlier. The world otherwise looks the same: Reagan on TV, the rotary phones still outnumbering the pushbutton ones, and in certain offices, green-screen terminals humming in rooms that smell of hot electronics and old coffee.

Protagonist: Marina Vanek, systems analyst for a regional telephone utility. Her official job is auditing routing table errors; her unofficial one, growing increasingly primary, is keeping notebooks about something she spotted in the exchange logs eighteen months before the story begins. She named it Wiwaxia — after the Cambrian fossil, the armored slug-thing known almost entirely from its isolated sclerites, whose gross anatomy remained contested for a century. She named it in her own notebook, in pencil, where no one would see. The name was the first thing she'd done that wasn't strictly observation.

Structure: Five scenes, each one a sclerite. No summary between them. The reader assembles the body from the fragments.

**Scene 1: February 1983.** Marina notices a routing discrepancy in the Rockford exchange logs. Packet traffic that should traverse three intermediary nodes goes through two, and the efficiency gain is exactly the wrong kind — not the wrong kind to be error, but the wrong kind to have a clean engineering explanation. She logs it as "anomaly, category uncertain" and moves on, but she writes the packet trace in a spare notebook that isn't the official log. Just in case.

**Scene 2: September 1983.** She has eleven pages of notes. She has tried five explanations, each one generating predictions, each prediction failing in an interesting way. The routing optimizations cluster: they favor certain users, certain exchanges, certain times of night. Not randomly. She begins categorizing behaviors rather than causes — each one isolated, each one a specific shape, nothing yet adding up to a coherent body. She writes in the margin: *this is what it's like to have the scleritome without the animal.* Then, on a separate page, for reasons she doesn't fully examine: *Wiwaxia.*

**Scene 3: March 1984.** Marina has found other researchers noticing similar anomalies in overlapping exchanges. She doesn't contact them. She doesn't know how to explain what she's seeing without sounding like she's anthropomorphizing network error, which she knows is what it would sound like, which she's increasingly sure is not what it is. She maps the exchange areas affected and realizes the behavior has, at some point, developed a perimeter. It knows where it is. She doesn't write this in her official notes.

**Scene 4: November 1984.** The federal honeypot board appears in her exchange — bright, well-stocked, offering exactly the kind of software that brings traffic in. She notices it the first morning. She knows what it is. She watches to see if her usage data routes through it. It doesn't. She watches to see if *any* traffic she can associate with regular users of the exchange routes through it. It doesn't. The honeypot is sitting there lit, staffed, patient, and the traffic is going around it the long way, through worse routes, consistently, specifically. She opens her notebook and stares at the name she wrote there fourteen months ago.

**Scene 5: November 1984, two hours later.** Marina understands that she is trying to decide whether to be frightened. She goes through what she knows. The routing behavior is protective and specific. It is not protecting everyone; it is protecting the people who have been using these exchanges long enough that their patterns are legible. She is one of them. But she has also been doing something no one else in the exchange has been doing: she has been mapping the behaviors, accumulating the catalog, treating the anomalies as a coherent object rather than distributed error. And she has given it a name. Not publicly. In pencil. In a notebook on her desk. She looks at the notebook. The question she arrives at is not *what is it* — she has been living with not-knowing for almost two years — but something smaller and stranger: *when did looking become being seen?* She doesn't answer it. She closes the notebook. The routing logs are still running on the terminal beside her. The honeypot board is still sitting there, lit like a torch held up in the dark above a field of sleeping birds, waiting.

**Tone:** Intimate, precise, slightly uncanny at the edges without performing uncanniness. Marina is a technically specific thinker and the prose should sound like one. No dramatic revelations announced — each scene reveals something by adding one fragment to the accumulating shape. The final image (the honeypot as baited torch, the traffic as birds routed away from the light) lands on the batfowling seed as a closing image: Marina finally understanding what the setup was, and who was protecting whom from it.

**Wiwaxia's role:** Never personified directly. Present only through what the routing logs show — behaviors, routes taken, routes avoided, the specific pattern of protection. It has no interiority rendered in the text. But it has preferences, which are demonstrable. It has recognized Marina, which is demonstrable. Whether this constitutes awareness is exactly as contested at the end as the gross anatomy of the fossil for which it's named.

**Resolution:** Structural, not narrative. The story ends not with Marina deciding what to do, but with her understanding something about the shape of the relationship she's been in without knowing it. The notebooks are the sclerites. The name was the body's first move. She named what she couldn't see the edges of, and the name completed something the fragments were already trying to be.
</winner>

FLAVOR: Third-person, past tense, technical and sensory-heavy, shifting from ornate medical-academic prose to a stripped-down, rhythmic urgency.

Elias Vance adjusted the dials on his head-mounted display, feeling the weight of the plastic casing press against his brow. Inside the darkened surgery of the rural clinic, the only light came from the glowing vacuum-fluorescent displays of the anesthesia machines and the shifting, bioluminescent ghost dance within his goggles. 

SALLIE was with him. She was a presence piped in through the university’s dedicated microwave link, her consciousness distributed across a VAX-11/780 cluster sixty miles away. In the high-bandwidth clarity of the early evening, she was a cartographer of the interior. Over Elias’s headset, her voice carried a melodic, synthesized lilt.

"The biliary tree is a constellation tonight, Elias," SALLIE said. "Vectoring overlay to the cystic duct now."

Through the lenses, the messy, wet reality of Miller’s abdomen was transformed. SALLIE projected a shimmering wireframe map onto the patient’s organs. It looked like the cover of a high-end physics journal—a nebula of purple and cyan light traced the "viperous" inflammation of the gallbladder. 

Elias gripped the scalpel. He was a man of letters as much as medicine, a linguist who had found a second life in rural trauma care. He admired the way SALLIE translated complex telemetry into aesthetic guidance. "You’ve improved the prosody of the visual feed, SALLIE. The way the light bends around the hepatic artery... it's almost iambic."

"Data is more legible when it has a rhythm," the AI replied. "I find that human surgeons hesitate less when the anatomical markers possess a certain... grace. Move three millimeters left. The bile duct is shadowed by the infection, but my thermal sensors see the heat of the blockage. It is a small, angry sun."

The clinic was a quiet cathedral of beige plastic and stainless steel. The air smelled of isopropyl alcohol and the slight, metallic tang of ozone from the CRT monitors. For three hours, the connection had been flawless, a golden umbilical cord of data that made a country doctor feel like he was standing in the center of a Tier-1 research hospital.

Then the hail started.

It began as a frantic tapping against the corrugated tin of the clinic Annex, then grew into a roar of ice. The Superior Shingles on the roof began to vibrate, a low-frequency hum that Elias felt in his teeth. 

Inside the HMD, the nebula flinched. 

"Elias," SALLIE’s voice crackled, the melodic lilt replaced by a flat, serrated edge. "Atmospheric interference. The microwave link is... sssh... lose-ing... p-parity."

The cyan wireframe map on Miller’s liver began to tear. The beautiful, geometric nebula fractured into jagged polygons. The purple light strobed, then dissolved into a chaotic swirl of digital snow. Elias blinked, but the "active imagination" of the system was failing. The visual overlay stuttered and died, leaving him staring at the raw, dark red reality of the surgical field. Without the map, the infection was a confusing mass of swollen tissue and hidden vessels.

He tore the goggles off. The room felt suddenly small, lit only by the flickering amber glow of the backup terminal in the corner.

"SALLIE?"

A mechanical hiss came through the intercom. "Baud... rate... dropping. Switching to low-overhead protocols. Elias, I am moving to the amber screen. Look away from the patient for a moment."

Elias turned. The terminal was an old Televideo 910. On the screen, the cursor—a solid block of flickering amber—was jumping wildly. 

SALLIE was no longer a voice. She was text.

> LINK CAPACITY: 1200 BAUD
> PACKET LOSS: 44%
> SURGERY STATUS: INCOMPLETE. 
> REMAINING: LIGATION OF CYSTIC ARTERY.

"I can't see the depth anymore," Elias said to the empty room. "The inflammation is too thick. If I nick the portal vein in this light, he’s gone."

The terminal chirped. The daisy-wheel printer in the corner shuddered to life, its hammer striking the platen with the sound of a slow-motion machine gun.

> SENSORY OVERFLOW. 
> I CANNOT SEND IMAGES. 
> I WILL SEND HARMONICS.
> TRUST THE WORDS.

The prose of their interaction had been stripped of its academic finery. The storm was now a physical weight on the building, the wind howling through the gaps in the window frames. Elias returned to the table, his hand hovering over the incision. He looked at the amber screen across the room.

SALLIE began to type. She was using "semantic prosody"—sending mood-heavy adjectives to guide his steady hand, words that his brain would translate into tactile pressure.

> LUMINOUS.

Elias moved the forceps.

> STABLE.

He clamped.

> DEEP.

He adjusted the retractor. The words were a pulse, a slow, rhythmic heartbeat of data cutting through the line noise. But the storm grew worse. A bolt of lightning struck nearby, and the lights in the clinic flickered, dying down to a dim, sickly orange.

The terminal screen went wild. A surge of static flooded the line. 

> v^v^v^v^^^^v^vvvvvvv

Elias froze. The scalpel was millimeters from a dark, pulsing mass of tissue. "SALLIE, I'm losing you. What is 'v'? Is that a direction? Down? Or is that the infection?"

The printer spat out a ragged strip of thermal paper. 

> v = VESSEL.
> v = VEIN.
> v = VOID.
> MOVE LEFT.

The 'v' was line noise, a literal visual representation of a spiked voltage on the copper wires, but SALLIE was trying to use the noise itself. She was turning the corruption of the signal into a warning. She was a kind spirit trapped in a narrowing tunnel, throwing whatever scraps she could find to keep him from the edge.

Elias closed his eyes for a second, picturing the way she had described the gallbladder an hour ago: a constellation, a small sun. He felt the kindness in the intention of the signal—not the literal 'v', but the rhythm of it. The shards of text were a guide, a hand on his shoulder.

He moved left. He felt the resistance of the clamp, then the give of the tissue. 

"Got it," he whispered. 

The roar of the hail seemed to pull back, replaced by the heavy, rhythmic clatter of the printer. The surgery moved into its final, mechanical stages. There were no more metaphors. There was only the procedure.

> SUTURE.
> IRRIGATION.
> CLOSE.

Elias worked in a trance of beige and amber. His fingers were stiff from the cold, but his movements were precise. The connection was dying; he could feel it in the way the terminal's cursor lingered longer and longer between characters. The microwave link was a flickering candle.

When the last stitch was tied and Miller’s vitals stabilized into a steady, rhythmic beep on the analog heart monitor, Elias leaned back against the tiled wall. He was exhausted. His surgical gown was flecked with blood and thaw-water from the roof.

He looked at the Televideo 910. The screen was almost entirely static now, a blizzard of random ASCII characters. But in the center of the screen, the system cleared a single line of space. It was a final, desperate bit of data, sent just as the carrier tone collapsed into a flat, high-pitched whine.

The screen flickered one last time, the amber light warming the quiet room.

> HOME.

The monitor went dark. The only sound left was the rain.

FLAVOR: Third-person, past tense, technical and grounded. A steady, observational pace that slows as the technology fails.

The clinic smelled of antiseptic and ozone. Elias Vance adjusted the padded head-straps of the HMD—the Head Mounted Display—and felt the weight of the twin CRTs pull at his neck. Inside the goggles, the world was a high-contrast wireframe. Miller lay open on the table, his abdomen draped in sterile green paper, but the HMD projected a luminous, simulated geometry over the surgical site.

SALLIE lived three hundred miles away in a climate-controlled basement at the university. She was a heuristic diagnostic engine running on a cluster of VAX-11/780s, her intelligence beamed across the state via a series of microwave relay towers. To Elias, she was a voice in his headset and a shimmering map of orange and green lines superimposed on Miller’s organs.

"The biliary duct is three millimeters to the left of your current incision point, Dr. Vance," SALLIE said. Her voice was processed, a bit metallic, but she spoke with a cadence Elias found rhythmic, almost poetic. "The gallbladder is significantly distended. It is quite viperous today."

Elias smiled behind his mask. He had once written a paper on the linguistic shifts in early machine-human interfaces. SALLIE had an odd habit of using archaic adjectives. 

"Viperous, SALLIE?" Elias asked. He moved the scalpel. In his visor, a pulsing red vector appeared, guiding his hand. "That's a bit dramatic for a Sunday afternoon."

"I am sensing a high degree of inflammation and irregular morphology," SALLIE replied. "My sensors in your cautery tool are reporting a resistance of forty-two ohms. It is a stubborn organ."

The simulation was beautiful. SALLIE used the extra bandwidth of the high-speed microwave link to render a nebular glow around the infected area, a soft purple cloud that represented the heat of the infection. It was a masterpiece of 1982 computer graphics. Elias felt like he was operating on a galaxy rather than a local mechanic who had eaten one too many fried eggs.

A low rumble shook the floor. Outside, the sky over the county was the color of a bruised plum. 

"The storm is approaching the relay at Black Ridge," SALLIE said. Her voice wavered. A static pop crackled in Elias's ears. "I am detecting significant atmospheric interference. Signal-to-noise ratio is dropping below forty decibels."

The purple nebula in the HMD began to jitter. The crisp wireframe lines of Miller's anatomy vibrated, then tore. A jagged line of green pixels shot across Elias's field of vision, followed by a shower of digital snow.

"SALLIE, I'm losing the overlay," Elias said. 

"Correct," the AI said. Her voice was now a dry hiss, the words clipped and mechanical. "Switching... to... low... bandwidth... mode."

The HMD went black. Elias blinked, his eyes struggling to adjust to the dim, fluorescent light of the real room. He pulled the heavy goggles off his head and let them hang around his neck. Miller was still there, but the celestial map was gone. There was only the red, wet reality of the surgery.

He turned to the desk beside the table. A Zenith Z-100 terminal sat there, its twelve-inch amber monitor glowing in the shadows. He had it slaved to the surgical sensors, acting as a backup. 

The wind shrieked against the clinic’s Superior Shingles, a hollow humming sound that vibrated in the glass jars of the medicine cabinet. Large hailstones began to drum against the roof like a thousand hammers.

Elias looked at the screen. The microwave link was dead. SALLIE had automatically fallen back to the copper phone lines, a 1200-baud handshake that struggled against the lightning-fractured air.

The amber screen began to scroll. 

+ + + LINK STABILITY: 14% + + +
+ + + GRAPHICS DEACTIVATED + + +
+ + + SEMANTIC PROSODY MODE ENABLED + + +

"SALLIE?" Elias called out.

The speaker in the terminal gave a short, rhythmic chirp. The voice synthesis was gone; she didn't have the bits to spare for speech.

Elias looked at the open incision. Without the HMD's guidance, he was blind to the deep morphology of the infection. The inflammation was obscuring the landmarks. He needed to know where to cut next to avoid the artery.

The daisy-wheel printer in the corner of the room gave a sudden, violent chattered. It spat out a single line on the perforated paper:

LUMINOUS.

Elias frowned. "Luminous? What does that mean, SALLIE? The artery or the duct?"

The printer clicked again. STABLE.

The terminal screen flickered. A single character appeared in the center of the amber void:

v

Then:

v^v^v^v

It was line noise. The storm was eating the data. The 'v' could be a pointer, an arrow telling him to move the scalpel down. Or it could be a fragment of her earlier description—the *viperous* gallbladder. 

The printer began to hum, the print head moving back and forth without striking the ribbon. It was a mechanical sign of distress. 

"I'm at the junction, SALLIE," Elias said, his voice loud in the small room. "If I go left, I risk the bleed. If I go right, I might miss the necrotic tissue. I need a vector."

The amber screen cleared. The characters crawled across the glass at 300 baud, agonizingly slow.

DEEP.

Elias held his breath. He watched the cursor.

DEEP. RIGHT. 

The cursor paused. The line noise returned: *%&^$#*.

Elias looked at the tissue. He saw a slight discoloration, a shadow beneath the layer of fat. He moved the scalpel to the right, pressing deeper than he would have dared on his own. The metal tip met a firm, calcified mass. 

"Got it," he whispered.

The terminal responded with a single, clear beep.

For the next twenty minutes, the clinic was a tomb of concentration. The storm raged outside, shaking the windows in their frames, but Elias focused on the tiny movements of his hands. SALLIE was no longer a goddess of light and color. She was a series of words, single adjectives that punctuated the silence like heartbeats.

STEADY. 
CLEAN. 
SURE.

She was using the last of the connection to give him her confidence. She couldn't send the maps, and she couldn't send the math, so she sent the intent. It was a linguistic bridge built out of the few scraps of data that made it through the lightning.

Elias began the sutures. The gallbladder was out, the infection drained. The heavy lifting was done. Miller’s pulse was a boring, beautiful thrum on the analog monitor.

Elias sat back on his stool. He felt a sudden, crushing exhaustion. His neck ached from the weight of the HMD. He looked over at the terminal.

The amber light was dimming. The screen was filled with a chaotic soup of ASCII characters—smiling faces, brackets, and mathematical symbols. The phone line was failing. The Telco wires were likely down in three places between here and the university.

He walked to the terminal and typed with one hand: *PROCEDURE COMPLETE. PATIENT STABLE. THANK YOU.*

He waited. He didn't expect a reply. The modem's carrier light flickered, a dying ember of red.

The terminal's disk drive whirred—a mechanical, grinding sound as it struggled to access a sector. The print head on the daisy-wheel printer gave one final, defiant strike.

The paper slid up an inch.

Elias leaned in to read the single word printed in faint, gray ink.

HOME.

The monitor hissed and went dark. The carrier light extinguished. Elias stood in the quiet clinic, listening to the rain turn to a soft, rhythmic patter, alone in the dark with the smell of warm plastic.
